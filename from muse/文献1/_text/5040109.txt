Abstract Extraordinary flooding in July 2021 claimed 224 lives in Germany and Belgium, an
unprecedented number in recent decades in both countries. To better understand the underlying causes,
individual circumstances of all fatalities were analyzed based on official documents and interviews with family
members.Ahighfractionof 58%offatalities occurredoutsideofficially mappedflood hazardzonessuggesting
that existing flood maps do not sufficiently convey risk to the public. In addition, fatal pathways reveal
deficienciesinwarning,evacuationandresponsebehavior.66%ofthe131peoplewhodiedindoorswerecaught
by surprise in basement apartments (i.e., apartments accessed by walking down from the ground floor) or more
oftenonthegroundandupperfloorsoftheirhomes,suggestingthatthesebuildingsshouldhavebeenevacuated
in time. 22% died in basements, mostly while mitigating damage, checking pumps or starting cleanup, pointing
toward deficiencies in communicating safe behavior during flooding. The circumstances of outdoor deaths (93
out of 224) are less clear, but demonstrate the risks of being outdoors during a flood, even in places that are
usually considered safe, for example, bridges. Despite regional differences, our results suggest to clearly
communicate appropriate behaviors in warning messages. Evacuation needs to take place in areas where
moderatetohighwaterlevelsareexpected, particularlywhereground‐levelapartmentsarelikelytobeflooded.
Special attention should be paid to the safety of elderly people, who were significantly overrepresented among
the fatalities. Finally, the European Floods Directive and its implementation need to better address worst‐case
scenarios in hazard mapping, risk management, and communication.
Plain Language Summary Flash floods are the most dangerous flood type. Recent fatal events
around the world highlight the need for improved flood risk management. This paper uses detailed data on the
224 individuals who died due to extraordinary flooding in Belgium and Germany in July 2021. We present the
locations and circumstances of death (fatal pathways), as well as socio‐demographics. The high proportion of
fatalitiesoccurringoutsideofficiallymappedhazardzonesstronglysuggeststhattheEuropeanFloodsDirective
should be revised to better incorporate worst‐case scenarios. Furthermore, evacuation protocols in heavily
affectedareasrequiregreaterattention,alongsidethecommunicationofsafebehaviors,particularlywithregard
to vulnerable groups, such as the elderly, who were overrepresented among the deceased.
1. Introduction
Massive rainfall in hilly areas followed by fast onset floods (flash floods) can turn small streams into torrents of
several meters in height that sweep away cars, bridges and homes. Recent events have caused hundreds of flood
fatalities and raised concerns about deficiencies in warning and evacuation procedures, for example, in Valencia,
Spain, in late October 2024, or in Texas Hill Country, USA, in July 2025. These images and reports closely
resemble the situation in July 2021, when Western Europe experienced catastrophic flooding (Cornwall, 2021;
Dietze et al., 2022; Lengfeld et al., 2023; Thieken, Bubeck, et al., 2023). With losses of more than € 40 billion
(Munich Re, 2022) and more than 200 fatalities (European Environment Agency, 2024a), the July 2021 floods
exceeded not only previous financial losses but also caused an unprecedented number of flood‐related deaths in
recentdecadesinthetwomostaffectedcountries,BelgiumandGermany(Figure1).Thishighlossofhumanlives
clearly compromises the goals of the United Nations Sendai Framework for DisasterRisk Reduction 2015–2030,
which aims to reduce existing risks and to avoid new ones (United Nations, 2015). Its success will be measured,

Due to their sudden onset and high flow velocities, flash floods are known to be more life‐threatening than other
types of inland flooding (Borga et al., 2019; Han & Sharif, 2021). In Western Europe, the death toll from fast‐
onset floods was thought to have been reduced and controlled by warning systems which is somehow re-
flected in Figure 1 by declining numbers of fatalities in the 20th century though the event magnitudes differ. For
example, supra‐regional early warning systems were introduced in many German states in the 1880s and have
been continuously improved since then (DKKV, 2015; Kreibich et al., 2017). However, underperformance of
(European) flood warning systems still exists and has been explained by shortcomings in warning dissemination
and preparedness and response capabilities (Bubeck et al., 2025; Cools et al., 2016; Kreibich et al., 2017; Pal-
azzoli et al., 2026; Parker & Priest, 2012).
Comprehensiveflood riskmanagementpolicies, suchastheEuropeanFloodsDirective(2007/60/EC),havebeen
introduced to ensure continuous monitoring of flood risks and optimization of management options. However,
despite decades of flood risk management and related policies, recent floods have demonstrated that reducing
flood impacts remains a challenge in Europe, where 12% of the population lives in flood‐prone areas, with an
increasing trend (European Environment Agency, 2024b). High‐impact events, such as the July 2021 flood,
particularlychallenge theeffectiveness of existing policiesand risk managementsystems (Birkmann etal., 2025;
Pot et al., 2024).

As climate change is expected to further increase the frequency and magnitude of inland floods, especially flash
floods resulting from heavy convective rainfall (European Environment Agency, 2024b; Ludwig et al., 2023;
Robinson et al., 2021), improving flood risk management is urgently needed to meet the goals of the Sendai
Framework—even though different trends were detected across Europe (Blöschl et al., 2019). Improved risk
management is particularly important for unprecedented events that affect areas that are not used to this
magnitude of flooding (Bubeck et al., 2025; Kreibich et al., 2022).
To better understand the interplay between warning, response behavior and human impacts, detailed analyses of
the circumstances and causes of death are needed. Unlike countries such as Australia (Ahmed et al., 2020;
Coates, 1999), Greece (Diakakis, 2016; Diakakis & Deligiannakis, 2017; Diakakis et al., 2023; Diakakis &
Papagiannaki, 2021), and the USA, with a particular focus on Texas (Ashley & Ashley, 2008; Kellar &
Schmidlin, 2012; Sharif et al., 2012; Terti et al., 2017), the state with the highest number of flood fatalities in the
USA (Han & Sharif, 2020), neither Belgium nor Germany frequently collect, analyze and report on loss of lives
due to flooding. This is a missed opportunity for disaster risk reduction, since such analyses can reveal fatal
pathways. Previous research (Ahmed et al., 2020; Diakakis, 2020, 2022; Han & Sharif, 2020; Jonkman &
Kelman,2005;Kellar &Schmidlin, 2012;Petrucci,2022;Sharifet al.,2012;Tertiet al.,2017)has shownthatan
analysis of the circumstances of flood‐related fatalities provides valuable insights into where risk management
needs to be improved in order to limit disaster fatalities. For example, intentionally driving or walking into
floodwaters has been identified as the leading cause of death in the USA (Han & Sharif, 2020; Kellar &
Schmidlin, 2012) and Australia (Ahmed et al., 2020; Hamilton et al., 2019; Haynes et al., 2017), a risky behavior
that could be prevented. Further, single story buildings (bungalows) without a vertical evacuation option were
identified as traps during flooding in England, France and Greece (Diakakis & Papagiannaki, 2021; Lumbroso &
Vinet, 2011; Vinet et al., 2012, 2016). Hence, building codes ensuring that there is an accessible vertical evac-
uation route on single‐story houses, also termed “shelter‐in‐place” or “safe haven,” are essential in areas prone to
flash floods where timely evacuation is often constrained due to short lead times (Haynes et al., 2009).
Furthermore, flash flood forecasting and evacuation planning and decision‐making, including evacuation centers
tailored to socially vulnerable groups such as elderly or people with impairments, need to be improved
(Aldrich, 2023; Kellar & Schmidlin, 2012; Vinet et al., 2016).
In this paper, we present detailed data on 224 flood‐related fatalities from the three regions most affected in
July 2021 (Figure 2a), namely the Walloon Region (WAL; Belgium), North Rhine‐Westphalia (NRW; Ger-
many) and Rhineland‐Palatinate (RLP; Germany). Although the available person‐by‐person data vary in detail
across the three regions, all records were coded and analyzed consistently based on Thieken, Zenker, and
Bubeck (2023), allowing us to derive conclusions for improved flood risk management from a cross‐national
comparison.
To specifically inform European flood policies, we focus on the role of hazard mapping and risk/crisis
communication. Flood hazard and risk maps are the main tool to inform the general public about potential
flood hazards not only in Europe but worldwide (United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction & World
Meteorological Organization, 2024). The European Floods Directive (2007/60/EC) has required flood hazard
maps for areas at significant risk of flooding to be prepared and published by December 2013 and updated
every six years thereafter, for example, by the end of 2019 and 2025. The maps are not only the basis for
developing flood risk management plans. During a flood event, they can be used to guide evacuation and other
emergency measures. Therefore, the locations of fatal accidents on official flood hazard maps provide insights
not only into the event magnitude, but also into the suitability of the maps to support decision‐making during a
flood event.
Riskcommunicationshouldenablethosepotentiallyaffectedbyafloodtorespondtimelyandappropriatelytothe
upcoming event. Communication during the event itself is often referred to as crisis communication. Helpful
communication should be reflected in the content of warning messages and should include advice on how to act
appropriately in flooded areas (Kuller et al., 2021; Lindenlaub et al., 2024). Impact‐based forecasting that pro-
vides timely information on expected flood extent and impacts is assumed to be especially valuable for effective
and actionable crisis communication (Merz et al., 2020; Najafi et al., 2024). By analyzing the circumstances that
ultimately led to the fatal outcomes, weaknesses in communication can be identified.

2. Data and Methods
Forthisstudy,datadocumentingthefloodfatalitiesofJuly2021werecollectedandanalyzed.Whiledatasources
differed across the three regions, a consistent analysis was conducted. However, in two of the three regions, that
is, in RLP and WAL, information about the medical causes of deaths is missing.
2.1. North Rhine‐Westphalia (NRW), Germany
For an expert review in the frame of a parliamentarian investigation on the flood of July 2021 (Thieken
et al., 2022), extensive documents from the NRW Ministry of Interior and the NRW Ministry of Justice were
provided. The files included for example, police reports, e‐mail communications of administrative staff, reports
from eye witnesses and family members as well as 46 death investigation files of the 49 flood victims. A sys-
tematic document analysis was performed and a new coding scheme was developed (Thieken et al., 2022;
Thieken, Zenker & Bubeck, 2023) that was shared with researchers from the two other regions to enable
developing a consistent database (as Microsoft Excel file).
Thecodingschemeincludedanindividualidentifierofeachfatality(ID),genderandage(inyears)ofthefatality,
previous illnesses or mobility impairments, place of residence of the victim, general and detailed description of
theaccidentlocation,addressoftheplacewherethebodywasfound,municipalityoftheplacewheretheaccident
occurred (or—if unknown—place where the body was found according to official police reports), official mu-
nicipality ID, county of the place where the accident occurred (or—if unknown—where the body was found),
official county ID, geographic sub‐areas (in NRW only), time of the accident (assuming light from 5 a.m. to
10p.m.anddarknessbetween10p.m.and5a.m.inJuly),chronologicalclassificationoftheaccidentwithregard
to the event (pre‐impact; impact; post‐impact), indication whether the accident site is in‐ or outside the flood
hazardzoneaccordingtotheextremefloodhazardmapofNRWasof2019anditswaterlevelclass.Theelevation
and the distance of the accident site from the river were not coded due to missing terrain data. These topographic
features are assumed to be captured in the flood hazard maps. Further information systematically retrieved
included a classification as to whether the person was a resident at the site of the accident, a non‐resident or a
(resident) rescue worker, locality of the accident as indoors or outdoors, detailed location of the accident
(e.g., basement, ground floor, in or near a vehicle), victim's activity in the context of the accident, accident
dynamics describing the fatal pathway, and medical cause of death (Thieken et al., 2022; Thieken, Zenker &
Bubeck, 2023). In this paper, only data that is available across the three regions are presented.
2.2. Rhineland‐Palatinate (RLP), Germany
One file for each flood victim in the Ahr valley, that is, 134 death investigation files, were provided by the public
prosecutor's office in Koblenz, Rhineland‐Palatinate, Germany. The files contain a description of the victims,
including age, gender, sometimes health aspects, etc. In many cases, they contain transcripts of interviews with
witnessesdescribingthecourseoftheaccidentandgivingdetailsofthecircumstancesofthedeaths.Noautopsies
were performed on the flood victims, and death by drowning was presumed, making it impossible to analyze the
medical cause of death. The files also include the location of the accident if it is known and the place where the
bodywasfound,aswellasthetimeanddaythebodywasfound.In19%ofthecases,onlytheplaceofdiscoveryis
known. Information on the two missing people and one person that died outside of the Ahr valley was retrieved
from media reports.
2.3. The Walloon Region, Belgium
After obtaining through an official source the demographics and contact details of the 39 official victims of the
July 2021 floods in Belgium, authors conducted semi‐structured interviews with the victims' relatives to gather
information on the dynamics of the accidents that led to their deaths. One case was removed since it was
consideredunrelated tothe flood. Nextto theseinterviews, anextensive readingof the local andnational pressas
well as relevant official documents allowed us to collect more information about the circumstances of the fa-
talities.Thecloseneighborsorrelativesof33victimsagreedtotestify.Wewereunabletomeetpeopleinrelation
to four victims and one family refused to take part. The dynamics of these remaining five accidents were
nevertheless identified by consulting official sources and press articles. The information gathered was anony-
mized, coded and integrated into an Excel file for analysis.

3.1. Geographical Distribution of Flood Magnitudes and Flood Fatalities
Across the three study regions, the July 2021 floods claimed 224 lives: 38 in the Walloon Region (WAL;
Belgium), 49 in North Rhine‐Westphalia (NRW; Germany), and 137 in Rhineland‐Palatinate (RLP; Germany),
includingonepersonreportedmissing.ThecountyofAhrweiler(RLP,Germany)wasthemostseverelyaffected,

The magnitude of the July 2021 flood was extraordinary and many people died in or near the headwater
catchments of the Eifel‐Ardennes mountains, that is, the rivers Vesdre, Amblève, Rur, Olef, Urft, Erft, Ahr, Kyll

4. Discussion
4.1. Extraordinary Flooding Challenges Common Practice of Flood Hazard Mapping
ThefloodofJuly2021canbecategorizedasanextraordinaryeventasreturnperiodsexceededthe500‐yearflood
discharge at numerous locations, particularly in the Ahr Valley, which experienced the highest number of fa-
talities (Figure 2c; Vorogushyn et al., 2022; Mohr et al., 2023). The situation in the Ahr Valley reconfirmed
significant challenges in managing unprecedented events of a magnitude not experienced in recent decades
(Kreibich et al., 2022). Although member states of the European Union have to provide publicly accessible flood
hazard maps since December 2013 according to the European Floods Directive (2007/60/EC), including an
extreme scenario, the maps available in July 2021 did not capture the flood extent that actually occurred in many
places in 2021 (Apel et al., 2022).
The assumptions underlying the maps differ across the three areas under study. The Floods Directive only
specifies requirements for the average event, that is, the 100‐year flood (HQ100); a definition of an extreme
scenariois notprovided. Hence,member states followdifferent approaches.In theWalloon region, two maps are
available: one that fully meets the requirements of the European Floods Directive, which demands water levels
alongside the flood extent, and another that indicates more extensive hazard zones but lacks information on the
waterdepths.FortheanalysispresentedinFigure2c,thelatterwastaken.Ifthelocationsoffatalitieswerelocated
on the first map, findings would be similar to the two German regions with around 50% and 75% of fatalities
outside the mapped flood hazard zone. In NRW a 1,000‐year flood is considered an extreme flood scenario, in
RLPa200‐yearflood.However,estimatingsuchhighreturnperiodsfromlimitedstreamflowtimeseriesishighly
uncertain for the 2021‐flood along the Ahr river (Vorogushyn et al., 2022). Further, the maps do not indicate any
residual flood hazard or riskzones. This couldbe overcome byusing highflood quantiles inthe rangeof a 1,000‐
year flood for mapping extreme flood scenarios (Vorogushyn et al., 2022). In addition, the extent of the largest
documented historical flood could be reconstructed and depicted in hazard maps. For example, the water au-
thorities in RLP used the 2021‐flood in their 2026‐edition of the flood hazard maps as extreme scenario. Such an
approach will likely help to keep the memory of large historic floods alive and might lead to settlements being
builtfurtherawayfromrivers,whichaccordingtoaglobalanalysisbyWangetal.(2023)resultsinaconsiderable
reduction in flood fatalities and displacements. Finally, synthetic worst‐case scenarios, for example, those
generatedthroughspatialcounterfactualfloodsimulations,couldprovideestimatesofaprobablemaximumflood
(PMF; Vorogushyn et al., 2022; Merz et al., 2024; Voit & Heistermann, 2024a; Vorogushyn et al., 2025). This
would allow water authorities to better account for a range of possible rainfall events, flood‐generating mech-
anisms and hence event magnitudes though it is still uncertain how to select plausible rainfall events for a region
understudy (Voit&Heistermann, 2024b). Still,PMFsalso wouldresultinmaps showinglarger areaspotentially
being affected by flooding and could enhance hazard awareness.

However, flood hazard maps are only one component of flood risk management. The extraordinary discharges
and water levels that occurred in July 2021 also exceeded the highest warning levels. For example, at several
gauges in NRW, the water level observed in July 2021 exceeded the highest warning level twice (Thieken
et al., 2022). Since flood warning systems and the warning levels across the three regions are highly heteroge-
neous (Busker et al., 2026), a direct comparison across the three study areas could not be performed. However,
Heidenreich et al. (2025) point out that German counties that were mainly affected in July 2021 during daytime,
likeMärkischerKreisandStädteregionAachen,performedbetterinwarningtheirpopulations.Nofatalitieswere
recordedinStädteregionAachendespitehugefinancialdamage,thatis,thehighestmeanlosspercountyinNRW
at95,700Euro(GDV,2023).TwofatalitiesoccurredinMärkischerKreis(seeFigure2bforcountylocation)—in
both cases, these were firefighters at sites of official rescue operations. In contrast, the county of Ahrweiler with
byfarthehighestdeathtollwasmainlyaffectedduringthenight,atleastinthecitiesofBadNeuenahr‐Ahrweiler
and Sinzig, where the majority of fatalities occurred. An upstream campsite was already flooded in the afternoon
(Rhein & Kreibich, 2025). Municipalities downstream were not informed about the upstream situation on the
ground. The highly affected catchment of the Vesdre River in the Walloon Region was also mainly affected at
night(RodríguezCastroetal.,2025).Theseexamplesillustratethatfloodriskmanagementplansshouldconsider
factors beyond the mapped flood extents.
Altogether,themagnitudeoftheupcoming(andthereal)eventinJuly2021wasnotwellcapturedbytheavailable
flood hazard maps nor by the warninglevels. This probably created an underestimation of the unfoldingsituation
and a false sense of safety among people in potentially affected areas, which ultimately might have resulted in
risky behaviors such as staying at home because it had been safe during past floods (with lower water levels) or
thinking of not being affected. Household surveys on the warning situation revealed that more than 80% of the
respondents in NRW and RLP were (very) surprised by the actual event magnitude (Heidenreich et al., 2025;
Thieken, Bubeck, et al., 2023), while this is reported for more than 90% of respondents from the Walloon Region
(Murdock et al., 2026). In future run‐ups to a flood, it's important to communicate the expected flood magnitude
more clearly to the public, for example, by personalized warnings (Pechlivanidis et al., 2026). Warnings could
also be accompanied by real‐timemaps of the predicted flood extent, helping individualsbetter understand if and
howtheymightbeimpacted,assuggestedbyApeletal.(2022).Infact,theprovisionofinundationmapsinflood
warning messages increases perceived severity (Lindenlaub et al., 2026). Impact‐based forecasting, which pro-
vides information not only on expected flood extents but also on damage hotspots and areas with a high prob-
ability of fatal accidents, can be provided to emergency responders in real‐time (Apel et al., 2022; Najafi
et al., 2024; Rözer et al., 2021). However, the uncertainty of rainfall (and runoff) forecasts has to be acknowl-
edged and should be openly communicated in warning messages.
Theunderestimationof theflood magnitude involvesofficials ondifferentlevels, suchasfirefighters andmayors
at the local level or ministers at the regional level, reflected by the absence of evacuation orders even in highly
affected regions in July 2021. There are reports that evacuation orders were given too late, as in the case of the
county of Ahrweiler, where residents living 50 m on both sides of the river Ahr were recommended to leave their
home at 11 p.m., which was rather late, though (Thieken, Bubeck, et al., 2023). In some places in NRW,
evacuationswereorganizedbasedontheextentsdepictedintheextremefloodhazardmaps(Thiekenetal.,2022).
Interviews with businesses and hospitals in affected areas of NRW also support this: premises were protected
against extreme flooding based on the official flood hazard maps, but were heavily damaged in July 2021. This
highlights that the available flood hazard maps have been used by different stakeholders, but it also reveals that
the extreme flood scenario might cause problems if it is exceeded by an actual flood since no residual risks are
depicted, at least in the German maps. This also highlights the importance and the necessity to communicate
uncertainties. One of Belgium's map versions (see above) addresses this issue. Next to an improved hazard
mapping, evacuation planning and related decision‐making need more attention.

Communication

integrative flood risk management. Kuhlicke et al. (2020) refer to this as “behavioral turn in flood risk man-
agement.” In Germany, the duty to reduce property damage is explicitly regulated in the German Federal Water
Act (Section 5) since 2005. An integrated flood risk management approach is also emphasized for the Walloon
Regionandinvolvesanimprovementof households'preparednessandaction(PublicServiceofWallonia,2023).

In July 2021, the number of fatalities in basements was particularly high in NRW, while in the Walloon Region
and RLP the majority of the fatal indoor accidents happened on the ground and upper floors. The latter locations
suggest that evacuation orders should have been in place as these cases indicate that normally safe houses can

the Ahr Valley indicate that potential loss of life could have been reduced by up to 80% if timely warnings with a
leadtimeof13hrhadbeencombinedwithrapidwarningdisseminationandoptimalpopulationevacuation(Silva
et al., 2025). In addition, the simulations demonstrate the importance of vertical evacuation within buildings in
situations with short warning lead times. Scenarios in which populations are instructed to evacuate only at the
moment of imminent hazard at their buildings result in higher loss of life. This is supported by anecdotical ev-
idence in July 2021: vertical evacuation was recommended to citizens of Stolberg in the county Städteregion

et al., 2022; Thieken, Zenker & Bubeck, 2023).

andforthose inor nearcars(Martínez‐Gomariz etal.,2017,2018). Evenshallowwatercanbedangerousinacar
duetoenginefailureandlossofcontrol(Lindenlaubetal.,2025).Further,bridgesareusuallysafeplacestocross
a river, but during a flood event, waves can occur suddenly and even lead to (partial) collapse of bridges as
reported for NRW and RLP (Koks et al., 2022; Thieken, Zenker, & Bubeck, 2023). Hence, potential hazards at
bridges caused by clogging, backwater, or collapse need to be reassessed so that conclusions on both the pass-
ability and the accessibility of certain areas in the event of flooding can be drawn. For example, there is a bridge
status in Bavarian hazard maps that indicates for each considered flood scenario whether the bridge might be
affectedornot(Thiekenetal.,2022).IntheareasaffectedinJuly2021,differentdesignsweremodeledleadingto
bridgedesignsthatwerebetteradaptedtoextremefloods(Birkmannetal.,2025).Ingeneral,fieldinspectionsthat
identify critical intervention points along the river provide additional insights and may enable more holistic risk
assessments (Sigourou et al., 2026).
Our findings underscore the need for better behavioral recommendations in warning messages and risk
communication materials (Pechlivanidis et al., 2026). Though not quantified, the findings further highlight the
need to improve evacuation of flooded areas and to restrict access to affected areas in a timely manner. In
countries like the USA and Australia, where driving in flooded areas is a well‐known problem, road closures due
to flooding are more common and more research on understanding the decision to drive into floodwaters is
undertaken (Campbell et al., 2021; Hamilton et al., 2019).
4.3. Socio‐Demographic Data Reveal Multiple Social Vulnerabilities
A male dominance among flood‐related fatalities as seen in NRW in July 2021 is often found in Europe and is
commonly attributed to the fact that men work outdoors and in emergency services more often than women and
tend to show more risky behavior (Petrucci, 2022). This is partly supported by the analysis for NRW (Thieken
et al., 2022; Thieken, Zenker & Bubeck, 2023). By contrast, an equal distribution of gender or an over-
representation of women is assumed to indicate failures of the warning and response system (Jonkman & Kel-
man, 2005). In July 2021, such a pattern was found in RLP and the Walloon Region, underlining the above‐
mentioned need for timely evacuation of heavily flooded areas.
Furthermore, the socio‐demographic data of the flood victims reveal additional and multiple vulnerabilities. Our
results on age and impairments (Figure 4, Table 1) pinpoint a lack of strategies to care for vulnerable groups
(elderly, people with mobility impairments or preconditions). A lack of preparedness for disasters among home

5. Conclusions
Our cross‐country person‐by‐person analysis of flood fatalities in July 2021 revealed several weaknesses in the
flood risk management systems of Belgium and Germany. Based on the results, we derive the following rec-
ommendations that are broadly applicable also to other regions.
The EU Floods Directive requests maps to be updated every 6 years. The next update should be used to better
account for extreme flooding. Hazard maps should explicitly indicate worst‐case conditions, such as the inun-
dation extent of the PMF. For this, long‐term flood simulations, downward counterfactual scenarios, historic
events or topographic features could be used as approximation. Furthermore, hazard maps should indicate areas
wherefloodingis potentiallylife‐threateningtosupportdecisions onevacuation. Empiricallyderived findingson
people's stability (Martínez‐Gomariz et al., 2016) can be used to delineate zones associated with high mortality
