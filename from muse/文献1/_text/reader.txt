ABSTRACT
Coastal areas of the United States are vulnerable to substantial loss of lives and property damage
from repeatedly occurring hurricanes and evacuation is the usual recourse to prevent loss of life
when high storm surge threatens. The fundamental question in evacuation modeling is to
explore the complex evacuation decision-making process leading to an individual's decision to
evacuate or not during a hurricane threat. Recent studies suggest that the social network
characteristics of individuals could potentially determine overall evacuation patterns. This study
explores the joint evacuation decisions of individuals in personal networks by using ego-centric
social network data obtained from Hurricane Sandy and by considering the nested structure of
the ego-centric network data, i.e. close contacts (alters) as nested within an individual (ego). In
this regard, the study develops a multinomial multilevel model of joint evacuation decisions at
the dyadic (ego-alter tie) level utilizing a Hierarchical Generalized Linear Modeling (HGLM)
approach. Model estimation results suggest factors that deﬁne a social tie (contact frequency,
discussion topic and geographic proximity) signiﬁcantly inﬂuence the evacuation decisions
between individuals and their social partners. In addition, individuals' (both ego and alter) own
socio-demographics such as age, marital status, previous evacuation experience, evacuation
order, household's type, size, location and proximity to a water body also aﬀect the decision to
evacuate. These ﬁndings are useful to help emergency managers implement eﬃcient evacuation
strategies and to facilitate planning by policymakers by determining fractions of people
evacuating or not for a major hurricane within the context of their social networks.
1. Introduction and motivation
Hurricane Sandy (October 2012) caused about 254 deaths in the US, Caribbean, and Bahamas with an estimated economic loss
of $65 billion (USD) (Ehrhart et al., 2012). Seventy-two fatalities, including 41 drownings from storm surge, resulted from Sandy's
wind and ﬂood impact (Gladwin et al., 2013). Timely evacuation could have averted such storm surge deaths that were higher than
all but two prior hurricanes (Katrina and Camille) in the past 60 years (National Hurricane Center, 2015). Most of Katrina's direct
storm surge drowning deaths occurred in Mississippi included 200 fatalities that is nearly 0.00054% of the population. While
Sandy's wind and waves heights were less than Katrina's category 4 hurricane winds and 30 foot waves in Mississippi, the death toll
did not remotely approach the same proportion of the population aﬀected by Katrina in Mississippi. Nonetheless, this death toll and
what it could have been in a category four or ﬁve hurricane mandates further study of public evacuation decision-making for Sandy.

This research indicates that social networks within and outside of households are likely to play a major role in facilitating evacuation

A likely mechanism for the eﬀect of social networks in evacuation decision-making is social capital operating in social networks
and social ties both traditionally deﬁned, and as augmented by social media. According to one deﬁnition of social capital, individuals
exchange resources such as information, ideas, support and others through relationships with other people, thus considering social
capital as the extent to which an individual involves himself in diﬀerent informal networks as well as formal civic organizations
(Putnam, 1995, 2001). This conceptualizes social capital as the many diﬀerent ways members of a given community interact with
each other as an indication of that community's associational life and civic health, including participating in recreational activities,
talking to neighbors, joining political parties or environmental organizations and so on. Similarly, Coleman deﬁned social capital by
its function that includes diﬀerent entities with two common elements: an aspect of social structures and certain facilitating actions
of actors within the structure (Coleman, 1988). The prospect of social capital in building more resilient communities has been
explored recently (Aldrich, 2012) explaining how such communities may become proactive in absorbing sudden shocks specially
during post-disasters. That study identiﬁed social capital as the single most contributing factor during post-disaster recovery and
suggested individuals and neighborhoods with weaker ties faced more diﬃculties as a result of inaccurate interactions with organized
and mobilized groups.
A more network-oriented perspective relevant to social capital is to consider the personal networks of individuals’ i.e. ego-centric
social networks (Borgatti and Halgin, 2011; Burt, 1984, 1985, 2000; Halgin and Borgatti, 2012). The General Social Survey (GSS),
ﬁrst conducted in 1972, measured core discussion networks among Americans (Burt, 1985; Marsden, 1987) and several studies have
used these GSS discussion network measures (Brashears, 2011; Wellman, 1979). The particular questionnaire item involved from
GSS has been widely used by researchers because of its parsimony, general nature, applicability to various contexts and ability to
capture ego's core discussion networks without focus on speciﬁc network content (Bailey and Marsden, 1999; Burt, 1984; Carrasco
et al., 2013; Kowald et al., 2010; Sadri et al., 2015a; van den Berg et al., 2009). In contrast, Granovettor gave more stress to the
cohesive power of weak ties and suggested that occasional and informal ties have the ability to link diﬀerent groups of individuals
that could provide access to newer resources or support (Granovetter, 1973). As a result, conceptual debates on social capital cannot
be resolved in an empirical vacuum (Grootaert et al., 2003).
Frequently occurring hurricanes in the coastal areas of United States require that diﬀerent stakeholders, particularly emergency
planners and oﬃcials, understand the overall evacuation process thoroughly and identify key determinants of evacuation behavior in
reducing the amount of risk associated with vulnerable communities. The key requirement is to explore evacuation decision-making
behavior (i.e. who evacuates and who does not) and the causal factors inﬂuencing this behavior. Within the overall amount of
research conducted on the issue of evacuation, few studies do this. The central idea of this paper is to analyze joint evacuation
decision-making behavior of individuals in a personal network setting using ego-centric social network data obtained from
Hurricane Sandy and considering the nested structure of the data i.e. friends or close contacts (alters) being nested within an
individual (ego). In this regard, this study develops a multinomial multilevel model of joint evacuation decision outcome at the
dyadic (ego-alter tie) level using Hierarchical Generalized Linear Modeling (HGLM) approach to investigate the eﬀects of relational
or tie attributes in addition to individuals' own socio-demographic characteristics.
Social media served as an essential source of information sharing during Hurricane Sandy. Many residents lacking access to
traditional sources of information (television for example), received information on smart-phones using social media (Kaufman
et al., 2012). Social media communications in areas without power (Midtown Manhattan was the most common tweeting location)
continued
throughout and after the storm as the continuous distribution of tweets spread throughout the city (Floating Sheep, 2012).
While social media pertains to virtual or online social connections, they can reﬂect real social ties that can inﬂuence individual
evacuation decisions. Hasan and Ukkusuri found that warning information propagates faster within networks with greater inter-
community connections using a simulation based approach (Hasan and Ukkusuri, 2011). Another earlier study presented a
contagion model to explain how people ﬁrst receive emergency warning information and then spread the information to others
(Rogers and Sorensen, 1991). These network eﬀects were stressed in the policy proposal of Gladwin et al. on necessary actions
required by appropriate agencies and organizations to support social science research on the major issues in the hurricane forecast
and warning system (Gladwin et al., 2007). Social network characteristics and warning information sources signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced
individual-level evacuation decision making during Hurricane Sandy (Sadri et al., 2016).
This study is organized as follows: the next section summarizes existing literature on hurricane evacuation decision-making;
Section 3 describes the data and explains the personal network research design approach; Section 4 presents the methodology used
to develop the model; Section 5 elaborates the model estimation results; and Section 6 concludes the study with a discussion of key
ﬁndings.
2. Background and related work
Evacuation is a typical form of travel during extreme events and it is the usual recourse to prevent loss of life if high storm surge
occurs. However, evacuees often show synchronized behavior in terms of when they depart and what route they take. (Wolshon,
2002). Several studies show important factors aﬀecting evacuation decisions such as hurricane trajectory and warning system,

characteristics of evacuees and their households, etc. to explain the overall evacuation process (Baker, 1991; Carnegie and Deka,
2010; Gladwin et al., 2001, 2007; Lindell and Prater, 2007b; Urbina and Wolshon, 2003). Dash and Gladwin reviewed research
showing that households who are expected to evacuate do not evacuate and vice versa, as well as many contradictory eﬀects on
evacuation of socio-demographic characteristics such as age, gender, race, ethnicity, income, children or elderly persons in the
household, disability, previous experience, and geographic-location (Dash and Gladwin, 2007). Studies of evacuation in one major
hurricane (e.g Peacock et al. for Hurricane Andrew) typically ﬁnd as signiﬁcant variables such as being in an evacuation zone,
household size, and presence of elderly people and children (Peacock et al., 1997). On the other hand, comparing storms, Whitehead
et al. found storm intensity to be the principal determinant of the decision to evacuate (Whitehead et al., 2000). However, Lindell
et al. showed stronger correlations between evacuation decisions and geographic characteristics, information utilization, evacuation
order, oﬃcial warning and so on (Lindell et al., 2005).
Some studies developed statistical models presenting a diverse set of behavioral aspects related to evacuation decisions (Fu and
Wilmot, 2004, 2006; Solis et al., 2009). Mileti and Sorenson introduced the notion of risk perception based on personal assessment
of own risk in addition to that of family and household (Mileti and Sorensen, 1987). Hasan et al. developed a random parameters
binary logit model of hurricane evacuation decision where the random parameters account for the heterogeneous responses of
households towards a major hurricane (Hasan et al., 2010). That study found heterogeneities in terms of location, evacuation notice,
work constraint, and the number of children. Apart from modeling the decision to evacuate, other studies have explored follow up
questions such as (a) when to evacuate? (Fu and Wilmot, 2004, 2006; Hasan et al., 2013; Lindell and Prater, 2007a), (b)
mobilization time, i.e., time elapsed from decision to evacuate to actual evacuation (Dixit et al., 2008, 2011; Sadri et al., 2013a), (c)
where to evacuate? (Cheng et al., 2008; Mesa-Arango et al., 2012), (d) which mode of travel? (Deka and Carnegie, 2010; Sadri et al.,
2014) (e) which route to take? (Murray-Tuite et al., 2012; Sadri et al., 2013b, 2015b) among others.
Social networks characteristics of individuals, however, can also aﬀect the underlying evacuation patterns. These social ties
pertain to informal networks based on neighbors, co-workers, family members, and friends (Gladwin et al., 2007). Drabek and Boggs
found that individuals with small social networks are less likely to evacuate (Drabek and Boggs, 1968). Cliﬀord found that social ties
aﬀect the disaster warning dissemination and adaptation (Cliﬀord, 1956). Studies also show that African American households with
stronger kinship ties are more likely to spread out warning information and evacuate (Perry, 1979; Quarantelli, 1985). Riad et al.
identiﬁed 'social inﬂuence' as a social psychological process inﬂuencing the complex phenomenon of why people sometimes chose
not to evacuate during a threat (Riad et al., 1999). However, that study concluded individuals are ﬁnally responsible for their
decisions despite social inﬂuences. Using a simulation based approach, Hasan and Ukkusuri suggested that an individual's risk
perception can be socially inﬂuenced as evacuation decisions spread from individual to individual through social ties (Hasan and
Ukkusuri, 2011). That study considered diﬀerent community mixing patterns in explaining information cascade and evacuation
decision behavior.
While previous studies examined why people decide to evacuate based on individual level characteristics, this study focuses on
evacuation decision-making behavior of people based on their social network structure. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is
the ﬁrst study to collect ego-centric social network data of individuals based on their response to Hurricane Sandy and examine the
level of inﬂuence of social ties on their joint evacuation decision-making process. Estimation ﬁndings suggest that the characteristics
of social ties, such as frequency of contact and geographic proximity signiﬁcantly aﬀect how people evacuate during a disaster in
addition to some individual level characteristics. This study's ﬁndings will help emergency planners and policy-makers develop
better evacuation plans and strategies by determining fractions of people evacuating or not during a major hurricane and thus
predicting evacuation demand more accurately.
3. Data collection
In this study, data was collected by surveying residents from high storm surge risk areas of New York and New Jersey all of whom
participated in the survey voluntarily (see Fig. 1). The survey was funded by the National Science Foundation. The sample frame for
the survey was based on US census block group areas in coastal New Jersey and New York likely to be at risk of life-threatening
storm surge in a major hurricane; the block groups within ﬁve km of the coastline or inland tidal water body and having a mean
elevation of less than eight meters. In addition, the study includes block groups, not meeting this condition, located in New York City
evacuation Zone A. Approximately 350 block groups were randomly selected from the list of qualifying block groups and a sample of

telephone numbers randomly drawn from those block group areas. Forty-ﬁve percent of the numbers were listed land line numbers

The survey was designed to reveal how individuals from the sample area reacted to Hurricane Sandy. While Sandy approached,
people had a short time to understand and decide what to do. Respondents provided relevant details about their social ties with
people with whom they interacted closely during Sandy. This included people with whom they talked, gave or received help, or stayed
close before and during the storm. Ego-centric social network data of individuals were obtained (Borgatti and Halgin, 2011; Burt,
1984; Halgin and Borgatti, 2012; Wellman, 1979) by following the personal network research design (PNRD) approach. According
to this approach, name generator questions create an exhaustive list of alters with whom the respondent (ego) has some type of
relationship. Name interpreter items are then asked to elicit the attributes for each alter identiﬁed, as well as the relational (dyadic)
attributes between ego and alter. The geographic proximity between ego and alters was part of the name interpreter items where egos
provided the distance in miles (or equivalent driving distance in minutes) as open-ended responses. These open-ended responses
were grouped into three categories: one mile or less (47.52%), two to ﬁve miles (18.21%), and more than ﬁve miles (34.27%). Many
classical studies of ego-centric networks used this approach previously (Burt, 1984; Fischer, 1982; Laumann, 1966, 1973; Wellman,
1979). The speciﬁc questionnaire items from General Social Survey (GSS) involved have been widely used by researchers due to their
applicability to various contexts and ability to capture ego's core social contacts (Bailey and Marsden, 1999; Burt, 1984; Carrasco
et al., 2013; Kowald et al., 2010; Sadri et al., 2015a; van den Berg et al., 2009). However, this approach has some limitations such as
biases that occur in recall and people's propensity to forget their close contacts among others (Brewer, 2000; Carrasco et al., 2013;
Marin, 2004; Marin and Hampton, 2007).
4. Social networks, social inﬂuence, and evacuation decisions
Social networks include individuals as nodes and their relationships as links or edges. Such relationships help to construct a
network of individuals (Jackson, 2010) and guide how the process of social inﬂuence takes place between peers. Studies have
suggested that sources of inﬂuence may include personal inﬂuence between the direct contacts, inﬂuence between social groups or
social circles, and inﬂuence from information sources such as media (Kadushin, 2012). As such, there exists a unique
interdependence between the network structure, the inﬂuence mechanism, and the source itself (Maness, 2015). In recent times,
transportation researchers have been extensively looking at social networks and social inﬂuence mechanisms to study several
behavioral dimensions speciﬁc to travel and trip making such as mode choice (Dugundji and Walker, 2005), joint trip frequency for
regular activity travel (Sadri et al., 2015a), carpooling (Rezende et al., 2016), participation in social activities (Carrasco and Miller,
2007), cycling (Sherwin et al., 2014), drunk driving (Kim and Kim, 2012), among others.
Diﬀerent forms of travel may possess varying social inﬂuence processes and it requires great care in selecting an appropriate
social inﬂuence mechanism given the social networks under consideration and the type of inﬂuence sources (Maness, 2015).
Evacuation
is a speciﬁc form of travel during extreme events that helps to prevent loss of life when high storm surge occurs. During
an evacuation, the key behavioral concern for emergency managers is to understand whether people would prefer to evacuate or not.
Studies that presented the need to integrate social networks, social inﬂuence mechanisms and other social factors into evacuation
modeling is discussed in Section 2. In this regard, Sadri et al. (2016) explored how social networks and information sources served
the purpose of disseminating warning information during Hurricane Sandy. The study looked at the eﬀects of social network size,
homophily (similarity), heterogeneity (diversity), evacuation timing of the peers, average length of relationship on evacuation
decision-making by using a mixed-logit approach and ego-centric network data obtained from the high storm surge areas.
However, whether individuals, during major hurricanes, make similar decisions or equally perceive the risk due to peer pressure,
is yet to be uncovered. This study is intended to unfold such joint evacuation decision-making behavior given individuals are parts
identical social networks. In particular, this study explores the similarities in evacuation decisions between social partners as a
function of the nature of social relationship, geographic proximity, and socio-demographic factors such as age, gender, marital status
among others. This has been done by looking at the personal network data obtained from New York and New Jersey residents and by
considering the nested structure of the ego-centric network data i.e. friends or close contacts (alters) being nested within an
individual (ego).
In order to model joint evacuation decisions at the dyadic (ego-alter) level, this study develops a multinomial multilevel model of
evacuation decision outcomes using Hierarchical Generalized Linear Modeling (HGLM) approach. The categorical dependent
variable was based on respondents (ego) self-report of their own evacuation decision as well as the people (alters) they closely
interacted during Hurricane Sandy. Data shows that the number of social ties varied from a minimum of 1 to a maximum of 6 and
shows a nesting structure i.e. alter level information being nested within each ego. As a result, the evacuation decision outcome at the
dyadic level has four categories: (a) both ego and alter evacuate (b) ego evacuates, alter does not evacuate (c) alter evacuates, ego
does not and (d) both ego and alter do not evacuate. Fig. 2 shows the distribution of the discrete evacuation decision outcomes at the
dyadic (ego-alter) level. Table 1 presents descriptive statistics of explanatory variables used in the ﬁnal model speciﬁcation with
mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum. After accounting for missing data for some explanatory variables in the ﬁnal

5. Modeling framework
In this study, a Hierarchical Generalized Linear Modeling (HGLM) approach for a multinomial model is used to assess the joint
evacuation decision outcome during a major hurricane at the dyadic (ego-alter) level. The multinomial multilevel modeling approach
is appropriate for clustering data with a multinomial outcome considering the following two speciﬁc reasons: (1) multinomial
analysis is more eﬃcient than a series of binary logit analyses since the former approach involves a large number of comparisons
(Scott Long, 1997). With four categories such as the type of evacuation outcome at the dyadic level in this study, six comparisons
should be made whereas the multinomial model needs only three comparisons. (2) multilevel analysis is advantageous over
traditional ordinary least squares (OLS) regression analysis since it accounts for the dependence among alters within egos network
and provides eﬃcient parameter estimates in unbalanced, nested structure of data (Raudenbush and Bryk, 2002).
However, the problem of evacuation decision-making involves four possible outcomes at the dyadic (ego-alter) level: (1) both ego
and alter evacuate, (2) ego evacuates, alter does not evacuate, (3) alter evacuates, ego does not and (4) both ego and alter do not

In this study, the case that both ego and alter evacuate (case 1) has been treated as the reference category. The constant terms
suggest, all else being equal, it is more likely that both the ego and alter are more likely not to evacuate as compared to the other
cases. This ﬁnding provides more evidence that people, in general, tend not to evacuate depending on several determining factors.
This factor is similar to ﬁndings from Hurricane Ivan (Hasan et al., 2010) and depicts the overall unwillingness of people in the

6.2. Eﬀects of alter characteristics
Apart from the signiﬁcant dyadic attributes, individual attributes of ego and alter play signiﬁcant roles in aﬀecting the evacuation
outcome at the dyadic level. For example, alter age shows higher mean odds (1.03 for case 2 and case 4; 1.02 for case 3) indicating
the likelihood that both ego and alter evacuate reduces with the increased age of alters. On the other hand, alter income results in
lower mean odds as deﬁned for case 2 and case 4 (0.47 and 0.63 respectively) indicating that if alter belongs to the high income
group they are less likely to evacuate. Alter gender was found to be highly signiﬁcant for case 4 with higher mean odds (47%) which
indicates that having male alters in the network makes it more likely that both ego and alter do not evacuate. These ﬁndings suggest
how the characteristics of the people in social networks signiﬁcantly impact evacuation decision-making.
6.3. Eﬀects of ego characteristics
In the ﬁnal model speciﬁcation, characteristics related to ego include location, age, marital status, household size, proximity to
large water body, evacuation experience and evacuation order from oﬃcials inﬂuence evacuation decision. For example, if ego is
from New York it results in higher odds (75%) as deﬁned for case 4 indicating that individuals and their social partners from this
area are less likely to evacuate. This important ﬁnding captures the overall unwillingness of the people from New York to evacuate. If
ego is an elderly person (65 years or over), ego is more likely to evacuate as suggested by lower odds (52%) deﬁned for case 3. This
important ﬁnding indicates the higher perception of risk by senior citizens. On the other hand, the odds for case 3 and case 4 were
3.33 and 1.78 times greater for egos who are married, respectively showing relative unwillingness of married individuals to evacuate.
Similar eﬀects were observed for the number of years ego lived in the present county or city. Ego being the only person in the
household shows higher odds both for case 2 and case 3 suggesting the likelihood of either ego or alter to evacuate. Proximity to large
water body or ocean (twenty-ﬁve miles or less) also suggests that either the ego or alter do not evacuate if ego living in close
proximity (within 25 miles of an ocean or large water body). This should be given due consideration since individuals staying at close
proximity needs to evacuate for safety.
However, previous evacuation experience and receiving evacuation order from emergency oﬃcials result in lower mean odds for
each of the cases compared to the base outcome (both ego and alter evacuate). This result provides a crucial insight on the fact that
individuals having previous experience and receiving an evacuation order can persuade their social partners to evacuate and show
synchronous evacuation behavior. These ﬁndings from Hurricane Sandy validates the ﬁndings from Hurricane Ivan (Hasan et al.,
2010) and have important implications. The key take-away from this result is that the more people who receive mandatory
evacuation orders from oﬃcials the more likely they will evacuate which can expedite the evacuation process. However, individuals
having evacuation experience prior to Sandy were more likely to evacuate compared to those without prior experience. This result
contrasts with Hurricane Ivan ﬁndings and depicts the importance of educating people in coastal regions who do not have previous
experience about the necessity of evacuation. The odds of case 3 (alter evacuates, ego does not) relative to the base case were 0.50
times lower for ego living in a single-family home.
7. Conclusions and key ﬁndings
In this study, the role of social ties on evacuation behavior is explored by using ego-centric social network data obtained from
Hurricane Sandy and by considering the hierarchical structure of the data i.e. friends or close contacts (alters) being nested within an
individual (ego). With the help of Hierarchical Generalized Linear Modeling (HGLM) technique, this study develops a multinomial
multilevel model of joint evacuation-decision outcome based at the dyadic (ego-alter tie) level. Estimation ﬁndings suggest that joint
evacuation decisions of closely connected individuals are signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced by several underlying factors. The main
contribution
of this study in the evacuation modeling literature is to report important dyadic or relational attributes being
representative of the characteristics of social ties and dominating the joint evacuation decision outcome. Some of the key insights
based on the ﬁnal model speciﬁcation are listed below:
 All else being equal, it is more likely that both the ego and alter are less likely to evacuate.
 Household size, location and proximity to large water body inﬂuence evacuation decision-making. People, staying in a single-
person household, are less likely to evacuate. Individuals staying close to large water body are more likely to evacuate.
 Previous evacuation experience and evacuation order from emergency oﬃcials signiﬁcantly impact evacuation decision-making.
Individuals who experienced a major hurricane previously and receive evacuation order have a heightened level of risk perception
and thus evacuate.
 Both ego and the alters (social partners) are more likely to evacuate if they contact each other daily and live in close geographic
proximity (less than ﬁve miles or ﬁfteen minutes’ drive).
 If the topic of discussion between ego and alter is family matters in general, it has mixed eﬀects on evacuation decisions at the
dyadic level. This is indicative of family relations in social networks being responsible for within-household negotiation and

 Age, gender, marital status and income of alters signiﬁcantly impact evacuation outcome at the dyadic level. Age has

This study provides meaningful implications for a clear understanding of evacuation decision-making behavior that can help
forecasters and emergency oﬃcials create targeted and eﬃcient evacuation plans. Evacuation orders from oﬃcials make it more
likely that individuals will evacuate as do frequently contacted social ties. Evacuation rates would be higher and faster for closely
connected communities. Thus, strong social ties (more contact frequency) can serve the purpose of eﬀective dissemination of
warning information. On the other hand, if friends live far apart, chances of evacuation are signiﬁcantly reduced. Based on this,
group-level evacuation policies can be introduced for people residing in mandatory evacuation zones and increase the rate of overall
evacuation. More consideration should be given to special population groups such as elderly people and senior citizens by providing
them desired facilities required for evacuation. Increased public education is needed for people residing in the evacuation zones who
never experienced a major hurricane.
This study incorporates a disaggregate approach to model evacuation decision-making i.e. how an individual and close contact
both behave during a major hurricane threat. However, a more aggregated approach would be to model evacuation decision-making
at the individual level by including network measures like density, homophily, heterogeneity and so on to explore network eﬀects.
Also, the study is based on ego-centric social network data from the east coast i.e. New York and New Jersey during Hurricane
Sandy. Future studies could collect data from similar types of disasters to validate the results obtained in this study and check if the
model is applicable and transferable to other coastal areas in the United States. The survey did not obtain the actual content of the
communication between ego and alter with respect to the hurricane or evacuation, however, such crisis communication patterns can
also be important predictors of joint evacuation decisions.
