A B S T R A C T
Understanding human mobility during extreme flood events is critical for emergency evacuation 
and resilient transport planning, yet flood-induced behavioral heterogeneity remains difficult to 
characterize. Progress has been constrained by data scarcity and the inherent complexity of 
emergency decision-making, which often induces abnormal travel behaviors and anomalous 
mobility fluxes. We propose the Disaster-aware Trajectory Generator (DTG), integrating Large 
Language Model (LLM) agents into an agent-based framework to generate individual-level 
mobility trajectories under evolving urban flood conditions. Validated against 1.32 billion 
mobility records from the 2021 “7.20” Zhengzhou flood in China, DTG outperforms all traditional 
and LLM-enabled baselines, achieving a flood-induced inflow-shift prediction accuracy of 0.713 
with outflow and OD distribution JSD of 0.069 and 0.047 during the disaster period. By 
combining accessibility-constrained disaster context reasoning with the representation of het­
erogeneous socio-demographic responses, DTG reveals critical infrastructure vulnerabilities and 
abnormal behavioral patterns, offering actionable insights for flood-resilient transport planning.
1. Introduction
Climate change has intensified the frequency and severity of extreme urban flooding events worldwide, posing unprecedented 
threats to the safety, livelihoods, and socioeconomic functioning of urban communities (Tang et al., 2023). During such events, routine 
mobility patterns are profoundly disrupted: residents abandon planned activities, seek emergency shelters, avoid inundated roads, and 
make rapid, context-dependent travel decisions under severe uncertainty (Deng et al., 2021; Guadagno, 2016). Even seemingly minor 
hazard incidents can trigger city-wide flow reorganization (Li et al., 2024), and the spatial configuration of disaster impacts funda­
mentally alters how transportation networks recover and how populations redistribute (Shen et al., 2024; Helderop and Grubesic, 
2019). Understanding the mechanisms through which flood-induced mobility shifts unfold, and how these shifts interact with the 
physical vulnerability of transportation infrastructure at both aggregate and individual levels, is therefore critical to effective emer­
gency evacuation planning, resilient transportation management, and equitable allocation of relief resources (Bagloee et al., 2019; 
Kumar et al., 2024).

Bottom-up reconstruction of individual-level travel behavior represents a promising approach for understanding the heterogeneous 
mobility responses that emerge under disaster conditions, as it allows direct examination of how personal risk perceptions, hazard 
exposures, and infrastructure constraints jointly drive divergent decisions (Golshani et al., 2019; Yabe et al., 2019; da Mata Martins 
et al., 2019). Unlike normal human movements, flood-induced travel exhibits stronger spatial heterogeneity, temporal non- 
stationarity, and pronounced individual variability driven by evolving hazard conditions and constrained infrastructure accessi­
bility (Han et al., 2019; Golshani et al., 2019), with evacuation decisions further shaped by disaster severity, warning lead time, and 
socioeconomic factors (Golshani et al., 2019; Yabe et al., 2019). At the network level, connectivity links break down, aggregation 
hotspots shift, and new infrastructure stress hotspots emerge in historically low-risk areas, patterns often driven by mobility structures 
rather than geographic proximity to the hazard (Li et al., 2024; Song et al., 2014, 2016; Tang et al., 2023). Capturing these coupled 
processes demands bottom-up modeling approaches that can jointly represent individual behavioral reasoning and those dynamically 
evolving spatiotemporal constraints, thereby enabling researchers to probe the micro-level mechanisms underlying macroscopic flow 
disruptions.
While empirical studies using mobile phone data (Fu et al., 2021; Xing et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2012), GPS traces (Yabe et al., 2017; 
Raei et al., 2026), and social media records have advanced post-disaster mobility analysis, such retrospective approaches are inher­
ently constrained by data availability: obtaining high-resolution trajectory data during sudden-onset urban floods remains extremely 
difficult given infrastructure disruption and privacy regulations (Luca et al., 2020). This data scarcity limits our capacity to anticipate 
population movement under disaster conditions, motivating the development of modeling-based alternatives.
Modeling-based approaches offer a potential means of overcoming this constraint, and have been employed in resilience assess­
ment and disaster planning (Li et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2022; da Mata Martins et al., 2019). However, existing modeling methods 
exhibit fundamental limitations in representing the micro-level behavioral heterogeneity that characterizes disaster mobility. Rule- 
driven approaches, including Markov-based models (Lv et al., 2016), gravity models (Gonzalez et al., 2008), and conventional 
agent-based models (Chen et al., 2023; Frias-Martinez et al., 2011), demonstrate generalizability in data-scarce settings but often 
depend on rigid, expert-crafted rules. Under extreme flood events, where individuals deviate substantially from routine behavior, such 
assumptions break down, and therefore, cannot capture the nuanced decision-making observed during emergencies (Golshani et al., 
2019). Data-driven approaches based on GANs (Cao et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2023) and VAEs (Chen et al., 2021) can reproduce 
complex spatiotemporal patterns from normal conditions, yet they depend heavily on extensive training data and face inherent 
challenges in out-of-distribution generalization (precisely the regime that disaster events impose). Moreover, both classes of methods 
often treat mobility as a statistical phenomenon and offer relatively limited insights into the behavioral reasoning that drives evac­
uation decisions, route choices, and destination shifts under extreme flooding conditions.
Recent advances in LLM-based generative agents have shown promise in modeling semantically coherent daily mobility trajectories 
through contextual understanding and role-playing capabilities (Liang et al., 2024; Park et al., 2023). This capacity to generate 
intention-consistent, interpretable travel behavior at the individual level offers a new avenue for probing the micro-level heterogeneity 
of disaster mobility. However, current LLM agents, while capable of generating contextually plausible text, are not inherently equipped 
to perform accessibility-grounded reasoning under dynamically evolving flood hazards, for instance, evaluating travel feasibility given 
time-varying flood impacts, and therefore cannot reliably represent how individuals adapt travel decisions to shifting risk and survival 
needs (Song et al., 2014; Shao et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). This deficiency constrains the insights that can be drawn regarding 
population redistribution, emerging resource pressure, and the coupling between human dynamics and the built environment during 
urban floods.
To address these gaps, this study introduces an integrated methodological approach, namely Disaster-aware Trajectory Generator 
(DTG), a modeling framework that employs LLM agents endowed with disaster-specific contextual knowledge to reason about indi­
vidual travel decisions under dynamically evolving hazard conditions, without requiring large-scale historical trajectory corpora 
specific to the target flood event, thereby enabling prospective simulation in data-sparse urban contexts. Grounded in the Theory of 
Planned Behavior (Ajzen, 1991), DTG comprises three core modules (incl., Daily Routing Generation, Geo-Alignment, and Disaster 
Context-aware Reasoning) that decouple travel motivation from spatial constraints. More importantly, the Disaster Context-aware 
Reasoning module integrates dynamically evolving hazard inputs to constrain the agent decision space, ensuring that generated 
mobility patterns align with both practical constraints and individual risk perceptions. Evaluated against 1.32 billion mobility records 
from the 2021 Zhengzhou “7.20” flood in China, DTG demonstrates consistent performance improvements over a range of conven­
tional rule-driven and recent LLM-based approaches.
Overall, this study contributes to the investigation of disaster-resilient transportation by advancing a behaviorally grounded, 
accessibility constrained approach that explicitly links micro-level individual decisions to macro-level transportation system perfor­
mance under extreme flood scenarios. The main contributions of this study are as follows: 
• We develop a data-efficient LLM-agent modeling framework that generates behaviorally realistic mobility trajectories under 
extreme urban flood scenarios, reducing dependency on event-specific historical data and thus enabling prospective analysis of 
disaster-induced mobility dynamics for cities where such data are limited.
• We embed the Theory of Planned Behavior into LLM reasoning and couple it with heterogeneous persona profiles, yielding 
intention-consistent, geographically plausible trajectories that capture the adaptive and demographically varied travel decisions 
observed in real floods. Ablation confirms that this theory-grounded reasoning, rather than statistical pattern replication, drives the 
model’s high disaster-adaptation accuracy.
• Through the mobility-driven emergency resource load (ERL) index, we demonstrate that post-disaster infrastructure stress is 
shaped more by evacuation flow convergence than by static population density. DTG thus functions as a scenario-testing tool that

2. Related work
2.1. Human mobility in disasters
During extreme disaster events, residents’ travel behaviors often deviate dramatically from their normal patterns (Deng et al., 
2021; Guadagno, 2016; Lu et al., 2017). Understanding these behaviors is fundamental for effective emergency traffic management 
(Bagloee et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2018). Previous research has leveraged diverse data sources to capture mobility dynamics during 
disasters. Among them, mobile phone signaling data has been widely used due to its extensive coverage, allowing researchers to 
uncover large-scale population movement and evacuation trends in the aftermath of disasters (Fu et al., 2021; Xing et al., 2021). For 
instance, Lu et al. (2012) analyzed mobile location data following the 2010 Haiti earthquake and found that, despite the severity of the 
disaster, population movement patterns remained largely predictable. This insight helped inform targeted delivery of aid. Similarly, 
GPS traces and social media check-in data have provided researchers with higher spatiotemporal resolution for investigating indi­
vidual evacuation choices and post-disaster recovery behaviors (Song et al., 2016; Yabe et al., 2017). Yabe et al. (2019) compared 
evacuation behaviors across multiple earthquake events, highlighting how disaster severity and early warning lead time significantly 
impact both the initiation time and spatial choices of evacuations. Machine learning methods have also been applied to disaster 
mobility modeling: for example, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks trained on historical evacuation data can forecast traffic 
demand and congestion patterns (Roy et al., 2021). However, such models remain heavily dependent on copious event-specific 
training data, and their predictive performance degrades markedly when confronted with the out-of-distribution conditions that 
characterize sudden-onset urban floods.
However, a common assumption across these studies is the availability of high-quality, event-specific human mobility or individual 
trajectory data. In reality, especially during sudden-onset events, obtaining such large-scale, high-resolution mobility data is extremely 
difficult or even impossible (Luca et al., 2020). This data availability bottleneck greatly limits the applicability of existing analysis 
methods, highlighting the urgent need to develop more effective low-data-dependency mobility modeling methods for better 
modeling.
2.2. Human mobility modeling
Human mobility modeling aims to generate large-scale, statistically realistic modeled movement data to support a wide range of 
applications, such as urban planning, disaster response, public health, and transportation studies (Feng et al., 2020; Song et al., 2016; 
U˘gurel et al., 2024). Existing approaches can be broadly categorized as rule-driven and data-driven methods.
Rule-driven approaches generate trajectories based on prior theoretical frameworks or statistical rules. Classic examples include 
Markov models (Lv et al., 2016) based on transition probability matrices and methods leveraging macro-level patterns, such as dis­
tance distributions following power laws (Gonzalez et al., 2008). This category also encompasses traditional Four-Step Models and 
simplified agent-based models (ABMs) (Chen et al., 2023; Frias-Martinez et al., 2011), both of which demonstrate strong generaliz­
ability in data-scarce environments. However, their fundamental limitation lies in rigid, pre-specified behavioral assumptions. Rule- 
driven methods often struggle to capture the nuanced travel decision processes at the microscopic level, such as spatiotemporal activity 
constraints, trip chaining behaviors, and the heterogeneous preferences influenced by socioeconomic attributes. As a result, while 
these models may reproduce reasonable aggregate flows, they often lack behavioral realism and semantic interpretability at the in­
dividual level. More importantly, under disaster conditions, infrastructure networks that underpin these behavioral assumptions, such 
as road capacities, travel times, destination accessibility, are themselves dynamically altered by flooding, rendering rule sets calibrated 
under normal conditions even more inapplicable.
Data-driven approaches, particularly those utilizing deep learning, have achieved remarkable success in recent years. Deep 
generative models based on Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) (Cao et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2023) or 
Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) (Chen et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2022) are capable of learning complex spatiotemporal patterns from 
large collections of real trajectories, thus producing highly realistic synthetic trajectories. While these models excel at replicating 
intricate mobility behaviors under normal conditions, they exhibit a strong dependence on the availability of extensive training data 
and face inherent challenges in out-of-distribution generalization. Disaster events fundamentally disrupt travel behaviors (Han et al., 
2019), rendering patterns learned from normal conditions invalid (Li et al., 2024a; Song et al., 2015; You et al., 2022) and limiting the 
effectiveness of data-driven models.
In summary, neither rule-driven nor data-driven approaches adequately capture the adaptive, accessibility constrained individual 
mobility behaviors that characterize disaster scenarios, motivating the need for a more integrated modeling approach.
2.3. LLM-based agents for social modeling
In recent years, large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated transformative potential across various domains owing to their 
strong capabilities in contextual learning (Brown et al., 2020) and zero-shot reasoning. Studies have shown that once LLMs reach a 
critical parameter scale, emergent complex reasoning abilities arise, enabling them to perform credible role-playing tasks (Shanahan 
et al., 2023). With the integration of modules such as memory, planning, and tool use, LLMs have been developed into generative

agents, capable of modeling human interactions within complex social environments and showcasing significant promise in modeling 
human decision-making (Lai et al., 2025; Movahedi and Choi, 2024; Park et al., 2023).
The application of LLM-based agents in urban science and transportation is still nascent, with early studies exploring their use in 
modeling daily activities and travel behaviors (Shao et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). To enable trajectory generation 
with LLMs, current research follows two primary strategies. One integrates LLM-generated travel intentions with classic trip models, 
such as coupling intention outputs with gravity models for destination choice prediction (Shao et al., 2024). The other uses a small set 
of real user trajectory histories as prompts, guiding the LLM to synthesize new trajectories consistent with individual preferences 
(Wang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023).
These studies show that LLMs can feasibly generate semantically coherent daily mobility trajectories. However, they struggle in 
disaster scenarios for two interconnected reasons. First, methods that rely on historical user trajectories fail because emergency be­
haviors deviate sharply from daily routines. Second, approaches that integrate classic models such as gravity models implicitly assume 
stable accessibility patterns, which break down when roads are inundated and facilities become inaccessible. More fundamentally, 
existing LLM-based trajectory generation lacks mechanisms to ground decisions in physical constraints, such as flood hazard hotspots, 
road closures, and network connectivity failures, leading to outputs that may be semantically plausible but physically infeasible.
Consequently, a key challenge lies in developing an integrated LLM agent modeling framework that, under the constraint of limited 
historical trajectory data, can generate emergency mobility trajectories that are both behaviorally realistic and physically feasible. 
Meeting this challenge requires embedding dynamic disaster context into the agent’s reasoning process while restricting its decision 
space to the physical disaster impacts. Solving this would not only advance mobility modeling per se, but also furnish a new analytical 
capability for flood-resilient transport planning, that is, the ability to prospectively identify hidden network vulnerabilities and stress- 
test mitigation strategies before a flood strikes.

3.2. Overall approach
The DTG framework comprises three tightly coupled modules that jointly operationalize the interaction between flood hazard 
dynamics and travel behavior (Fig. 1): (i) Daily Routing Generation translates traveler attributes and hazard awareness into activity- 
travel intentions via theory-grounded behavioral reasoning; (ii) Geo-Alignment maps these intentions onto reachable destinations; and 
(iii) Disaster Context-aware Reasoning continuously updates trip-chain decisions based on the agent’s current state and dynamic 
disaster context. Together, these modules form a closed hazard–behavior feedback loop, capturing how cascading flood impacts 
reshape individual mobility from intention formation through destination selection to en-route adaptation.
3.3. Daily routing generation
As the entry point of the hazard–behavior loop, this module converts traveler attributes and infrastructure failure states into 
activity-travel intentions. The LLM agent role-plays as an urban resident whose travel motivations are shaped by flood-induced 
physical constraints, including road-network disruption, facility closures, and degraded service capacity. We employ a Chain-of- 
Thought (CoT) reasoning process (Wei et al., 2022) grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior, combined with heterogeneous 
user profiles, to generate diverse yet psychologically plausible travel intentions that reflect the altered decision-making landscape of 
disaster scenarios.
3.3.1. TPB-guided individual decision-making
The generation of realistic and psychologically plausible travel intentions forms the cornerstone of our daily routing process. To 
achieve this, we base our approach on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) (Ajzen, 1991), a well-established model in social psy­
chology for predicting human behavior. TPB posits that behavioral intention (BI) is the most immediate precursor to actual behavior, 
and it’s shaped by three core factors: attitude (A), subjective norm (SN), and perceived behavioral control (PBC). Each factor con­
tributes to the overall intention with varying weights (w1,w2,w3), as expressed by the equation: 
BI = w1 ⋅A + w2 ⋅SN + w3 ⋅PBC
(1) 
In DTG, this formulation organizes the three TPB perspectives used in the agent’s CoT reasoning. Here, A represents an individual’s 
favorable or unfavorable evaluation of performing a specific behavior, SN reflects the perceived social pressure to engage or not engage 
in the behavior, and PBC signifies the perceived ease or difficulty of performing the behavior.
We operationalize TPB by designing a structured set of travel belief criteria, which are tailored to each user’s profile to model and 
generate realistic, personalized, and psychologically plausible mobility intentions, especially under disaster scenarios. This integration 
of TPB’s core components with the character’s profile is crucial for guiding the LLM agent’s decision-making patterns for both routine 
and disaster-responsive travel. This process forms a key part of our systematic CoT prompting strategy, enabling the LLM to derive 
intentions in a structured, theory-driven manner. In this CoT procedure, TPB guides the LLM to consider benefits, preferences, risks, 
and costs as attitude; perceived family, community, and official expectations as subjective norm; and time, distance, transport, flood, 
and facility constraints as perceived behavioral control when generating and ranking intentions.
Specifically, we guide the LLM agent through a multi-step reasoning process to model an individual’s internal deliberations:
First, the LLM agent generates candidate travel intentions hop by hop as an ordered daily intention list, denoted by Li=[qi,1,qi,2,…, 
qi,Ji], where qi,j=(ti,j,posi,j,ei,j) records the travel time, destination POI category, and travel purpose at hop j. At each hop, it forms a 
candidate set Ti,j from the detailed user profile (e.g., occupation, age, interests, and family status), the current environmental context 
(e.g., date, weather, and holiday status), the preceding intention state, and the city POI taxonomy. This step allows the LLM to consider 
plausible travel purposes while constraining posi,j to available destination categories. Under disaster conditions, the prompt addi­
tionally provides time-varying information about nearby rainfall and flooding, affected areas, roads and facilities, activated shelters, 
and official advisories. In addition, it provides several intention examples, such as seeking shelter, checking on family, and acquiring 
emergency supplies, as few-shot demonstrations of how a routine plan may change. Following the TPB-guided reasoning described 
below, the selected intention is returned as JSON, parsed into qi,j, and used to update the agent state for the next hop.
Once I cand is established, the LLM agent evaluates each candidate intention within this set in the same CoT reasoning process, 
considering the three TPB dimensions. For each intention Ii ∈I cand: 
1 Attitude Assessment: The LLM agent internally determines its stance toward each candidate intention based on the user profile and 
situational context, generating a preference ranking
2 Subjective norm Interpretation: The agent infers prevailing social expectations for each intention, considering societal conventions, 
peer or community practices, and relevant environmental conditions. In disaster contexts, prompts provide situational feedback 
such as the extent of local disruption, shaping the agent’s normative assessment

3.4. Geo-alignment
With travel intentions established, this module maps them to feasible destinations in the city. LLMs lack inherent geographic 
knowledge, and the candidate space is vast (e.g., over 870,000 POIs in Zhengzhou). To address this, we use a two-stage retrieval 
strategy that combines semantic relevance and spatial constraints to efficiently map abstract intentions to actionable destinations.

3.5. Disaster context-aware reasoning
The preceding stages have established plausible travel intentions and POI candidates, and the focus now shifts to determining the 
next destination, considering disaster-induced travel heterogeneity and accessibility constraints. Accessibility constraints here refer to 
subjective travel disruptions caused by objective factors like flooding, which reduce the willingness to visit certain destinations or 
interrupt travel plans. In dynamic disaster scenarios, where inundation zones expand and road closures propagate, individuals must 
adjust their decisions in real time. This section introduces the POI Reasoning module, which refines decision-making by incorporating 
contextual cues, user profiles, historical behavior, and disaster-specific factors. The Memory module further ensures trajectory con­
sistency, enabling adaptive adjustments as environmental conditions evolve.

3.5.2. Disaster context-aware adaptation
Urban floods are inherently evolving processes: inundation zones expand as rainfall persists, road closures propagate through the 
network as water levels rise, and emergency information is continuously updated by authorities. To faithfully reproduce this evolving

and facilities where access may be significantly impeded at time t; (iii) shelter status, including the set of currently activated emergency 
shelters and their locations; and (iv) official advisories, such as evacuation orders or travel warnings issued for specific districts.

heterogeneous progression of real flood impacts. A disaster-context adapter implements this query by matching the agent’s current

3.6. LLM tuning
To operationalize the hazard–behavior loop at scale, we distill the reasoning capability of a large teacher model (LLMT, e.g., GPT-

4.2. Data collection
We collected sociodemographic and built-environment data (Fig. 2): (i) population distribution from WorldPop (~1 km grid) 
(WorldPop, 2023); (ii) 870,000 POIs with categories, names, and coordinates; (iii) emergency shelter locations and capacities; and (iv)

For evaluation, we obtained mobile phone signaling data covering all of Zhengzhou from July 13–23, 2021 (~1.32 billion records), 
spanning the pre-disaster phase (July 13–19) and the disaster phase (July 20–23). The data were segmented into hourly intervals and 
processed into origin–destination (OD) matrices. All individual records were anonymized and spatially aggregated to grid cells of 1 km 
resolution before analysis, in compliance with local data protection regulations.
WorldPop is used solely as a publicly available static 1-km population prior for initializing the spatial distribution of synthetic 
residents. It does not provide dynamic mobility observations. Mobile-phone signaling data are not used for agent initialization or 
decision generation; after simulation, they serve as the aggregate benchmark for dynamic mobility validation. Evaluation aligns both 
sources to 1-km cells and compares normalized flow distributions and relative pre-/during-disaster changes rather than absolute user 
totals.
4.3. Baselines and metrics
Baselines. We compare against rule-driven and LLM-driven agent-based approaches that require minimal real trajectory data. 
Rule-driven baselines include Random Walk (Rhee et al., 2011), Gravity Model (Truscott and Ferguson, 2012), and Markov Model 
(Qiao et al., 2018). LLM-driven baselines include LLM-Mob (Wang et al., 2023), which models spatiotemporal dependencies, and CoPB 
(Shao et al., 2024), which combines chain-of-thought reasoning with geographic information.
Baselines operate within their native information interfaces. Rule-driven baselines use the structured inputs represented by their 
predefined transition, distance-decay, or historical-pattern mechanisms; they cannot directly take POI semantics and unstructured 
disaster text information as input. LLM-driven baselines are provided with POI information, disaster context, shelter information, and 
flood-affected areas through the input formats supported by their original frameworks. Because their prompting, architecture, and 
decision pipelines differ, these shared inputs are not processed through an identical end-to-end interface. All methods are evaluated in 
the same Zhengzhou study area, time period, initial spatial distribution, POI database, trajectory scale, 1-km evaluation grid, and 
metric system.
Metrics. We evaluate from both statistical and semantic perspectives. Statistical metrics: JSD-inflow/outflow (grid-level flow 
divergence), JSD-OD (OD distance distribution divergence), and flow Correlation (Corr.). Semantic: an LLM-as-Judge framework 
assigns a plausibility score to each trajectory (Li et al., 2024b). Disaster-adaptation metric (diff-ACC): a noise-filtered classification 
accuracy measuring agreement between modeled and observed inflow-change patterns (increase vs. decrease) across grid cells during 
the disaster-phase transition. This metric directly captures whether the model reproduces the directional flow reorganization induced 
by the flood, beyond pure distributional similarity.
Alongside aggregate flow metrics, the LLM-as-Judge score provides a supplementary assessment of the semantic and logical 
coherence of generated daily trajectories, including purpose–POI consistency, activity ordering, and consistency with hazard 
constraints.

5. Results
5.1. Overall performance
DTG demonstrates strong performance across both pre-disaster and during-disaster aggregate indicators, outperforming all 
baseline models in reproducing population flow distributions and disaster-induced behavioral transitions (Table 1). Our model notably 
excels in capturing the differential changes in population movement patterns during disasters and the transition from pre-disaster to 
disaster periods. This superiority is evident in DTG’s higher predictive accuracy for both evacuation and aggregation behaviors. 
Quantitatively, DTG exhibited the lowest JSD (inflow) and JSD (outflow) values for both pre-disaster (0.0603 and 0.0528, respec­
tively) and during-disaster (0.0707 and 0.0690) periods, indicating that our modeled mobility patterns most closely align with the 
statistical properties of macro-level population distributions. The model also achieved the highest diff-ACC of 0.7134 for capturing 
differential changes, significantly outperforming the baselines (Table 2), which will be discussed in detail in Section 5.2.
5.2. Disaster-adaptive flow modeling analysis
To evaluate the model’s disaster adaptation capability, this section systematically examines how well the DTG framework captures 
and reproduces such abnormal mobility dynamics across three dimensions: behavioral patterns, spatial redistribution, and human/ 
resource interaction. In Section 5.2.1, we compare different approaches for modeling two distinct patterns of inflow increase and 
inflow decrease during disasters to assess their performance in capturing these contrasting mobility behaviors. In Section 5.2.2, we 
aggregate modeled trajectories into mobility heatmaps to characterize the spatial redistribution of populations and assess the model’s 
consistency with real-world observations. In Section 5.2.3, we extend the analysis to human-resource interactions by constructing an 
Emergency Resource Load (ERL) index, which measures the dynamic balance between population inflows and shelter capacities. 
Through these analyses, we demonstrate that DTG effectively captures fine-grained behavioral adaptations, reproduces realistic spatial 
dynamics, and reflects the emergent human-environment resilience mechanisms observed in real disasters.
5.2.1. Two mobility patterns during flooding
Under extreme flooding, urban mobility reorganizes into a heterogeneous dual-state pattern: inflows decline in severely impacted 
and high-risk zones where normal activities are suspended, while inflows surge in safer locations and designated shelters receiving 
evacuees (Tang et al., 2023). Capturing this dual-state pattern is essential for realistic disaster-phase modeling and evacuation 
planning.
Fig. 3 shows the change in average daily inflow per 1 km grid cell between pre-disaster and disaster phases. DTG accurately re­
produces pronounced inflow reductions in the disaster-affected urban core (blue) and inflow increases in peripheral safe zones and 
shelters (red). Baselines fail in characteristic ways: RandomWalk produces spatially meaningless noise; GravityModel and LLM-Mob 
miss the nuanced spatial variation; MarkovFlow partially captures decrease zones but misidentifies increase zones. DTG’s advan­
tage stems from each agent’s ability to interpret environmental semantics (e.g., road flooded) and integrate them with persona-specific 
behavioral attributes (e.g., shelter-seeking) within a bottom-up reasoning process, allowing macro-scale avoidance and attraction 
patterns to emerge organically from individual decisions.
5.2.2. Spatial flow hotspots analysis
Accurate prediction of crowd aggregation hotspots is critical for emergency management, particularly in data-scarce regions where 
statistical extrapolation is unreliable. High-fidelity models are therefore essential for proactive planning and resource allocation. Our 
DTG model, with its bottom-up, scenario-aware agent-based decision-making mechanism, effectively models the spontaneous for­
mation of crowd hotspots under external shocks, offering a viable solution to this challenge.
Fig. 4 presents a population mobility density heatmap of Zhengzhou generated by the model during a disaster scenario. The 
modeling results exhibit strong agreement with real-world data in terms of overall spatial distribution and successfully capture the 
emergence of primary aggregation centers in the urban core and secondary hotspots in surrounding areas. This finding demonstrates 
the model’s effectiveness and reliability in reproducing crowd aggregation patterns during disasters.

The advantage of the DTG model becomes particularly evident in disaster scenarios, where travel decisions are highly dynamic and 
spatio-temporally heterogeneous. Traditional models may fall short in capturing such multi-factor, context-dependent decision-

pretability and adaptability. In one illustrative case, an agent originally planned to “go home after work”. However, upon receiving 
environmental feedback that the destination which located 18.3 km away had been affected by flooding, and that persistent heavy

nearby hotel accommodation. The reasoning process was explicitly expressed in the agent’s output: Given the ongoing heavy rain and 
flooding at my destination, traveling there would be highly inconvenient. I need to find a hotel nearby to rest.
This case shows that the DTG model is capable not only of generating plausible trajectories under normal conditions but also of 
dynamically adjusting decisions in line with human-like reasoning when faced with evolving environmental constraints. By modeling 
the underlying motives of travel behavior, DTG transcends the traditional paradigm of “pattern reproduction”, providing a deeper and 
more explanatory tool for understanding and predicting mobility under complex scenarios.

5.5. Pre-/during-disaster behavioral adaptation
To examine how simulated mobility adapts across disaster phases, we analyze fixed samples of 10,000 DTG-generated daily tra­
jectories in each of the pre-disaster and during-disaster periods. The comparison links changes in activity chains and destination 
composition with the spatial redistribution and context cues underlying these adjustments.
Flooding reorganizes activity space rather than producing a spatially uniform reduction in travel. The pre-/during-disaster com­
parison shows a reduced share of inflow in the top 10 % destination zones (approximately 58 % to 55 %) and an increase in short trips 
below 5 km (approximately 60 % to 64 %), while medium- and long-distance trips decline. These patterns indicate that hazard and 
accessibility constraints shrink feasible activity spaces and replace some cross-area trips with nearby accessible facilities.
Based on fixed samples of 10,000 agents in each period, Fig. 8 summarizes the same adjustment at the trajectory level. Mean hops 
per daily trajectory decline from 9.90 to 5.56. Shelter visits increase from 1.23 % to 11.26 %, while retail, recreation, and accom­
modation shares decline. Shelter is an operational category that includes dual-use urban facilities; it may therefore overlap with a 
facility's ordinary POI category.
Generated rationales provide descriptive supporting evidence: during disaster, heavy-rain, shelter, safety, and flooding cues appear 
in 49.8 %, 29.2 %, 13.4 %, and 11.4 % of rationales, respectively, whereas the nearest-location cue decreases from 29.5 % to 20.7 %. 
These cue frequencies indicate that safety and accessibility become more prominent constraints than proximity alone.
5.6. Ablation study
To assess the effectiveness of key components in the DTG model and examine the influence of hyperparameters, we conducted a 
series of ablation experiments. Our analysis focused on three core modules: (1) the User Profile Generator, which enhances the di­
versity of modeled populations; (2) POI Spatial Reranking, which enforces geographical plausibility of generated trajectories; and (3) 
the Disaster Context-aware Module, the central mechanism enabling adaptive behavior under disaster scenarios. Additionally, we 
investigated the effect of the decoding temperature hyperparameter.
The following model variants are evaluated on the during-disaster dataset, and the results are reported in Table 3. 
• DTG (Full Model): The complete framework with all components, serving as the performance baseline.
• without Profile: Removes the diversified user profile generator; all agents share a uniform, standardized resident profile.
• without Rerank: In the intent-to-POI mapping stage, uses only semantic similarity for coarse ranking, omitting spatial distance for 
reranking.
• without Disaster: Operates in disaster scenarios without any interactions between flood-affected built environment and behavioral 
responses, that is, we eliminate all dynamic context-aware information and considerations regarding coupled relationship for all 
agents.
• High Temp (T = 1.0): Full model with increased decoding randomness, leading to more diverse and creative token generation.
• Low Temp (T = 0.0): Full model with greedy decoding, removing stochasticity and thus producing more deterministic and less 
varied tokens.
The experimental results demonstrate that all of these components make positive contributions to the overall performance of the 
model.
(1) Removing the Disaster Context-aware Module (w/o Disaster) caused a substantial drop in diff-ACC (from 0.713 to 0.496) and 
consistent degradation across distributional metrics. Without dynamic perception and reasoning over disaster-related conditions, the 
model fails to reproduce adaptive behaviors, confirming the critical role of this module in understanding flood-induced mobility and 
disaster-affected built environment in travel decision-making. The residual diff-ACC of 0.496 likely reflects macro-level spatial 
structure inherited from the Geo-Alignment module and baseline persona patterns, which lack any dynamic hazard adaptation.
(2) Eliminating spatial reranking (without Rerank) led to substantial increases in JSD metrics, indicating that semantic matching 
alone is insufficient; without geographic distance constraints, agents tend to select semantically relevant yet geographically 
implausible POIs, which degrades the statistical realism of the generated trajectories.
(3) Removing the User Profile Generator (without Profile) also degraded performance, confirming that modeling heterogeneous 
population characteristics better captures the complexity of mobility patterns.

In addition to the component ablations and decoding-temperature analysis, Table 4 reports Geo-Alignment sensitivity. The balance 
coefficient β controls the relative role of semantic similarity and inverse distance; K is the number of POIs retained by coarse semantic 
retrieval; and P is the final number of spatially reranked candidates. When β approaches 0 or 1, either spatial proximity or semantic 
relevance is over-emphasized, increasing JSD and reducing Semantic; β = 0.50 provides the best balance. Increasing K from 100 to 200 
improves inflow, outflow, and OD consistency, while the additional gain at K = 300 is limited. In contrast, P has little effect on JSD but 
affects semantic quality: a small final candidate set restricts semantically relevant options, Semantic saturates around P = 30, and 
further expansion can introduce irrelevant POIs and computational cost. We therefore use β = 0.50, K = 200, and P = 30.
Beyond the hyperparameter sensitivity analysis for Geo-Alignment, we further examined the influence of memory representation in 
the Disaster Context-aware Reasoning stage. To assess whether the compact POI-sequence representation sacrifices behavioral 
coherence, we conducted a memory-field ablation. As shown in Table 5, removing the POI sequence reduces Semantic from 0.732 to 
0.518, whereas adding purpose, dwell-time, or visit-frequency fields changes Semantic by at most 0.006 relative to M0; the complete 
memory increases prompt tokens by 26.9 %. This supports the use of compact POI-sequence memory for the present city-scale, one-day 
objective; the additional fields can be incorporated when richer episodic continuity is required.
6. Discussion
DTG takes a fundamentally different approach to modeling disaster-induced human mobility: rather than fitting statistical patterns 
to historical data, it constructs a bottom-up agent-based model grounded in deliberate human decision-making. Each resident is 
represented as an LLM agent equipped with a persona-conditioned profile, episodic memory, and real-time disaster awareness, whose 
decisions about where to go, when to move, and for what purpose collectively produce emergent population-level mobility patterns. 
This design makes DTG a transparent, hypothesis-generating framework that does not require disaster-specific historical trajectory 
data and shows promising transferability to unseen scenarios, as the LLM’s reasoning operates on generic environmental descriptions 
rather than event-specific training samples.
The TPB-grounded reasoning module enables agents to generate shelter-seeking, family-reunification, and emergency-supply in­
tentions that are absent under normal conditions. Ablation confirms that removing the disaster context module causes the largest 
performance drop, affirming that adaptive behavioral reasoning, rather than statistical regularity, drives realistic disaster mobility 
modeling. This reinforces the value of theory-grounded behavioral modeling over rule-based heuristics (Golshani et al., 2019), and 
suggests that evacuation design should explicitly account for behavioral heterogeneity across population segments.
Complementing these behavioral findings, the ERL analysis reveals that disaster-time resource pressure is mobility-driven rather 
than density-driven: high-load zones emerge where shelter coverage is sparse but inflows converge, independent of pre-disaster 
population density (Li et al., 2024). This carries direct infrastructure implications, as evacuation corridor planning and shelter 
siting should draw on dynamic flow predictions rather than static density maps (Shen et al., 2024).
At the agent level, hop-by-hop disaster context injection enables agents to adaptively reroute as hazard conditions evolve. 
Aggregated across the population, this micro-level adaptivity reproduces the macro-level contraction and reorganization of mobility 
networks observed empirically. Unlike deep learning approaches (Cao et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2023), which function as opaque 
pattern replicators, this mechanism offers semantic interpretability, providing a transparent, auditable basis for evidence-based 
transportation resilience planning.
The same design also mitigates behavioral homogenization, a key failure mode in LLM-based multi-agent modeling, by coupling 
diverse persona profiles with geospatial constraints. Its architecture, which decouples the LLM from city-specific POI databases, 
demonstrates strong potential for cross-city and cross-hazard scalability.
Beyond modeling fidelity, DTG provides actionable decision support for transportation resilience planning. The model enables 
resilience gap quantification: contrasting pre-disaster and disaster-phase mobility under different inundation scenarios reveals how 
network accessibility degrades as hazard intensity increases. ERL outputs further serve as infrastructure prioritization signals, directing

Beyond TPB, disaster-behavior research offers complementary perspectives. Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) explains pro­
tective motivation through threat and coping appraisal (Rogers, 1975, 1983); the Protective Action Decision Model (PADM) describes 
how environmental cues, official warnings, and social information shape protective-action decisions, particularly warning response, 
evacuation, and sheltering (Lindell and Perry, 2012); the Health Belief Model (HBM) represents perceived susceptibility, severity, 
benefits, barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy (Rosenstock, 1974); and Prospect Theory explains risk-sensitive choice through 
reference dependence, loss aversion, and probability weighting (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979). These theories offer valuable ex­
tensions, but PMT and PADM principally organize protective action and warning response, HBM does not organize multi-purpose 
urban activity chains, and Prospect Theory does not itself prescribe a process from intention to destination. DTG therefore uses 
TPB as the intention-centered framework that connects persona and time-varying context to routine and disaster-related travel pur­
poses and subsequently to POI selection.
The present within-day memory records the POI sequence, which is sufficient to preserve the continuity of the daily activity chain 
in the current city-scale simulations; retaining additional temporal, purpose, dwell, or frequency fields yielded limited marginal 
benefit for the objectives considered here. The framework can nevertheless be extended naturally because these attributes can be 
expressed as compact natural-language descriptions and incorporated into the context at subsequent decision hops. Such an extension 
would allow the LLM to reason over richer episodic information, for example habitual dwell duration, repeated visits, or the purpose of 
an earlier trip, while retaining the same context-conditioned decision framework.
Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. LLM-based agents are susceptible to hallucination, generating spatially implausible 
outputs that require quality-control filtering, and output quality remains sensitive to prompt design. Large teacher models depend on 
cloud APIs, raising concerns about latency and data privacy in emergency contexts; the distilled 7B student model partially addresses 
this. Furthermore, the synthetic training data distilled from the teacher model may inherit subtle sociodemographic biases present in 
the LLM’s pre-training corpus, potentially affecting the representativeness of certain persona groups. Although Persona Hub broadens 
the diversity of agent conditions, it is not designed as a local census frame; explicit demographic calibration to the target city remains a 
potential direction for future work. The use of Euclidean distance as a spatial proximity proxy, while computationally efficient, may 
underestimate travel impedance in urban contexts with highly irregular street networks. Future iterations could incorporate network- 
distance-based formulations. Finally, the empirical validation of our framework faces constraints in both scope and resolution. On the 
one hand, validation is currently restricted to a single flood event; therefore, generalization to other hazard types and urban contexts 
requires broader testing. On the other hand, strictly privacy-protected reference data cannot support point-by-point validation against 
real individual trajectories. Consequently, individual-level outputs are currently evaluated for semantic and logical coherence only.
7. Conclusion
In this study, we introduce the Disaster-aware Trajectory Generator (DTG), a modeling framework designed to simulate urban 
mobility under extreme flood scenarios by extending behaviorally-grounded agent reasoning from routine activity patterns into 
disaster-disrupted conditions. Departing from the conventional reliance on historical trajectory corpora, DTG employs Large Language 
Model (LLM) agents to emulate heterogeneous resident behaviors and generate intention-consistent movements. To bridge abstract 
agent intentions with the city’s physical reality, we implement a geospatial anchoring mechanism that integrates semantic relevance 
with spatial distance, yielding geographically viable next-POI decisions.
Validated against 1.32 billion mobility records from the Zhengzhou “7.20” flood, DTG surpasses both rule-based and state-of-the- 
art LLM baselines. Macroscopically, DTG achieves superior distributional alignment and higher correlation, more accurately capturing 
the differential shifts in crowd movement during catastrophic events. At the granular level, it generates semantically coherent activity 
chains that remain strictly aligned with individual agent roles and intentions.
Beyond merely replicating flows, DTG enhances emergency planning by uncovering dynamic emergency resource loads (ERL). Our 
analysis reveals that high-stress zones can materialize in historically low-risk areas driven by transient population inflows, highlighting 
the critical necessity of context-aware behavioral modeling for adaptive relief and evacuation strategies.
While DTG demonstrates robust fidelity in simulating urban flooding scenarios, we acknowledge specific computational trade-offs. 
The current implementation excludes direct agent-to-agent interactions due to processing constraints. Future research should prior­
itize scalable methods to model collective dynamics and extend the framework to a broader spectrum of hazard types and urban 
transportation contexts.

Appendix A. Prompt template for travel decisions
This appendix provides the representative prompt used when DTG selects an actual destination at each daily-routing decision hop. 
After TPB-guided reasoning generates the current travel intention and Geo-Alignment retrieves and spatially reranks POI candidates, 
the prompt combines the agent profile, current state, within-day trajectory history, localized disaster context, and candidate POIs to 
support destination selection. The disaster-context adapter refreshes the relevant local information at every hop; under routine con­
ditions, the same template is used with the corresponding non-disaster environment. Variables in double braces are populated from the 
agent state and current local context.
[User Profile] Please act as {{persona}}, a city resident deciding the next travel action. Keep actions consistent with the role's 
occupation, age, interests, household situation, routine, and risk preference; adapt the routine when safety, accessibility, or basic needs 
change.
[Current State and Environment] Your current date, weekday, weather, time, location, and routine setting are {{current_­
state_and_environment}}. Your current travel intention is {{current_intention}}. First assess whether it remains reasonable and 
feasible.
[Trajectory History] Today’s previous visits and activities are {{trajectory_history}}. Use this history to preserve continuity of the 
within-day activity chain.
[Disaster Context] The adapter has transformed the local rainfall, waterlogging, affected roads/facilities, shelters, and official 
advice into {{disaster_context}}. Consider the accessibility of each candidate POI.
[Available POIs] The following are semantically retrieved and spatially reranked candidate POIs: {{retrieved_poi_candidates}}. 
Select one actual POI that fits the persona and current intention while considering distance, flood impact, safety, accessibility, and 
facility availability.
[Few-shot Demonstrations] {{few_shot_demonstrations}}. Shelter seeking, checking on family, and obtaining supplies are il­
lustrations only, not required actions or a closed purpose set.
[TPB Guidance] {{tpb_guidance}}. Internally evaluate attitude (benefits, preference fit, risk, and travel/detour cost), subjective 
norm (family responsibility, community expectations, and official advice), and perceived behavioral control (safety, accessibility, and 
feasibility under time, distance, transport, flood, and facility conditions). Then choose the most appropriate action.
[Output Format] Output JSON only: {‘time’: ‘HH:MM’, ‘poi_selection’: {‘poi_name’: ‘selected candidate POI’, ‘poi_category’: ‘POI 
category’}, ‘purpose’: ‘travel purpose’, ‘reason’: ‘brief persona-, context-, and accessibility-based rationale’}.
