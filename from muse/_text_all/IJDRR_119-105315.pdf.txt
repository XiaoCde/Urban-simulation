ABSTRACT
Households often refuse to evacuate until floodwaters surpass their coping thresholds. Grasping 
the root causes of this behavior is crucial for implementing effective interventions amid escalating 
flood risks. While existing literature offers insights into evacuation decisions for various hazards, 
it overlooks key motivations and barriers specific to flood evacuation in rural Vietnam. This study 
endeavors to bridge this gap by investigating the factors influencing rural households’ evacuation 
decisions in response to the significant 2020 floods in Central Vietnam. Data for analysis were 
gathered through in-person interviews with 407 households across Phong Dien, Quang Dien, and 
Huong Tra districts of Thua Thien Hue Province. The logistic regression model results disproved 
the influence of variables associated with sociodemographic characteristics, hazard proximity, 
and flood risk experience. The findings, meanwhile, underscore the impact of those related to 
threat appraisal, coping appraisal, trust in external support, and perceived evacuation barriers. 
Proper assessment of flood threats has emerged as a critical factor in promoting evacuation, 
whereas overconfidence in one’s coping capacity and the overestimation of external support 
adversely affect evacuation decisions. Concerns about potential property loss further hinder 
evacuation motivation. The proposed interventions encompass prioritizing educational campaigns to clarify flood risk dynamics, categorizing household structure resilience against floods, 
highlighting challenges associated with self-evacuation, mitigating reliance on external support, 
enhancing living conditions in evacuation sites, and implementing security measures during flood 
seasons.
1. Introduction
Climate change has increased the frequency and severity of natural disasters globally [1]. Floods have emerged as a significant 
concern, inflicting widespread devastation and posing a substantial risk to communities worldwide. According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), climate variability and extreme weather events contribute to the intensification of flooding 
occurrences [2]. This trend underscores the importance of robust disaster preparedness and mitigation efforts, particularly in regions 
vulnerable to flooding [3].

Nestled within a geographical zone susceptible to extreme climate and natural hazards, Vietnam faces significant challenges in 
mitigating the pervasive threats posed by such disasters, particularly floods. The Global Climate Risk Index 2021 report has underscored Vietnam’s vulnerability, ranking it among the top 20 nations most affected by extreme climatic events over the past two decades 
[4]. Vietnam ranks sixth worldwide in terms of population share, with 45.8%—approximately 45.5 million people—facing substantial 
flood risks, according to the 2020 Report on Poverty and Shared Prosperity [5]. With its unique geographical and climatic characteristics, Central Vietnam exemplifies this vulnerability and often bears the brunt of recurrent flooding. The devastating flood of 
November 1999, which claimed at least 547 lives and left dozens missing, remained a stark reminder of the susceptibility of the region. 
Recent data on the 2020 floods in Vietnam’s central provinces further highlight the ongoing risks, resulting in the loss of 249 lives and 
substantial economic damages exceeding 30,000 billion Vietnamese dong (approximately equivalent to $1.2 billion USD [6,7]. These 
circumstances emphasize the urgent need for robust disaster preparedness and mitigation.
Minimizing flood damage has emerged as a critical priority for the Vietnamese Government. Despite the diverse range of riskmitigation measures, these efforts generally fall into structural and non-structural categories [6,8]. Structural measures, such as the 
construction of reservoirs, irrigation dams, and large hydropower plants, have been instrumental in reducing the frequency of floods. 
However, their efficacy in decreasing the flood intensity remains limited [7]. During heavy rainfall, the authorities often face the 
dilemma of rapidly releasing water from these structures to prevent dam failure. This unintended consequence can lead to sudden and 
widespread downstream flooding, posing a significant threat to individuals and property. Recent flood events, notably those in 2017 
and 2020 that rival the magnitude of the historic 1999 flood, illustrate the shortcomings of relying solely on structural measures. 
Studies conducted in this region underscore the pivotal role of rural households in mitigating flood risk through proactive preparedness measures [7,8]. Although their efforts are commendable and effective in managing minor flood events, significant and 
unpredictable floods necessitate evacuation to safeguard lives and essential assets. Our field observations indicate that despite the 
imperative to evacuate, households exhibited varied responses to evacuation warnings. While some evacuate preemptively to mitigate 
risks, others remain home despite rising floodwaters and official evacuation advisories despite residing in high-risk areas. This reality 
underscores the intricate nature of evacuation decision-making, which can be influenced by various factors. Therefore, comprehending 
the motivations and barriers that shape household evacuation decisions is crucial for designing targeted and effective interventions.
Evacuation decisions in response to natural hazards have garnered global research attention. Human behavior has been explored 
using various methodological approaches encompassing diverse geographical contexts and types of natural hazards, offering insights 
into the factors influencing individuals’ and households’ evacuation decisions. Commonly studied natural hazards include hurricanes 
[9–15], floods [16–19], cyclones [20,21], tsunamis [22], landslides [23], earthquakes [24], and wildfires [25]. In Vietnam, a country 
with a substantial portion of its population vulnerable to flood risks, flood risk reduction has garnered significant research attention 
among scholars across various disciplines [6–8,26–29]. Despite this focus, a notable gap remains in the literature concerning Central 
Vietnam regarding examining floodplain residents’ evacuation behavior, which plays a crucial role in risk mitigation. Understanding 
the drivers and barriers shaping individual and household decision-making processes is imperative. Such comprehension can show 
why many people disregard evacuation recommendations and remain in their homes despite imminent danger. Addressing these 
inquiries serves as pivotal groundwork for proposing effective intervention measures to enhance evacuation rates in response to flood 
hazards.
Acknowledging the importance of evacuation decision-making and the knowledge gaps in the literature, this study delves into the 
determinants shaping rural households’ evacuation decisions during the 2020 flood in Thua Thien Hue Province, Central Vietnam. By 
examining these determinants, our research contributes to the discourse on disaster preparedness and response, aiming to deepen our 
understanding of the intricate dynamics shaping flood evacuation decisions among rural communities, thereby providing a foundation 
for formulating informed policies and targeted interventions to bolster disaster preparedness and response mechanisms.
2. Literature review and conceptual framework
This section provides insights from previous studies on the factors influencing evacuation decisions and behaviors across different 
types of natural disasters, including floods. While this research focuses specifically on floods in a developing country, examining 
various disaster scenarios is essential. Such an approach allows us to identify overarching patterns and insights into evacuation behaviors, which can inform a more comprehensive understanding of flood-specific contexts and enhance the generalizability of the 
findings.
Owing to the interdisciplinary nature of the problem, scholars from various research backgrounds have identified the factors that 
influence evacuation decisions and behaviors. Among the fundamental factors considered, sociodemographic and socioeconomic 
characteristics have been the most explored.
Sociodemographic characteristics such as gender, age, education, household size, presence of vulnerable people (children, elderly, 
individuals with special needs), race and ethnicity, pets, and so on, often influence evacuation decisions [9–12,16,17,30–34]. Older 
adults are generally more susceptible to health risks during disasters and are often more compliant with official evacuation orders, 
which can increase their likelihood of evacuation. Their prior experiences with disasters may enhance their awareness of the dangers 
associated with remaining in place [35–37]. Nonetheless, the decision to evacuate can be complicated by physical and cognitive 
limitations, such as reduced mobility and confusion under stress. Furthermore, a strong attachment to a place or a desire to avoid the 
discomfort of evacuation shelters may influence their choice to stay behind [38,39]. Previous studies have also emphasized the 
increased likelihood of evacuation among households with young children, as well as those with members who have disabilities or 
chronic illnesses [16,17,31]. The presence of vulnerable household members, such as children, the elderly, or individuals with disabilities, often heightens the motivation to evacuate due to concerns for their safety. Caregivers may feel an increased responsibility to

protect these individuals, yet limited resources can sometimes hinder their ability to take necessary protective actions. Conversely, the 
presence of these vulnerable members can also reduce the likelihood of evacuation, particularly if the process is perceived as too 
difficult or risky for them [40,41]. For instance, households with elderly or disabled members may be hesitant to evacuate due to the 
logistical challenges involved or concerns about whether shelters can adequately meet their needs. A study by Lim et al. [16] in Quezon 
City, Philippines, emphasized the adverse impact of educational attainment on evacuation decision-making. This study found that the 
probability of evacuation decreased as the educational level increased. While this finding aligns with Mondal’s [17] research on 
Riverine Floodplain Communities in Bangladesh, it contradicts the conclusions of several other studies, including those by Whitehead 
et al. [9], Hasan et al. [42], Hasan et al. [43], and Medina and Moraca [31]. For example, the study by Medina and Moraca [31], 
conducted in the flood-prone area of Bukidnon, Philippines, revealed that individuals with a university degree were twice as likely to 
evacuate compared to those with lower levels of education. Additionally, educated respondents were more likely to seek refuge with 
friends or family in non-flooded areas, thereby alleviating congestion in evacuation shelters. This shift allows disaster managers to 
more effectively allocate resources to low-income groups who are dependent on these facilities. Education is often associated with 
greater evacuation motivation, as it enhances risk perception, access to information, and trust in official warnings [44]. Educated 
individuals generally better understand disaster risks and take proactive protective measures [45–47]. However, in certain contexts, 
education may reduce evacuation motivation. Highly educated individuals might overestimate their ability to manage risks, fostering a 
false sense of security and leading them to stay behind [40,48]. Furthermore, they may distrust authorities or question evacuation 
orders, especially if they have previously experienced false alarms [49,50]. Conversely, those with lower educational levels may lack 
access to accurate information or may distrust official warnings, leading to delayed or no evacuation.
Gender has been consistently highlighted as a significant factor in evacuation behavior, with various studies elucidating the 
complexities of this relationship. Bateman and Edwards [10] demonstrated that women, particularly those heading households, are 
more inclined to evacuate during hurricanes due to heightened risk perception and a stronger emphasis on family safety. This elevated 
propensity is attributed to socially constructed gender roles that assign caregiving responsibilities to women, thus increasing their 
awareness of and responsiveness to evacuation warnings. Women’s heightened risk perception is often compounded by their residence 
in areas of greater risk exposure compared to men. However, gender can also diminish evacuation motivation, especially in patriarchal 
cultures where men may be reluctant to leave their homes. This reluctance stems from a perceived duty to protect property and societal 
expectations of bravery, leading men to downplay risks or prioritize economic concerns, which can result in delayed or non-evacuation 
[51,52]. From another perspective, Whitehead et al. [9] and DeYoung et al. [11] identified a significant association between racial 
dynamics and evacuation decisions. Specifically, DeYoung et al. [11] exhibited that White individuals are more likely to comply with 
both mandatory and voluntary evacuation orders. Meanwhile, Whitehead et al. [9] observed that non-White households were less 
inclined to utilize motels, hotels, or shelters, opting instead to seek refuge with friends or family.
Research by Lindell and Perry [53] and Lindell [54] offered a robust theoretical framework derived from five decades of evacuation 
studies. Complementing this, a systematic review by Huang et al. [55] synthesized the existing literature on the factors influencing 
hurricane evacuation decisions. The statistical meta-analysis (SMA) by Huang et al. [47] indicated that demographic variables are 
generally weak and inconsistent predictors of evacuation behavior. Supporting this perspective, a recent study by Lindell et al. [56] 
examining flash floods in Uttarakhand, North India, found only modest correlations between demographic characteristics and various 
aspects of response, including behavioral actions, information sources, and psychological reactions. These conclusions align with 
earlier research on hurricane evacuation (Baker [57] and Huang et al. [55]) and earthquake hazard adjustment (Lindell [58] and by 
Lindell & Perry [59]), underscoring the limited predictive power of demographic factors across different hazard contexts.
Socioeconomic characteristics serve as valuable predictors of evacuation behavior among individuals and households. Medina and 
Moraca [31] illustrate that individuals with an average income of less than 5000 pesos exhibit a greater likelihood of evacuation. 
Meanwhile, Maghelal et al.’s [12] research findings indicated that households with higher income levels frequently evacuated to 
multiple groups at various times. These findings diverge from the conclusions of Whitehead et al. [9] and Adeola [60], who reported 
that job status and total income were not significant determinants of evacuation behavior. Wealthier households typically have better 
access to private vehicles, can afford to book hotels, or stay with friends and family in safer areas, facilitating a timely and comfortable 
evacuation. In contrast, lower-income households face significant financial barriers that impede their ability to evacuate. These 
barriers include a lack of private transportation, insufficient funds for gas, accommodation, or emergency supplies, and limited 
financial flexibility for last-minute departures. As a result, lower-income individuals are more likely to remain in disaster-prone areas, 
which heightens their vulnerability to the adverse effects of natural disasters [40,44,61]. Lim et al. [16] and Whitehead et al. [9] 
investigated the influence of housing characteristics to investigate evacuation decision making. Lim et al. [16] revealed that households in multilevel homes displayed a heightened propensity to remain in place during evacuations, as evidenced by the negative 
coefficients. They further noted that households dwelling in structures constructed of concrete demonstrated a heightened tendency to 
stay at home during evacuation events compared to those inhabiting wooden structures. Household members often opt to stay at home 
during evacuation events driven by a sense of security, safety, and comfort. This tendency is reinforced by the availability of ample 
food and supplies prepared in advance. Additionally, homeowners exhibited an increased likelihood of evacuation compared with 
renters. Renters are less inclined to evacuate due to concerns about property theft. Ng et al. [62] reported similar results, in contrast to 
the findings of Hasan et al. [42] and Maghelal et al. [12]. Whitehead et al. [9] found that residents of mobile homes were nearly seven 
times more inclined to evacuate than individuals residing in other types of residences. Overall, the research underscores the pivotal 
role of socioeconomic characteristics in shaping evacuation behavior.
Concerning psychological factors, risk perception or threat assessment is crucial for understanding evacuation behavior [9,20,22,
23,25]. Kakimoto et al. [23] highlighted that precautionary evacuation is primarily motivated by factors associated with threat appraisals such as the anticipated severity and frequency of damage. The fear of natural disasters further encouraged households to take

precautionary evacuations. Whitehead et al. [9] revealed that households facing higher flood risk were nearly twice as likely to 
evacuate compared to those with lower risk levels, with the evacuation likelihood increasing as the flood risk escalated. Individuals 
with a deeper understanding of local disaster response plans are more likely to adhere to evacuation directives [13]. Similarly, Parvin 
et al. [20] and Sugiura et al. [22] underscore the significant influence of risk and threat perceptions on evacuation decisions. McLennan 
et al. [25] emphasized how a location’s threat history can shape individuals’ evacuation decisions and behaviors.
The studies conducted by Lim et al. [16], Kakimoto et al. [23], Parvin et al. [20], McLennan et al. [25], Paul [63], and the SMA by 
Huang et al. [55] provide further insights into the role of warning information. Paul [63] highlighted that trust in warning messages—often known as "warning belief" (e.g., Mileti & Beck [64])—emerged as the primary determinant influencing the decision to seek 
refuge in safer shelters. Huang et al. [55] SMA determined that an official warning—such as a National Hurricane Center hurricane 
watch or warning, a local official’s recommendation, or a voluntary or mandatory evacuation order—serves as a strong predictor of 
household evacuation. This is likely because individuals perceive these authorities as possessing a high level of expertise regarding 
hazards, transmitting information accurately, and bearing the responsibility to alert households at risk. Similarly, Lim et al. [16] 
revealed that households exhibit a greater inclination to evacuate when they receive official evacuation warnings from authorities as 
opposed to information from other sources such as friends, relatives, television, or radio, suggesting a preference to await official 
guidance, thereby strengthening their resolution to evacuate. The decision to evacuate was significantly associated with warnings 
issued by local authorities rather than those from local news media sources, as demonstrated in studies by Lindell et al. [65], Mileti 
et al. [66], and Taylor et al. [67]. Insufficiently informative warnings can lead to self-delayed evacuation, wherein residents are more 
likely to prioritize information searches over initiating evacuation actions [25,68,69]. Flood events occur more frequently than instances when evacuation alarms are activated, underscoring the necessity of enhancing trust between public authorities and citizens 
and implementing self-reliant evacuation strategies in high-risk areas [23]. Individuals perceive information through their cultural 
context, leading to varied interpretations of the same information and messages. Warnings alone do not prompt evacuation and individuals must perceive the level of risk involved [40].
Other factors, though often overlooked, have been reported to affect household evacuation decisions. Alongside hazard-related 
aspects, such as floodwater depth and proximity to water sources, which significantly influence evacuation choices [16,31], individuals often postpone evacuation until they discern unequivocal signs of risk, actively seek warning messages through various 
channels, including monitoring river behavior, rainfall patterns, and cloud formations, or consult alternative sources for alerts [63]. 
Furthermore, concerns related to looting risks, distance to flood shelter locations, space availability, facility inadequacies, and social 
variables such as residence duration and experience play a substantial role in evacuation decisions [21,60,70]. The interplay of social 
dynamics within communities further shapes evacuation intentions, with psychological factors such as perceived inconvenience 
affecting precautionary evacuation behaviors [23]. Analyzing various demographic groups’ responses during evacuation scenarios 
emphasizes the importance of factors such as cyclone warning content, timing of evacuation directives, and shelter accessibility [20]. 
Additionally, the indispensable role of non-governmental organizations (NGOs) in emergency responses, encompassing relief distribution and evacuation efforts, cannot be overstated [17]. Individual differences in social capital and cohesion also contribute to 
disparities in evacuation rates, highlighting the need for tailored evacuation messages directed at diverse demographic groups [71].
While literature has provided valuable insights into the factors influencing evacuation decisions across various natural hazards, a 
conspicuous gap exists concerning the motivations and barriers unique to flood evacuation in the Vietnamese context. Given a 
country’s escalating vulnerability to floods, notably in the central region, a comprehensive understanding of flood evacuation decisionmaking in this setting is imperative. Despite the pressing need for research in this domain, there is a dearth of empirical studies on the 
intricacies of flood evacuation decision-making processes in Vietnam. This study addresses this gap by comprehensively analyzing the

After conducting a comprehensive review of the existing research, our study further refined its focus through consultations with 
flood preparedness experts and group discussions in the study areas. This iterative process aimed to formulate a conceptual framework 
for this study, comprising the most relevant factors. By integrating expert knowledge with academic scholarship, we strive to enhance 
the precision and relevance of understanding flood evacuation decision-making processes in rural Vietnam. Key categories encompassing sociodemographic characteristics, hazard proximity, risk experience, threat appraisal, coping appraisal, trust in external 
support, and perceived evacuation barriers were identified. The terms "threat appraisal" and "coping appraisal" are derived from 
Rogers’ [72] Protection Motivation Theory (PMT), which suggests that individuals’ protective behaviors are influenced by their 
perception of a threat and their perceived ability to cope with it. These terms have been widely used in recent research, such as 
Babcicky & Seebauer [73], Floyd et al. [74], Oakley et al. [75], Shillair [76], Faryabi et al. [77], to explore protective behaviors in the 
context of natural hazards and risk management.

analyze the influence of the factors separately. The categorization of these factors, as illustrated in Fig. 1, aims to enhance our analysis’s clarity and systematic nature, thereby facilitating a more structured and coherent discussion of the results obtained.
3. Study site and methods
3.1. Study site
This research was conducted in Thua Thien Hue Province (with a total population of about1.167 million people in 2023), a region 
in Central Vietnam profoundly susceptible to natural disasters. Situated along a coastline of over 100 km and traversed by a complex 
river network, the province is particularly vulnerable to various natural disasters, including tropical storms and flooding. Flood risks in 
this location originate from multiple sources, including coastal surges, river inundation, and torrential rains, which often occur 
concomitantly. The intricate interplay between these factors amplifies the complexity of flood risk assessment and management. 
Approximately two-thirds of the province’s population is estimated to be exposed to flood hazards during intense rainfall.
Floods significantly impede socioeconomic development in various localities, plunging numerous households into a relentless cycle 
of poverty [6,8,78–80]. Thua Thien Hue Province, in particular, has endured numerous devastating flood events over recent decades. 
On average, the province faces 3–4 floods at alarm level 2 and higher each year. Flood magnitudes also tend to be erratic and difficult 
to predict, posing challenges for governmental and community response activities (Table 1). The 1999 flood deemed the most severe 
flood in the province’s history, remained etched in collective memory as a harrowing episode. During this calamity, the recorded 
precipitation within a single day amounted to a staggering 1422 mm, equivalent to nearly the annual rainfall. The ensuing deluge 
surpassed all expectations, with floodwaters at Kim Long Station on the Huong River reaching a height of 5.81 m, equivalent to a 
two-story house. This catastrophic event claimed hundreds of lives, left dozens missing, and caused extensive damage to approximately 
650,000 homes. The tangible economic toll was estimated to be as high as 4536 billion VND [6,8,81].
The efforts of the provincial government to construct massive structures such as the Ta Trach, Binh Dien, Huong Dien, and A Luoi 
dams have contributed significantly to the reduction in flood frequency. However, despite these efforts, mitigating the flood intensity 
remains challenging. The considerable flood event in 2017 served as a poignant example, arising in the aftermath of the formidable 
storm No. 12, named Damrey. Damrey, with wind speeds of up to 150 km/h, has been one of Vietnam’s most powerful storms over the 
past two decades. Constrained by its limited capacity, the government was compelled to release water to prevent dams from bursting. 
However, this necessary action, coupled with prolonged heavy rains, resulted in flooding conditions comparable to those of the 1999 
historic flood in rural areas.
Most recently, in 2020, a series of consecutive and impactful floods occurred in the central region of Vietnam, encompassing Thua 
Thien Hue Province. Beginning on October 6 and persisting until October 13, the initial flood severely affected infrastructure and 
human life in the central provinces, causing widespread devastation. A subsequent flood occurred on October 16, coinciding with a 
new tropical depression in the central region that evolved into a storm. Notably, the flood peaks at certain hydrological stations 
surpassed the levels recorded during the 1979 and 1999 events, establishing a new and unprecedented record. The third flood occurred

3.2. Methods
3.2.1. Data collection
The basic data for this study were obtained through household surveys, group discussions, and key informant interviews conducted 
in the Phong Dien, Quang Dien, and Huong Tra districts of Thua Thien Hue province, Central Vietnam. Following the pilot survey 
conducted in July 2022, the questionnaire was redesigned for the official survey conducted from January to April 2023.
Six group discussions were held across the three districts to enhance rigor and contextual relevance, involving 11–16 participants 
per session, including local authorities, village leaders, and flood-affected households. These discussions addressed flood risk perceptions, impact trends, coping strategies, and evacuation behaviors. Additionally, seven key informant interviews with provincial 
disaster management experts, local officials, and village leaders provided insights into recent flood risk trends, government efforts, and 
institutional responses to flood risk management. These qualitative methods informed the household survey design, grounding it in 
local realities and adding critical contextual depth to interpreting the logistic regression results. They shed light on factors influencing 
evacuation decisions that might not be evident from survey data alone, enabling a comprehensive understanding of household responses to flood risks.
Focusing on households’ decisions regarding flood evacuation, the sample size was calculated based on the number of households 
experiencing flooding exceeding 0.5 m, according to Thua Thien Hue province’s statistical data. Application of the TaroYamane 
formula [82] for an estimate of the minimum sample size yielded N = 445 households. However, only information from 407 
households with complete data was used in the analysis.

Household interviews were conducted using a semi-structured questionnaire developed in consultation with local experts on flood 
risk reduction and refined through group discussions and pilot household interviews. We incorporated different question types, 
including closed, semi-closed, and open-ended questions, to capture quantitative and qualitative information. The questionnaire

related to the 2020 flood (preparation, damages, evacuation, etc.), flood threat appraisal, flood-coping appraisal, trust in external 
support, and perceived evacuation barriers. Most of the questionnaire content was addressed sequentially during the interviews, 
except for a few instances in which the interviewer exhibited flexibility in adapting to and following the informants’ narratives. In 
addition to the semi-structured questionnaire, in-depth interviews were conducted with several households to gather qualitative insights. These interviews aimed to explore personal experiences during the 2020 flood, the underlying reasons behind evacuation 
decisions, and perceived barriers to evacuation. The qualitative data obtained from these interviews complemented the survey results 
by offering a deeper understanding of the factors influencing household preparedness and evacuation behaviors, which could not be 
fully captured through the quantitative survey alone.

3.2.2. Data analysis
This study employed a mixed-methods approach, combining both quantitative and qualitative methods. The quantitative approach 
was used to examine trends and associations among variables, while the qualitative analysis provided further insights into the meaning 
and context behind the numerical data. The integration of both methods allows for a more comprehensive understanding of the 
phenomenon, as quantitative data offers statistical generalizability, whereas qualitative data enhances the interpretation by exploring 
deeper motivations and experiences that might not be captured by numerical analysis alone [83,84]. This combination is well-suited 
for understanding complex phenomena like flood evacuation decisions, where patterns and individual experiences must be considered 
to grasp the decision-making process fully.
The key focus of this study is to utilize a binary logistic regression model to investigate the factors influencing household evacuation 
decisions in response to the 2020 historic floods. Our pilot survey revealed diverse evacuation practices, encompassing scenarios such 
as early evacuation (initiated during low floodwater levels) and delayed evacuation (implemented as floodwater rises). Evacuation 
types also varied between voluntary, mandatory, and partial evacuation (involving only specific family members) and complete 
evacuation (involving all family members). Experience has shown that delayed evacuation, which occurs when floodwaters rise, poses 
considerable challenges and risks. Under such circumstances, many households cannot influence self-evacuation and often require 
external assistance. Similarly, the decision to forego a complete evacuation strategy poses manifold risks to individuals who choose to 
remain in their residences. Hence, complete evacuation while floodwaters remain low is a crucial prerequisite for reducing the flood 
risk.
In this study, households that evacuated completely when the floodwater was still low (without substantial external assistance) 
were categorized as “early complete evacuation” and assigned a value of “1” in the model. Conversely, all other cases, including those 
who did not evacuate, only partially evacuated, waited until floodwaters had risen significantly, or required considerable external 
assistance, were categorized as “other” and assigned a value of “0.”
This study investigated 15 predictor variables, including household sociodemographic characteristics (gender, age, education, 
presence of vulnerable members), hazard proximity, risk experience, threat appraisal, coping appraisal, trust in external support, and 
perceived evacuation barriers. In this study, "vulnerable members" are defined as children, the elderly, and individuals with physical or 
mental disabilities. "Hazard proximity" refers to the geographic placement of households in high-risk flood areas, including those 
situated near rivers or lakes, in low-lying regions, or in locations susceptible to isolation during flooding events. "Risk experience" 
encompasses both direct (personal) and indirect experiences of risk. Indirect experiences of risk refer to vicarious experiences, such as 
observing others facing emergencies due to not evacuating in time or being aware of such situations through media or social connections. In this study, variable X7 (Have seen others fall into emergencies due to not evacuating) explicitly captures this dimension. 
"Threat appraisal" and "coping appraisal" pertain to individuals’ evaluations of the severity of a threat and their perceived capacity to 
manage it, respectively. "Trust in external support" denotes the degree of confidence individuals have in their neighbors and local

4.2. Factors associated with household evacuation decisions
4.2.1. Model performance and fit
Table 4 summarizes the model’s predictive accuracy. The model accurately classified 203 of 236 households in the “other” category, achieving a correct classification rate of 86.0 %. Similarly, it correctly predicted 131 of 171 households in the “early complete 
evacuation” category, resulting in an accuracy rate of 76.6 %. Overall, the model achieved an average success rate of 82.1 %, 
demonstrating its effectiveness in predicting outcomes (see Table 5).
Given the binary nature of the outcome variable in this study, the model’s fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness 
of fit test [85]. While this test has its limitations, such as potential discrepancies in results when performed with a smaller number of 
covariate samples compared to the number of objects, it remains widely recognized and utilized by previous researchers [6,85,86]. The 
p-value obtained from the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness of fit test (0.182) suggests that the model fits the data adequately. In other 
words, there is a plausible overall relationship between the explanatory factors and households’ evacuation probability (χ2 = 11.36; df 
= 16; p = 0.182). Furthermore, Nagelkerke’s R square value (0.60) suggests that the explanatory variables in the model explain 60 % of 
the probability of household evacuation decisions.
4.2.2. Household evacuation decisions and associated factors

0.59, p = 0.21, and p = 0.19, respectively). These findings differ from those of previous studies [11,12,16,17,31]. Prior research has 
often suggested a higher likelihood of evacuation among female-headed households [10,16], households with young children [16,31], 
and households that accommodate members with disabilities or chronic ailments [17]. Educational attainment has emerged as a 
multifaceted determinant, as evidenced by previous studies. While some scholars [9,31,42,43] identified a positive correlation

These findings could be attributed to the specific characteristics of the study sample, the inherent complex effects of sociodemographic variables, and the socio-cultural context of rural Vietnam. Vietnamese rural communities are frequently characterized by 
robust social cohesion and well-established collective norms. While these features foster strong communal support, they can also 
introduce certain subjectivities that impact protective behaviors [8]. In such environments, the influence of sociodemographic factors, 
such as gender, age, education, and the presence of vulnerable household members, on protective behaviors may be diminished. For 
instance, community leaders or collective agreements may influence evacuation decisions more than individual household characteristics. Similarly, well-established support networks within these communities, including assistance from neighbors and extended 
families, might mitigate the practical challenges faced by households with vulnerable members, thereby reducing the observable 
impact of these sociodemographic factors. These socio-cultural dynamics may dilute the individual-level variability typically associated with gender, age, or education in influencing protective behaviors.
Moreover, the limited influence of sociodemographic characteristics may arise from their inherently intricate influence interactions. For instance, in this study, nearly 96 % of the study’s participants had only completed lower secondary education or less, 
with just 4 % attending upper secondary school. This uniform and relatively low education may be insufficient to make any difference 
in risk perception, access to information, or trust in official warnings, which often play an essential role in evacuation decisions, as 
noted by Morss et al. [44]. Furthermore, the potential multidimensional effects of sociodemographic variables could further obscure 
their overall impact. For example, better-educated individuals are generally better equipped to understand disaster risks and take 
protective actions [45,87]. However, they may also overestimate their ability to manage these risks, leading to a false sense of security 
and delayed evacuation [40,48]. Conversely, those with lower educational attainment may lack accurate information or distrust 
official warnings, resulting in hesitation or refusal to evacuate.

regression model results indicated that the association between hazard proximity and evacuation decisions was not statistically significant (p = 0.16).

concerns about potential asset loss. Conversely, a subset of households cognizant of their dwelling’s susceptibility steadily remains in 
place, bolstered by confidence in their preparedness measures. Many households mentioned concerns about property loss as a reason 
for not evacuating. Many prioritized protecting their assets over evacuation in high-risk areas due to fears of property loss during 
floods. Some chose to stay behind to safeguard valuable items like livestock and equipment, particularly those with limited financial

4.2.2.3. The influence of flood risk experience. Floods have become common among residents of low-lying areas, resulting in annual 
household damage of varying severity [6]. Numerous households have suffered substantial losses or have found themselves in 
emergencies because of the delayed implementation of protective measures, including early evacuation [7,89]. Hence, it is reasonable 
to assume that individuals who have witnessed or directly experienced significant flood risk are more likely to evacuate promptly. 
Contrary to our expectations, the analysis revealed no statistically significant association between households’ flood risk experience 
and evacuation decisions. In other words, the experiences of households that had either directly encountered or observed others facing 
emergencies due to delayed evacuation did not influence their decisions to evacuate (p = 0.53 and p = 0.35, respectively). In 1999 and 
2017, two catastrophic floods of comparable magnitudes instilled profound fear among many households due to the loss of property 
and even lives. During in-depth interviews, a village leader said, “Villagers tend to adopt a more cautious approach following such 
catastrophic events. However, these memories gradually diminish as floodwaters recede and damage lessens in the subsequent years. Hence, 
many households disregarded warnings and even mandatory evacuation orders from the village committee, believing it is premature to take such 
measures.” “They are unaware of the difficulties and dangers associated with evacuating once floodwaters have risen,” he explained. These 
findings align with Mondal’s [17] conclusion that individuals often refuse to evacuate until floodwaters inundate their homes. This is 
further supported by Adeola’s [60] study, which found no significant association between previous experiences of hazards of similar 
intensity and evacuation behavior. Our findings also align with the conclusions of Baker [57] and Huang et al. [55]. However, they 
contrast with Medina and Moraca’s [31] findings, which highlight the significant impact of past flood experiences on families’ 
evacuation decisions.
4.2.2.4. The influence of threat appraisal. Threat appraisal, often synonymous with risk perception, is widely acknowledged as a 
pivotal determinant of evacuation behavior. This study examines the appraisal of flood threats by floodplain residents by focusing on 
two key dimensions: flood intensity and unpredictability. Descriptive statistical analyses employing a Likert scale revealed a nuanced 
picture, with 57.25 % of respondents expressing moderate agreement that flood intensity is increasing, while the remaining 42.75 % 
perceived either no notable change or even a decline. Discrepancies in flood intensity assessments could stem from the impact of flood 
control measures, particularly the presence of large dams and reservoirs in Thua Thien Hue Province. While these infrastructural 
developments have generally curtailed the frequency and severity of annual floods, they can paradoxically precipitate adverse consequences, such as widespread deep flooding downstream owing to massive water releases from irrigation and hydropower reservoirs 
[8]. The inherent uncertainty surrounding flood intensity appraisals may engender a sense of complacency among households 
regarding preventive measures, notably evacuation. This complacency finds empirical support in the regression model outcomes, 
revealing a statistically insignificant correlation between flood intensity assessment and evacuation decisions (p = 0.11). Thus, 
elucidating the nuances of flood risk perception is imperative to craft targeted interventions and enhance disaster preparedness 
strategies. In addition to not evacuating when floodwaters were high, some households carried out other protective measures, such as 
reinforcing houses and preparing food, fresh water, and fuel. “During floods, they (referring to boat-based retailers) navigate their boats 
here to offer a diverse range of food items, including instant noodles, fresh vegetables, poultry, fish, and others. Food scarcity is not a concern 
during these times.” Boat-based retailers may provide essential food items, albeit exclusively, during low flood conditions. In contrast, 
this undertaking becomes nearly unfeasible as the water level increases. In such scenarios, households that fail to prepare adequately 
are susceptible to perilous situations.
In contrast, households that perceived floods as increasingly unpredictable tended to evacuate (p < 0.01). When controlling for 
other variables, each higher rating of flood unpredictability tripled the odds of household evacuation (OR = 3.13). Climate change and 
large-scale structural project implementation are the primary factors contributing to the unpredictable nature of floods in the area [6,
8]. Although the construction of major dams and reservoirs has contributed substantially to flood-control efforts, their efficacy is 
challenged during periods of intense rainfall and significant upstream water flow. Many households in high-risk areas evacuate 
pre-emptively to safeguard their families against unpredictable flood patterns. “The reliance on traditional knowledge for anticipating 
flood intensity has become increasingly ineffective since the appearance of hydroelectric and irrigation dams. The combination of heavy rainfall 
and dam release invariably leads to severe flooding. Evacuating early upon observing signs of rising floodwaters is imperative to ensure safety,” a 
resident from Phong Dien district shared. This finding, while also linked to risk perception, is particularly noteworthy as it sheds light 
on households’ understanding of a key characteristic of floods that has been largely overlooked in previous studies. This finding is 
consistent with the conclusions of several scholars [9,20,22,23,25]. Precautionary evacuation is primarily driven by factors related to 
threat assessment, such as the predicted severity and frequency of damage. Additionally, fear of natural disasters further motivated 
households to evacuate [23]. Perceptions of risk and threat significantly drive household decisions to evacuate early [20,22].

4.2.2.5. The influence of coping appraisal. Household coping appraisals significantly influenced the evacuation decisions. Those with 
high confidence in the structural integrity of their houses tended to disregard evacuations (p < 0.01). With each higher level of selfassessment of housing security, the odds of evacuation decreased by 29.9 % (OR = 0.70). Understandably, households may choose not 
to evacuate if they own houses with permanent structures and robust attics. However, these households represented only 20.15 % and 
11.30 % of the study population. Most remaining households owned semi-permanent structures characterized by temporary or semipermanent attics (refer to Table 2). Such housing structures typically have limited resilience and can withstand only minor floods. 
However, during major floods, fast-flowing water, or severe storms, the risk to the occupants escalates significantly. Despite this 
vulnerability, many expressed unwavering confidence in their houses’ ability to withstand floods and chose not to evacuate. For 
instance, a woman in Huong Tra district explained, “I inherited this house from previous generations. It was further reinforced with bricks, 
cement, and roofing tiles, and I believe it is strong enough to withstand floods. If floodwaters rise, all members will move to the attic.” However, 
when asked about the capacity of the attic to accommodate all five family members, this woman exhibited uncertainty and hesitation 
regarding security. Similarly, self-confidence in preparedness for emergency evacuation negatively affected evacuation decisions (p <
0.01). Each higher level of preparedness reduced households’ odds of evacuation by 55.2 % (OR = 0.45). Household preparedness for 
emergency evacuations primarily involves identifying evacuation routes, preparing means of transportation (such as small boats or 
homemade rafts), and acquiring supporting equipment (such as life jackets, flashlights, mobile phones, and necessary medications). 
The surveys revealed that only 23.59 % of households possessed small boats, and only 23.83 % had two or more lifejackets. Conversely, 
the prevalence of the use of other essential equipment exceeds 90 %. However, the preparation rates were below expectations. A 
member of the flood risk management board in Huong Tra district remarked, “Even with full equipment, self-evacuation in high water and 
strong currents poses significant hazards. Risks are particularly pronounced for children, the elderly, and even individuals with good health and 
swimming skills.” In other words, being prepared for emergencies does not necessarily guarantee the safety of households during 
evacuation. The negative regression coefficient suggests that households view emergency preparedness as either an alternative to 
evacuation or a means to delay evacuation until floodwaters become too deep.
This study reinforces the conclusions of Li et al. [90] emphasizing the importance of coping appraisals. However, the regression 
results indicate that the negative coefficients for coping appraisals offset the positive coefficients for threat appraisals in predicting 
evacuation decisions. This suggests that while coping appraisals are significant, their effect is balanced by threat appraisals in shaping 
households’ adjustment intentions under the pressure of natural hazards.
4.2.2.6. The influence of trust in external support. The results highlight the significant influence of trust in local governments and 
neighbors on households’ evacuation decisions during flood emergencies. Trust in local governments regarding flood evacuation, 
specifically, their capacity to mobilize equipment, vehicles, and personnel for timely and safe evacuations, had a negative effect (p <
0.01; OR = 0.43), indicating that higher levels of trust were associated with a decreased likelihood of evacuation. Similarly, trust in 
neighbors for flood evacuation also exhibited a negative effect (p = 0.01; OR = 0.60), suggesting that greater trust in neighbors 
correlated with a reduced probability of evacuation. This implies that households may opt to remain in their homes if they trust the 
assistance or protection provided by local government agencies or neighbors during flood events. A middle-aged man confidently 
shared, “Members of the flood and storm prevention committee used to regularly row boats to patrol this area, and I had their contact phone 
numbers. I believed they would promptly assist us if we encountered an emergency. Additionally, we had supportive neighbors who I was certain 
would not abandon us.” These findings underscore the ceaseless efforts of local governments and the key contribution of social connections to mitigating flood risks in rural Vietnam. However, excessive reliance on external support could potentially delay or 
compromise household evacuation motivation, leading to heightened risk. Owing to constrained financial resources, local emergency 
evacuation equipment remains relatively limited and largely dependent on human effort, basic tools, and apparatus. The effective 
rescue of numerous households within a short time frame during sudden floods poses a significant challenge. A village head affirmed, 
“Rescuing households during a major flood is exceedingly difficult. Our local force is relatively limited, and we must also prioritize the safety of 
our families.”

property during evacuation were frequently mentioned, revealing intriguing trends influencing households’ evacuation decisions.

evacuees are lodged with neighbors. However, statistical analysis revealed that these concerns did not significantly affect household 
evacuation decisions (p = 0.30). This suggests that while concerns about evacuation site conditions are widespread, they do not 
decisively influence evacuation behavior. Conversely, the fear of losing property during evacuation significantly affected evacuation

5. Conclusion and implications
Understanding the factors that hinder household evacuation decisions is crucial for developing effective interventions in the face of 
escalating flood risks. This study investigates the determinants influencing evacuation decisions among rural households in Thua Thien 
Hue Province, Central Vietnam, in response to the 2020 flood. The findings provide valuable insights into the complex interplay of 
factors that shape evacuation behaviors in this vulnerable region, with important implications for developing countries facing similar 
challenges in flood risk management.
The findings derived from the logistic regression model provided intriguing insights. Contrary to our initial expectations, the 
variables associated with sociodemographic characteristics, hazard proximity, and flood risk experience did not significantly influence 
evacuation decisions. Consequently, intervention strategies should be designed to be inclusive and comprehensive without discrimination based on these characteristics. While our findings challenge the established understanding of the role of sociodemographic 
characteristics in evacuation decisions, they also open avenues for future research. Understanding the root causes behind these nonsignificant associations will be critical in refining flood preparedness and response strategies, ensuring they are tailored to the actual 
drivers of behavior in the communities they aim to protect.
Our analysis, meanwhile, underscores the substantial impact of factors related to threat appraisal, coping appraisal, and trust in 
external support. A thorough evaluation of flood threats emerged as a pivotal factor positively associated with evacuating, while traits 
such as overconfidence in one’s ability to cope with floods and overestimating external forces’ support capacity negatively affected 
evacuation decisions. This can be considered “unrealistic optimism” in psychology as mentioned by Shepperd et al. [91]. Moreover, 
apprehensions regarding potential property loss further discouraged evacuation motivation.
Considering these findings, tailored interventions hold promise for enhancing public awareness and facilitating effective flood 
evacuation. First, local authorities must prioritize educational campaigns to enrich the public’s understanding of flood risk dynamics. 
Emphasis should be placed on the unpredictable nature of floods attributed to the escalating impacts of climate change and the 
contrasting effects of major dams and reservoirs in the region. Second, it is imperative to categorize and assess the resilience of 
common household structures such as temporary, semi-permanent, and permanent houses and attics against varying flood levels and 
patterns to enable individuals to gauge the associated risks accurately. Furthermore, highlighting the inherent challenges and perils of 
self-evacuation, particularly under severe flood conditions and limited household preparation, is crucial for fostering proactive 
responses.
Moreover, efforts should address the overestimation of support from external sources, such as that provided by local authorities, 
neighbors, or the community. Propaganda initiatives should emphasize the limited capacity of local authorities to facilitate large-scale 
emergency evacuations, thereby encouraging individuals to take personal responsibility for safety. Additionally, it is essential to 
elucidate the risks families face when they cannot access external support during emergencies, further reinforcing the urgency of early 
evacuation. Finally, local authorities should focus on enhancing the diversity and organization of living conditions in evacuation areas 
to ensure the convenience and comfort of evacuees. Implementing patrol plans during the flood season can also help to ensure security 
and curb opportunistic criminal activities, thereby enhancing the overall efficacy of evacuation efforts.
This study underscores the complexity of factors influencing evacuation decisions and calls for tailored interventions that take into 
account not only the socio-demographic factors but also psychological and social aspects. By implementing these recommendations, 
local authorities and stakeholders can enhance flood preparedness and response strategies, improving evacuation rates and safeguarding vulnerable communities in the face of escalating flood risks.
6. Study limitations and future directions
While this study sheds light on flood evacuation dynamics, acknowledging its principal limitations is crucial.
Firstly, the study utilized a binary logistic regression approach to classify evacuation behaviors into two categories for analytical 
clarity and simplicity. While this method allowed for focused exploration of early complete evacuation versus other behaviors, it did 
not fully capture the nuanced spectrum of evacuation practices, such as early, delayed, or no evacuation, or the variation in evacuation 
types (e.g., partial versus complete). Future research could explore the application of ordinal logistic regression to analyze these

gradations, offering a more detailed understanding of evacuation dynamics.
Secondly, the criterion of surveying only households that experienced flooding of 0.5 m or more may have limited the variability in 
evacuation behavior observed in the study. This narrowed range could potentially influence the strength of correlations between 
evacuation behavior and predictor variables, which should be considered when interpreting the findings. Future research should aim 
to include households with varying levels of flood exposure to capture a broader spectrum of evacuation behaviors and enhance the 
robustness of the results.
Thirdly, the study predominantly involved male household heads as respondents, reflecting the decision-making structure in rural 
Vietnamese households. While this approach aligns with cultural norms, it may have limited the representation of diverse household 
perspectives. Future studies should consider incorporating a more gender-balanced sample or investigating how gender roles influence 
evacuation decision-making processes.
Lastly, this study did not account for the interactions among household members in the decision-making process. Evacuation 
decisions are often collective, involving input from all household members rather than solely the individual head. The absence of a 
comprehensive exploration of these dynamics may have resulted in a limited perspective on the complexities of evacuation behavior.
By addressing these limitations, future studies can contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of flood evacuation dynamics, inform more effective disaster risk management strategies, and significantly advance scholarly understanding in this field.
