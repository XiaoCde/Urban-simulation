ABSTRACT
The increasing frequency of extreme flood events in recent years has posed substantial challenges to the emergency evacuation 
capacity of urban areas. Existing evacuation plans are largely based on rational assumptions, overlooking the complex decisionmaking mechanisms through which residents simultaneously weigh multiple attributes in real-world scenarios. This study focuses on residents of Beijing and employs a stated preference experiment together with a conditional logit model to examine 
their preferences regarding evacuation distance, bottleneck risk, traffic control, and organizational mode. It further explores 
the heterogeneous effects of gender, age, education level, and length of residence. The results reveal that, in the face of flooding, residents' evacuation decisions are driven by survival probability rather than by minimizing travel time. The combination 
of intelligent signals and government-planned routes is the only attribute consistently preferred across all groups. However, 
significant divergences exist among different population segments in terms of bottleneck aversion, organizational mode preference, and sensitivity to distance. Scenario analyses based on the model results indicate that the deployment of intelligent 
signals and government-provided guidance yield the highest universal benefits. Dynamic management of high-risk bottlenecks 
and community-based adaptations tailored to elderly groups can effectively enhance evacuation performance. These findings 
challenge the conventional view of homogeneous evacuation decision-making among residents and highlight marked differences in risk avoidance choices across population groups, providing empirical support for shifting emergency management from 
persuasion-based strategies to differentiated resilience approaches.
1 | Introduction
In recent years, climate change has led to more frequent and 
intense flood disasters worldwide, posing severe threats to 
lives and property (Merz et al. 2021; Kundzewicz et al. 2019; 
Tellman et  al.  2021). Floods are typically abrupt, highly destructive, and wide-ranging, making effective evacuation 
critical for reducing casualties (Cea and Costabile  2022). 
Structural flood defenses have inherent limitations, which is 
why nonstructural measures, particularly orderly emergency 
evacuation, have become the last line of defense against loss of 
life and property (Abioye et al. 2020). However, current evacuation plans often assume perfect rationality and treat residents 
as homogeneous utility maximizers, overlooking the bounded 
rationality, context dependence, and socio-demographic heterogeneity that shape real world decisions. Post-disaster studies have shown that evacuation behavior is influenced by a 
combination of past experience, social networks, trust in

information, and traffic conditions, resulting in systematic 
deviations from planned expectations (Brachman et al. 2020; 
Kako et  al.  2022). Such deviations can cause evacuation delays, inefficient route choices, traffic congestion, and even secondary hazards.
Evacuation preferences reflect the tendencies residents display when facing a threat, based on their perceptions, experiences, and environmental conditions (Misal et  al.  2023). 
These include decisions about timing, destination, and transport mode (Wu et al. 2024; Minami et al. 2024). Such decisions 
are shaped by a complex interplay of factors. At the individual level, demographic characteristics, including age, gender, 
education, health status, household composition and urgency 
level (Haghani et al. 2020), affect both the decision to evacuate and the choice of transport mode (David and Ramos 2022). 
Risk perception serves as a key mediator between warning receipt and action; even when official warnings are issued, low 
perceived risk may lead people to stay (Buylova et al. 2020). 
Environmental factors, such as the physical attributes of the 
flood and the built environment, define the feasible routes and 
modes available (de Albuquerque et al. 2024; Yu et al. 2020). 
Social and informational influences, such as neighborhood 
support, peer effects, and the credibility of warnings, also 
moderate individual responses (Du et al. 2017). Finally, institutional aspects, including shelter capacity, route planning, 
and drill frequency, shape overall preparedness and public 
confidence in the evacuation system (Zhai et al. 2014). Early 
studies relied heavily on retrospective analyses of historical 
disaster data (Huang et al. 2022), but they typically examined 
only one or a few factors, such as warning time or water depth 
(Dias et al. 2021; Dong et al. 2022), thus failing to capture the 
joint trade-offs that residents make among multiple attributes, 
such as distance, transport mode, and organizational arrangements. Consequently, systematic quantification of marginal 
trade-offs and structural preference heterogeneity across social groups remains scarce. Notably, media coverage of vehicle entrapment incidents in low-lying underpasses during the 
2012 “7·21” torrential rain in Beijing has shaped a collective 
risk memory among residents regarding the fatal hazards of 
such bottlenecks.
Stated preference (SP) methods, widely used in transportation 
(Yagi and Shiraishi 2017; Luo et al. 2016; Capodici et al. 2025), environmental valuation (Fang et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2015), and more 
recently in disaster research (Dias et  al.  2021), are well suited 
for eliciting preferences for low-probability hazards. Existing 
SP studies have explored policy interventions, warning responses, peer effects, and destination/mode choices (Mozumder 
and Vásquez  2018; Borowski and Stathopoulos  2023; Zhang 
et al. 2025). However, most of these studies focus on the binary 
decision of whether to evacuate, while optimization models often 
overlook behavioral factors and simply prioritize the shortest 
path (Shekhar et al. 2012; Aldahlawi et al. 2024). Four attributes 
(evacuation distance, bottleneck type, traffic control, and organization mode) were selected based on a literature review and pilot 
interviews. Evacuation distance and route safety are critical factors in flood evacuation (Mizumura and Nakamura 2025). Traffic 
control and organization mode were included because they are 
policy-relevant and have been identified as key institutional factors in recent evacuation planning reviews (Li et al. 2025).
To address these gaps, we employ an SP experiment and a conditional logit model to examine how residents trade off multiple evacuation attributes, specifically evacuation distance, 
bottleneck type, signal control strategy, and organizational 
mode. We shift the core question from whether to evacuate 
to how to evacuate, thereby enabling policies that offer differentiated options aligned with residents' preferences. We 
estimate marginal utilities for each attribute level and test for 
preference heterogeneity by gender, age, education, and length 
of residence using interaction terms. The main contributions 
are threefold. Theoretically, it shifts the focus from whether 
to evacuate to route choice behavior and quantifies marginal 
trade-offs among multiple attributes; methodologically, it 
enables ex-ante evaluation of intervention strategies not yet 
implemented; practically, it reveals systematic preference heterogeneity across groups, supporting a shift from uniform to 
differentiated evacuation strategies.
The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section  2 describes the study area. Section  3 presents the questionnaire 
design, data collection, and model specification. Section 4 reports the empirical results and discussion. Section 5 concludes 
the study, outlines its limitations, and suggests directions for 
future research.
2 | Study Area
As a megacity with over 21 million permanent residents, Beijing 
has a vast built-up area and a highly interdependent urban 
system reliant on complex underground corridors and rivercrossing bridges, making it particularly vulnerable to extreme 
weather induced evacuation pressures (Li et al. 2023).
Historically, Beijing has suffered severe impacts from extreme 
rainstorms and floods. In the July 21, 2012, torrential rain, 79 
people lost their lives (Beijing Flood Control and Drought Relief 
Headquarters 2012), and numerous waterlogging points formed 
across the city. In July 2023, another extreme rainfall event 
caused 33 deaths and affected nearly 1.29 million residents 
(Beijing Municipal People's Government 2023). In July 2024, 
continuous heavy rainfall again caused significant casualties 
and property damage, exposing major risks from bottlenecks 
such as flooded underpasses and interrupted river-crossing 
bridges.
The study focuses on the Tonghui River corridor (Figure  1) in 
Chaoyang District, Beijing. The Tonghui River is a primary drainage artery for central Beijing, serving as a major conduit for stormwater runoff from the city's core functional areas. This corridor 
has been repeatedly hit by severe flood events, including the July 
2016 extreme rainfall that caused the river to overflow onto pedestrian walkways in the CBD section, and more recent extreme flood 
events in July 2023 and July 2024 that exposed major risks from 
bottlenecks such as submerged underpasses and disrupted rivercrossing bridges. These recurring flood disasters establish the 
Tonghui River corridor as a high-risk zone with persistent exposure to flood hazards. Given the likelihood of future extreme flood 
events under climate change, understanding residents' evacuation 
preferences and route choice mechanisms in this area is essential 
for proactive, prevention-oriented emergency planning.

3 | Methodology
Using an SP survey and discrete choice models, this study 
quantifies the factors driving evacuation decisions. Attributes 
were derived from literature and pilot surveys, chosen for 
measurability and feasibility. They capture residents' core 
concerns and inform strategies to enhance flood evacuation 
in high-density cities.
3.1 | SP Questionnaire Design
Because major flood events are low frequency, actual evacuation 
behavior data are difficult to obtain. This study therefore adopts a 
stated preference method, presenting hypothetical flood scenarios 
to residents to elicit their evacuation route preferences. Compared 
with revealed preference (RP) data, the SP method allows systematic control of route attributes and enables the evaluation of facility improvements that have not yet been implemented in the study 
area (Xu and Nair 2026; Lovreglio et al. 2016). The questionnaire 
consists of two parts. The first part collects respondents' socioeconomic background information. The second part presents hypothetical evacuation choice sets generated by an orthogonal design.
In the socioeconomic background section, the questionnaire 
systematically collects key variables such as gender, age, education level, occupation, and length of residence in the current 
city. These variables are used not only for descriptive sample statistics but also as control variables in subsequent heterogeneity 
analyses, helping to reveal behavioral differences in evacuation 
decisions across social groups.
For the hypothetical evacuation scenarios, based on a literature review and preliminary investigation, we identified four 
key attributes and their levels: type of traffic bottleneck point, 
evacuation distance, traffic control method, and evacuation 
organization mode. The attribute selection was informed by a

If a full factorial design were used for all attributes and their 
levels, it would produce an excessively large number of hypothetical situations, placing a heavy cognitive burden on respondents and generating many redundant or invalid scenario

level appears in a balanced manner, we ultimately selected eight 
representative choice situations (Table 2). Each respondent completed eight choice tasks within 10min. A pre-test with 5 respondents confirmed that this length allowed consistent response

(Alternative A and Alternative B) with differentiated attribute 
levels, plus a Neither option (Alternative C). All situations are 
embedded in a consistent flood disaster scenario description, 
which mentions potential risks such as water and power outages 
and road disruptions, and assumes that the government has already issued an evacuation order. This enhances respondents' 
sense of immersion and the authenticity of their answers.
The survey presented a unified disaster scenario description before all choice situations: flooding is very likely to cause water 
and power outages, road disruptions, and inundation of low 
floor and old houses; emergency rescue may not arrive in time; 
and the government has clearly issued an evacuation order. 
Respondents were asked to choose their preferred option from 
the alternatives in each situation against this background.
3.2 | Data Collection
The survey was conducted from June to September 2025 
in the Beijing area, which faces relatively high flood risk. 
Questionnaires were distributed both online and offline. A total 
of 335 questionnaires were collected. After checking for completeness, logical consistency, and invalid responses, 307 valid 
questionnaires were retained, giving an effective response rate 
of 91.64%. Each respondent completed eight choice situations, 
each with two evacuation alternatives (A and B) plus a “neither” 
option. In all 2456 choice tasks, no respondent selected the “neither” option. This option was therefore excluded from the model 
estimation, yielding 4912 valid choice observations, which meets 
the large sample requirement for discrete choice models.

follows: 58.3% aged 18–30, 28.7% aged 31–50, 10.1% aged 51–
65, and 2.9% aged over 65. Regarding educational background, 
respondents with a high school education or below constituted 
14.66% of the sample, those with an associate or bachelor's

Table 3 shows that the coefficient for underpass congestion is 
−1.48, the largest negative impact among the three bottleneck 
types, followed by river bridges. Residents thus show the strongest aversion to underpasses, which are prone to both congestion and flooding. Bridge aversion stems from structural 
vulnerability and unreliable passage. Arterial road intersection congestion, the reference category, also reduces utility but 
is perceived as more visible and controllable, hence less dangerous. This ranking confirms that residents prioritize safety 
over efficiency.
The evacuation distance coefficient is significantly positive 
(β=0.055, p=0.026). This result is counterintuitive but reflects 
the joint influence of distance and route safety attributes embedded in the experimental design. In the choice scenarios, 
shorter routes were predominantly configured with high-risk 
bottlenecks such as underground passages, while longer routes 
were typically associated with arterial roads, which offer better visibility, drainage capacity, and more stable traffic flow 
during flood events. Consequently, the positive coefficient captures residents' willingness to accept additional travel distance 
in exchange for improved route safety and predictability. This 
interpretation is consistent with existing evidence showing that 
evacuees in flood and hurricane contexts frequently opt for familiar or safer routes, even when these routes are longer than 
officially recommended alternatives (Sadri et  al.  2014). More 
broadly, risk-averse evacuees have been shown to exhibit systematically higher sensitivity to route reliability and certainty. 
In flood evacuation settings, residents prioritize low-risk scenarios and are willing to trade time or distance for safety gains (Jin 
et al. 2025). A more nuanced reading, therefore, is that residents 
are not preferring longer distances per se, but are accepting longer distances as a necessary cost to avoid unsafe routes and to 
secure reliable guidance. This interpretation aligns with the 
broader behavioral pattern identified in this study, that survival 
probability, rather than distance minimization, drives evacuation decisions under flood stress.
For traffic control, the combination of intelligent signals and 
government planned routes has the most positive coefficient 
(0.632), reflecting a strong preference for orderly, guided evacuation. The “No signal control + self-navigation” scenario has a 
negative coefficient (−0.471), indicating that reliance on decentralized personal technology under extreme stress raises uncertainty (Xu and Nair 2026). Fixed signals with self-chosen routes 
(the reference) lie in between.
The coefficients for evacuation organization modes are not statistically significant, suggesting that residents care more about 
safe evacuation than about how it is organized. The three modes 
(private car, community organised, walking) are perceived as 
largely equivalent. Overall, residents follow a safety first logic. 
Traffic bottlenecks are the primary driver, with underpass congestion (β=−1.48) sharply reducing route attractiveness. They 
are willing to accept longer distances to avoid risks and obtain 
reliable guidance, as shown by the positive preference for intelligent signals (β=0.63). Traditional shortest path evacuation 
models may therefore be inappropriate for real world disasters 
(Aldahlawi et al. 2024).
4.2 | Heterogeneity Analysis of Evacuation 
Preferences Across Population Groups
According to Table 4, which presents utility coefficients across 
genders, lengths of residence, ages, and education levels, several 
patterns emerge. First, the combination of intelligent signals 
and government-planned routes is the only attribute consistently preferred by almost all groups, with positive and mostly 
significant coefficients across subgroups, most of which are 
statistically significant (Jin et  al.  2025). Second, underpasses 
and river crossing bridges are generally disliked, with aversion 
concentrated among males, residents who have lived in the city 
for more than 10 years, the elderly aged over 65, and those with 
a bachelor's degree. Among these, older adults show the largest negative coefficient for underpasses. Third, a preference for 
longer evacuation distances appears only among males, young 
adults (18–30), and those with a master's degree or higher; other 
groups do not exhibit this tendency. Fourth, preferences for organization modes are divergent. The elderly aged over 65 significantly prefer community organized evacuation, whereas males, 
young people, and those with associate or bachelor's degrees 
dislike private car evacuation with family, meaning they would 
rather walk than drive. Finally, females, those with lower education levels, people aged 51–65, and residents who have lived 
locally for less than 10 years show coefficients that are mostly 
nonsignificant, indicating no statistically significant difference 
in preferences from the reference group. Based on these findings, three phenomena warrant further discussion.
The divergence in preferences for organization modes reflects 
differing attitudes toward autonomy versus reliance (Haghani 
et al. 2020). The elderly aged over 65 significantly prefer community organized evacuation. In contrast, males, young people 
aged 18–30, and those with associate or bachelor's degrees show 
clear aversion to private car evacuation with family, preferring 
walking over driving. Older adults, possibly due to reduced 
physical capacity or lack of confidence in navigating complex 
road conditions, are more willing to delegate decision-making 
to community arrangements, thereby reducing psychological 
burden. Younger, more educated, and male groups, in contrast, 
dislike private-car evacuation with family, preferring walking 
over driving. This may stem from their daily commuting experience with congestion, leading them to perceive walking as more 
flexible and reliable in emergencies. These opposing preferences 
imply that evacuation planning should offer menu-like options 
rather than a one-size-fits-all prescription.
The preference for longer distances among males, young adults, 
and those with a master's degree or higher shows a significant 
preference for longer distances themselves, but rather an interpretation of longer distances as a proxy for better material supplies and higher shelter quality. Attribute correlation analysis 
indicates that longer routes tend to involve arterial roads and 
overpasses, whereas shorter distances often come with underpasses or river crossing bridges bottlenecks that most groups 
dislike. The observed preference for longer distances thus likely 
incorporates both a pursuit of better shelter resources and an

The effect of length of residence on evacuation preferences 
exhibits a nonlinear U shaped pattern. Both new residents 
who have lived in the city for less than 1 year and long-term 
residents who have lived there for more than 10 years show 
multiple significant preferences or aversions, whereas the intermediate group (residence of 1–10 years) has mostly nonsignificant coefficients, indicating no substantial difference from 
the reference group. New residents are highly sensitive to 
signal control because they are unfamiliar with the road network and rely on external guidance (Brachman et al. 2020). 
Long-term residents, based on long-standing experience, have 
formed strong negative memories of bottlenecks such as underpasses. The intermediate group is in a transitional phase 
where the sense of unfamiliarity has faded but extreme negative experiences have not yet accumulated. This U-shaped 
pattern suggests that information dissemination should be 
stratified, with detailed signal guidance for new residents and 
specific detour information for long-term residents around 
bottleneck sections.
4.3 | Scenario-Based Evaluation of Evacuation 
Route Improvements
To translate the estimated preference parameters into a concrete decision support tool and to assess the potential effects 
of different improvement strategies, this study used GISbased mapping tools to construct three representative evacuation routes based on the actual geographic setting of the 
study area, near the Tonghui River in Beijing. Residents must 
traverse waterlogging prone areas and pass through various 
traffic bottlenecks to reach safe shelters, thereby capturing the 
core constraints faced by evacuation routes in reality. Based 
on the road network structure and waterlogging risk distribution in this area, three representative evacuation routes were 
defined. These routes correspond to different combinations of 
bottleneck type, traffic control method, and evacuation distance, reflecting the key trade-offs residents make regarding 
the physical attributes of routes in the model. It should be 
noted that, between baseline and improvement scenarios, the 
transport mode chosen by residents is assumed to remain unchanged, so that the direct effect of improvements in the physical attributes of the route on utility can be isolated. Model 
parameters are all taken from the logit regression results in 
Table 3. By comparing the utility changes of the three routes 
under different intervention scenarios, the potential impact of 
each improvement strategy on residents' route preferences can 
be quantitatively assessed (Figure 2).
In the baseline scenario, three evacuation routes were evaluated. Route 1 is the shortest (2.8 km) but crosses a high 
risk underpass with fixed time signals; its estimated utility 
is −1.328 (0.055×2.8–1.482). Route 2 is a medium distance 
option (5 km) crossing a river bridge with limited capacity and no signals, yielding an estimated utility of −0.958 
(0.055×5 – 0.762 – 0.471). Route 3 is the longest (10 km) on arterial roads with fixed time signals, with an estimated utility of 0.55 (0.055×10). In relative terms, the utilities rank as 
Route 3 > Route 2 > Route 1. Despite being about 72% shorter 
than Route 3, Route 1's strong negative utility (−1.328) shows 
that the underpass risk outweighs any distance benefit in 
residents' stated preferences. Route 2 also remains negative, 
indicating that bridge capacity limitations and accident risks 
deter residents. Route 3, though longest, yields positive utility, 
meaning the perceived safety of arterial roads is valued more 
highly than extra travel time in the choice context. These baseline differences quantify the implicit costs of route attributes, 
confirming that risk avoidance can override conventional distance minimisation logic.

Three representative improvement scenarios were then designed, focusing on the highest risk bottlenecks and exploring enhanced safety through dynamic control and temporary 
protective measures under disaster conditions. For Route 1, 
instead of permanent reconstruction, the underpass is temporarily closed after a warning, with a parallel ground detour 
activated and water level monitoring and gates installed. 
Removing the underpass risk makes it comparable to an arterial road; the estimated utility improves to 0.165 (0.055×3), 
representing a utility gain of 1.493. This shows that warning 
responsive dynamic control can turn a previously unacceptable route into an attractive one with modest investment in 
terms of relative preference.
For Routes 2 and 3, improvements focus on soft operational 
measures, as physical capacity changes are costly. Signal control 
is upgraded from “No signal control + self-navigation” to “intelligent signals plus government planned routes”. Route 2's utility 
improves to 0.145 (0.055×5 –0.76+0.63), turning from negative 
to positive; Route 3 improves to 1.18 (0.055×10+0.63). The intelligent signal system delivers such gains because it reduces 
uncertainty and provides clear guidance. In normal conditions, 
navigation apps assist individual decisions; but during flood 
emergencies, the absence of signal lights means the traffic system lacks a unified rule framework, leading to chaotic intersections and heightened anxiety, even with navigation. Residents' 
dislike of the “no signals plus navigation” mode thus reflects fear 
of traffic order collapse. Intelligent signals, by contrast, provide 
a dynamically adjusted rule framework that not only compensates for physical deficiencies but also enhances psychological 
trust and route acceptance in the stated preference context.
Comparing baseline and improvements reveals two key policy 
insights about relative preferences. The underpass risk reduces 
utility by 1.48 relative to an arterial overpass, equivalent to a 
distance penalty of about 27km (1.48/0.055) in terms of the 
estimated trade-off between bottleneck risk and distance. Yet 
underpasses are indispensable in daily life, so permanent reconstruction is impractical. Instead, the strategy should shift 
from permanent engineering to temporary closure and dynamic 
control during disasters, supplemented by low cost protective 
measures (e.g., water level monitoring, automatic gates) at critical nodes, preserving normal function while avoiding extreme 
risks. Second, soft measures like intelligent signal upgrades 
yield a utility gain of 0.63, equivalent to creating an 11.5km distance advantage (0.63/0.055) within the estimated preference 
structure. This makes them a highly cost effective strategy that 
can substantially boost evacuation route attractiveness without 
large scale civil engineering.
5 | Conclusion
This study used a stated preference experiment and a conditional logit model to examine flood evacuation choices in a 
high-density urban area, providing micro-level evidence for improving urban flood resilience. The results show that residents 
are primarily driven by risk aversion: they actively avoid highrisk bottlenecks such as underpasses, and the disutility of these 
features far outweighs any benefit from shorter routes. This 
implies that resilience enhancement should focus on identifying and managing critical vulnerable nodes, rather than merely 
minimizing evacuation distances.
Residents generally prefer intelligent dynamic signals combined with government-planned routes, as this combination 
provides clear guidance and reduces uncertainty. Preferences 
vary significantly across groups. Older adults strongly favor 
community-organized evacuation and avoid underpasses, 
underscoring the need for community-level organizational resilience. Males, younger respondents, and more educated individuals tend to prefer longer distances and dislike private car 
use, indicating a demand for flexible travel modes. New and 
long-term residents are more sensitive to signals and bottlenecks than those with intermediate tenure, highlighting the 
importance of informational resilience. In contrast, women, 
less educated respondents, and people aged 51–65 show no 
significant preference for the attributes examined, suggesting 
that their core concerns are not captured by our design and 
require further inquiry.
These findings have direct implications for both preparedness 
and response. In the prevention phase, census data can help 
locate vulnerable groups such as the elderly, highly educated, 
and newcomers. Intelligent signal systems should be deployed 
at critical points, while dynamic control plans rather than 
permanent reconstruction should be developed for high-risk 
bottlenecks. For communities with many elderly residents, 
pre-arranged pick-up points and vehicles for community-led 
evacuation can strengthen social network resilience. In areas 
with many newcomers, regular information campaigns on 
evacuation routes and signal guidance can compensate for 
unfamiliarity with the local road network. During response, 
real-time location data can enable tailored messaging: older 
adults receive pick-up point locations and access instructions, 
while younger groups get congestion updates and estimated 
walking times. A feedback loop to adjust signal timing and 
route recommendations in real time can further improve 
adaptability. This layered, information-driven approach may 
enhance system resilience without requiring massive infrastructure investment.
The choice scenarios were set in a high-risk context with a 
mandatory evacuation order, and the preferences were elicited 
through hypothetical scenarios rather than observed behavior. 
The findings therefore primarily reflect residents' route preferences after the decision to evacuate has been made, rather 
than the initial decision of whether to evacuate. Caution is also 
needed when generalizing the conclusions to voluntary evacuation contexts or other geographical areas. Future work could 
further examine the external validity of these findings through 
post-disaster behavioral data or comparative studies in other 
settings.
