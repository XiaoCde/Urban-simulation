ABSTRACT
Coastal areas of the United States are vulnerable to substantial loss of lives and property damage
from repeatedly occurring hurricanes and evacuation is the usual recourse to prevent loss of life
when high storm surge threatens. The fundamental question in evacuation modeling is to explore
the complex evacuation decision-making process leading to an individual’s decision to evacuate or
not during a hurricane threat. Recent studies suggest that the social network characteristics of
individuals could potentially determine overall evacuation patterns. This study explores the joint
evacuation decisions of individuals in personal networks by using ego-centric social network data
obtained from Hurricane Sandy and by considering the nested structure of the ego-centric network
data, i.e. close contacts (alters) as nested within an individual (ego). In this regard, the study
develops a multinomial multilevel model of joint evacuation decisions at the dyadic (ego-alter tie)
level utilizing a Hierarchical Generalized Linear Modeling (HGLM) approach. Model estimation
results suggest factors that define a social tie (contact frequency, discussion topic and geographic
proximity) significantly influence the evacuation decisions between individuals and their social
partners. In addition, individuals’ (both ego and alter) own socio-demographics such as age,
marital status, previous evacuation experience, evacuation order, household’s type, size, location
and proximity to a water body also affect the decision to evacuate. These findings are useful to
help emergency managers implement efficient evacuation strategies and to facilitate planning by
policymakers by determining fractions of people evacuating or not for a major hurricane within
the context of their social networks.
1. Introduction and motivation
Hurricane Sandy (October 2012) caused about 254 deaths in the US, Caribbean, and Bahamas with an estimated economic loss of
$65 billion (USD) (Ehrhart et al., 2012). Seventy-two fatalities, including 41 drownings from storm surge, resulted from Sandy’s wind
and flood impact (Gladwin et al., 2013). Timely evacuation could have averted such storm surge deaths that were higher than all but two
prior hurricanes (Katrina and Camille) in the past 60 years (National Hurricane Center, 2015). Most of Katrina’s direct storm surge
drowning deaths occurred in Mississippi included 200 fatalities that is nearly 0.00054% of the population. While Sandy’s wind and
waves heights were less than Katrina’s category 4 hurricane winds and 30 foot waves in Mississippi, the death toll did not remotely
approach the same proportion of the population affected by Katrina in Mississippi. Nonetheless, this death toll and what it could have
been in a category four or five hurricane mandates further study of public evacuation decision-making for Sandy. Most of the New Jersey

would be safe staying in their homes. However, research done in the framework of Lindell’s protective action decision model (Lindell

A likely mechanism for the effect of social networks in evacuation decision-making is social capital operating in social networks and
social ties both traditionally defined, and as augmented by social media. According to one definition of social capital, individuals
exchange resources such as information, ideas, support and others through relationships with other people, thus considering social
capital as the extent to which an individual involves himself in different informal networks as well as formal civic organizations
(Putnam, 1995, 2001). This conceptualizes social capital as the many different ways members of a given community interact with each
other as an indication of that community’s associational life and civic health, including participating in recreational activities, talking to
neighbors, joining political parties or environmental organizations and so on. Similarly, Coleman defined social capital by its function
that includes different entities with two common elements: an aspect of social structures and certain facilitating actions of actors within
the structure (Coleman, 1988). The prospect of social capital in building more resilient communities has been explored recently
(Aldrich, 2012) explaining how such communities may become proactive in absorbing sudden shocks specially during post-disasters.
That study identified social capital as the single most contributing factor during post-disaster recovery and suggested individuals and
neighborhoods with weaker ties faced more difficulties as a result of inaccurate interactions with organized and mobilized groups.
A more network-oriented perspective relevant to social capital is to consider the personal networks of individuals’ i.e. ego-centric
social networks (Borgatti and Halgin, 2011; Burt, 1984, 1985, 2000; Halgin and Borgatti, 2012). The General Social Survey (GSS),
first conducted in 1972, measured core discussion networks among Americans (Burt, 1985; Marsden, 1987) and several studies have
used these GSS discussion network measures (Brashears, 2011; Wellman, 1979). The particular questionnaire item involved from GSS
has been widely used by researchers because of its parsimony, general nature, applicability to various contexts and ability to capture
ego’s core discussion networks without focus on specific network content (Bailey and Marsden, 1999; Burt, 1984; Carrasco et al., 2013;
Kowald et al., 2010; Sadri et al., 2015a; van den Berg et al., 2009). In contrast, Granovettor gave more stress to the cohesive power of
weak ties and suggested that occasional and informal ties have the ability to link different groups of individuals that could provide access
to newer resources or support (Granovetter, 1973). As a result, conceptual debates on social capital cannot be resolved in an empirical
vacuum (Grootaert et al., 2003).
Frequently occurring hurricanes in the coastal areas of United States require that different stakeholders, particularly emergency
planners and officials, understand the overall evacuation process thoroughly and identify key determinants of evacuation behavior in
reducing the amount of risk associated with vulnerable communities. The key requirement is to explore evacuation decision-making
behavior (i.e. who evacuates and who does not) and the causal factors influencing this behavior. Within the overall amount of
research conducted on the issue of evacuation, few studies do this. The central idea of this paper is to analyze joint evacuation decisionmaking behavior of individuals in a personal network setting using ego-centric social network data obtained from Hurricane Sandy and
considering the nested structure of the data i.e. friends or close contacts (alters) being nested within an individual (ego). In this regard,
this study develops a multinomial multilevel model of joint evacuation decision outcome at the dyadic (ego-alter tie) level using
Hierarchical Generalized Linear Modeling (HGLM) approach to investigate the effects of relational or tie attributes in addition to
individuals’ own socio-demographic characteristics.
Social media served as an essential source of information sharing during Hurricane Sandy. Many residents lacking access to traditional sources of information (television for example), received information on smart-phones using social media (Kaufman et al., 2012).
Social media communications in areas without power (Midtown Manhattan was the most common tweeting location) continued
throughout and after the storm as the continuous distribution of tweets spread throughout the city (Floating Sheep, 2012). While social
media pertains to virtual or online social connections, they can reflect real social ties that can influence individual evacuation decisions.
Hasan and Ukkusuri found that warning information propagates faster within networks with greater inter-community connections using
a simulation based approach (Hasan and Ukkusuri, 2011). Another earlier study presented a contagion model to explain how people first
receive emergency warning information and then spread the information to others (Rogers and Sorensen, 1991). These network effects
were stressed in the policy proposal of Gladwin et al. on necessary actions required by appropriate agencies and organizations to support
social science research on the major issues in the hurricane forecast and warning system (Gladwin et al., 2007). Social network characteristics and warning information sources significantly influenced individual-level evacuation decision making during Hurricane
Sandy (Sadri et al., 2016).
This study is organized as follows: the next section summarizes existing literature on hurricane evacuation decision-making; Section
3 describes the data and explains the personal network research design approach; Section 4 presents the methodology used to develop
the model; Section 5 elaborates the model estimation results; and Section 6 concludes the study with a discussion of key findings.
2. Background and related work
Evacuation is a typical form of travel during extreme events and it is the usual recourse to prevent loss of life if high storm surge
occurs. However, evacuees often show synchronized behavior in terms of when they depart and what route they take. (Wolshon, 2002).
Several studies show important factors affecting evacuation decisions such as hurricane trajectory and warning system, characteristics of
evacuees and their households, etc. to explain the overall evacuation process (Baker, 1991; Carnegie and Deka, 2010; Gladwin et al.,
2001, 2007; Lindell and Prater, 2007b; Urbina and Wolshon, 2003). Dash and Gladwin reviewed research showing that households who

are expected to evacuate do not evacuate and vice versa, as well as many contradictory effects on evacuation of socio-demographic
characteristics such as age, gender, race, ethnicity, income, children or elderly persons in the household, disability, previous experience, and geographic-location (Dash and Gladwin, 2007). Studies of evacuation in one major hurricane (e.g Peacock et al. for Hurricane
Andrew) typically find as significant variables such as being in an evacuation zone, household size, and presence of elderly people and
children (Peacock et al., 1997). On the other hand, comparing storms, Whitehead et al. found storm intensity to be the principal
determinant of the decision to evacuate (Whitehead et al., 2000). However, Lindell et al. showed stronger correlations between
evacuation decisions and geographic characteristics, information utilization, evacuation order, official warning and so on (Lindell et al.,
2005).
Some studies developed statistical models presenting a diverse set of behavioral aspects related to evacuation decisions (Fu and
Wilmot, 2004, 2006; Solis et al., 2009). Mileti and Sorenson introduced the notion of risk perception based on personal assessment of
own risk in addition to that of family and household (Mileti and Sorensen, 1987). Hasan et al. developed a random parameters binary
logit model of hurricane evacuation decision where the random parameters account for the heterogeneous responses of households
towards a major hurricane (Hasan et al., 2010). That study found heterogeneities in terms of location, evacuation notice, work constraint, and the number of children. Apart from modeling the decision to evacuate, other studies have explored follow up questions such
as (a) when to evacuate? (Fu and Wilmot, 2004, 2006; Hasan et al., 2013; Lindell and Prater, 2007a), (b) mobilization time, i.e., time
elapsed from decision to evacuate to actual evacuation (Dixit et al., 2008, 2011; Sadri et al., 2013a), (c) where to evacuate? (Cheng et al.,
2008; Mesa-Arango et al., 2012), (d) which mode of travel? (Deka and Carnegie, 2010; Sadri et al., 2014) (e) which route to take?
(Murray-Tuite et al., 2012; Sadri et al., 2013b, 2015b) among others.
Social networks characteristics of individuals, however, can also affect the underlying evacuation patterns. These social ties pertain
to informal networks based on neighbors, co-workers, family members, and friends (Gladwin et al., 2007). Drabek and Boggs found that
individuals with small social networks are less likely to evacuate (Drabek and Boggs, 1968). Clifford found that social ties affect the
disaster warning dissemination and adaptation (Clifford, 1956). Studies also show that African American households with stronger
kinship ties are more likely to spread out warning information and evacuate (Perry, 1979; Quarantelli, 1985). Riad et al. identified
’social influence’ as a social psychological process influencing the complex phenomenon of why people sometimes chose not to evacuate
during a threat (Riad et al., 1999). However, that study concluded individuals are finally responsible for their decisions despite social
influences. Using a simulation based approach, Hasan and Ukkusuri suggested that an individual’s risk perception can be socially
influenced as evacuation decisions spread from individual to individual through social ties (Hasan and Ukkusuri, 2011). That study
considered different community mixing patterns in explaining information cascade and evacuation decision behavior.
While previous studies examined why people decide to evacuate based on individual level characteristics, this study focuses on
evacuation decision-making behavior of people based on their social network structure. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the
first study to collect ego-centric social network data of individuals based on their response to Hurricane Sandy and examine the level of
influence of social ties on their joint evacuation decision-making process. Estimation findings suggest that the characteristics of social
ties, such as frequency of contact and geographic proximity significantly affect how people evacuate during a disaster in addition to
some individual level characteristics. This study’s findings will help emergency planners and policy-makers develop better evacuation
plans and strategies by determining fractions of people evacuating or not during a major hurricane and thus predicting evacuation
demand more accurately.
3. Data collection
In this study, data was collected by surveying residents from high storm surge risk areas of New York and New Jersey all of whom
participated in the survey voluntarily (see Fig. 1). The survey was funded by the National Science Foundation. The sample frame for the
survey was based on US census block group areas in coastal New Jersey and New York likely to be at risk of life-threatening storm surge
in a major hurricane; the block groups within five km of the coastline or inland tidal water body and having a mean elevation of less than
eight meters. In addition, the study includes block groups, not meeting this condition, located in New York City evacuation Zone A.
Approximately 350 block groups were randomly selected from the list of qualifying block groups and a sample of telephone numbers
randomly drawn from those block group areas. Forty-five percent of the numbers were listed land line numbers and could be matched to
block groups through address geo-coding. Fifty-five percent were of cell phones requiring additional pre-interview screening and

4. Social networks, social influence, and evacuation decisions
Social networks include individuals as nodes and their relationships as links or edges. Such relationships help to construct a network
of individuals (Jackson, 2010) and guide how the process of social influence takes place between peers. Studies have suggested that
sources of influence may include personal influence between the direct contacts, influence between social groups or social circles, and
influence from information sources such as media (Kadushin, 2012). As such, there exists a unique interdependence between the
network structure, the influence mechanism, and the source itself (Maness, 2015). In recent times, transportation researchers have been
extensively looking at social networks and social influence mechanisms to study several behavioral dimensions specific to travel and trip
making such as mode choice (Dugundji and Walker, 2005), joint trip frequency for regular activity travel (Sadri et al., 2015a), carpooling (Rezende et al., 2016), participation in social activities (Carrasco and Miller, 2007), cycling (Sherwin et al., 2014), drunk
driving (Kim and Kim, 2012), among others.
Different forms of travel may possess varying social influence processes and it requires great care in selecting an appropriate social
influence mechanism given the social networks under consideration and the type of influence sources (Maness, 2015). Evacuation is a
specific form of travel during extreme events that helps to prevent loss of life when high storm surge occurs. During an evacuation, the
key behavioral concern for emergency managers is to understand whether people would prefer to evacuate or not. Studies that presented
the need to integrate social networks, social influence mechanisms and other social factors into evacuation modeling is discussed in
Section 2. In this regard, Sadri et al. (2016) explored how social networks and information sources served the purpose of disseminating
warning information during Hurricane Sandy. The study looked at the effects of social network size, homophily (similarity), heterogeneity (diversity), evacuation timing of the peers, average length of relationship on evacuation decision-making by using a mixed-logit
approach and ego-centric network data obtained from the high storm surge areas.
However, whether individuals, during major hurricanes, make similar decisions or equally perceive the risk due to peer pressure, is
yet to be uncovered. This study is intended to unfold such joint evacuation decision-making behavior given individuals are parts
identical social networks. In particular, this study explores the similarities in evacuation decisions between social partners as a function
of the nature of social relationship, geographic proximity, and socio-demographic factors such as age, gender, marital status among
others. This has been done by looking at the personal network data obtained from New York and New Jersey residents and by considering the nested structure of the ego-centric network data i.e. friends or close contacts (alters) being nested within an individual
(ego).
In order to model joint evacuation decisions at the dyadic (ego-alter) level, this study develops a multinomial multilevel model of
evacuation decision outcomes using Hierarchical Generalized Linear Modeling (HGLM) approach. The categorical dependent variable
was based on respondents (ego) self-report of their own evacuation decision as well as the people (alters) they closely interacted during
Hurricane Sandy. Data shows that the number of social ties varied from a minimum of 1 to a maximum of 6 and shows a nesting structure
i.e. alter level information being nested within each ego. As a result, the evacuation decision outcome at the dyadic level has four
categories: (a) both ego and alter evacuate (b) ego evacuates, alter does not evacuate (c) alter evacuates, ego does not and (d) both ego
and alter do not evacuate. Fig. 2 shows the distribution of the discrete evacuation decision outcomes at the dyadic (ego-alter) level.
Table 1 presents descriptive statistics of explanatory variables used in the final model specification with mean, standard deviation,
minimum and maximum. After accounting for missing data for some explanatory variables in the final model specification, the ego-level
and alter-level observations are 412 and 1109 respectively.
5. Modeling framework
In this study, a Hierarchical Generalized Linear Modeling (HGLM) approach for a multinomial model is used to assess the joint
evacuation decision outcome during a major hurricane at the dyadic (ego-alter) level. The multinomial multilevel modeling approach is
appropriate for clustering data with a multinomial outcome considering the following two specific reasons: (1) multinomial analysis is
more efficient than a series of binary logit analyses since the former approach involves a large number of comparisons (Scott Long,
1997). With four categories such as the type of evacuation outcome at the dyadic level in this study, six comparisons should be made
whereas the multinomial model needs only three comparisons. (2) multilevel analysis is advantageous over traditional ordinary least
squares (OLS) regression analysis since it accounts for the dependence among alters within egos network and provides efficient
parameter estimates in unbalanced, nested structure of data (Raudenbush and Bryk, 2002).
However, the problem of evacuation decision-making involves four possible outcomes at the dyadic (ego-alter) level: (1) both ego
and alter evacuate, (2) ego evacuates, alter does not evacuate, (3) alter evacuates, ego does not and (4) both ego and alter do not
evacuate. Here, the variance in outcome variable can be analyzed at two hierarchical levels (Raudenbush and Bryk, 2002; Snijders et al.,

In this study, the case that both ego and alter evacuate (case 1) has been treated as the reference category. The constant terms

regions to evacuate. This reality points to the importance of determining the causal factors that hinder the necessary evacuation.
However, these factors and their effects can broadly be classified into three distinct groups:
6.1. Effects of ego-alter relational (dyadic) attributes
Relational attributes between ego and alter have differential effects on each type of evacuation outcome as shown in Table 2. These
attributes include frequency of contact (indicating how often ego and alter contact each other), discussion topic (indicating the topic that
occurs in their discussion in general) and geographic proximity (indicating how close or how far they live from each other). Frequency of
contact was defined for cases where either ego or alter evacuates (case 2 and case 3) and found to be statistically significant. The results
indicate that people contacting each other on a daily basis are more likely to evacuate compared to people with less frequent contact.
Since contact frequency is defined for case 2 and case 3, there is no difference between case 1 and case 4 on account of frequency. More
specifically, this implies that people tend to make similar evacuation decisions if they communicate more frequently. However, the
likelihood that people make similar decisions (yes-yes, no-no decisions) declines as contact frequency decreases. This important finding
indicates the evidence that frequently contacted persons are more concerned about the storm threat that allows them perceive and react
to the situation in a similar manner. Discussion topic was defined for case 2 and case 4 suggesting that individuals discussing family
matters (as compared to any other topic) had higher odds of not evacuating (85% and 62% respectively). This is relevant since most
family discussion occurs among family members and there could be significant household matters constraining their evacuation
(confirming findings of Gladwin et al. (2001)). However, the results indicate mixed effects since yes-yes (case 1) and no-yes (case 3)
decisions are not different from each other. This is also indicative of family relations in social networks being responsible for withinhousehold negotiation and improvisation on the decision to evacuate. The variable geographic proximity was found to be statistically significant (p<0.01) and this was defined for all the three cases (cases 2, 3 and 4). Model estimation results show higher log-odds

6.2. Effects of alter characteristics
Apart from the significant dyadic attributes, individual attributes of ego and alter play significant roles in affecting the evacuation
outcome at the dyadic level. For example, alter age shows higher mean odds (1.03 for case 2 and case 4; 1.02 for case 3) indicating the
likelihood that both ego and alter evacuate reduces with the increased age of alters. On the other hand, alter income results in lower
mean odds as defined for case 2 and case 4 (0.47 and 0.63 respectively) indicating that if alter belongs to the high income group they are
less likely to evacuate. Alter gender was found to be highly significant for case 4 with higher mean odds (47%) which indicates that
having male alters in the network makes it more likely that both ego and alter do not evacuate. These findings suggest how the
characteristics of the people in social networks significantly impact evacuation decision-making.
6.3. Effects of ego characteristics
In the final model specification, characteristics related to ego include location, age, marital status, household size, proximity to large
water body, evacuation experience and evacuation order from officials influence evacuation decision. For example, if ego is from New
York it results in higher odds (75%) as defined for case 4 indicating that individuals and their social partners from this area are less likely
to evacuate. This important finding captures the overall unwillingness of the people from New York to evacuate. If ego is an elderly
person (65 years or over), ego is more likely to evacuate as suggested by lower odds (52%) defined for case 3. This important finding
indicates the higher perception of risk by senior citizens. On the other hand, the odds for case 3 and case 4 were 3.33 and 1.78 times
greater for egos who are married, respectively showing relative unwillingness of married individuals to evacuate. Similar effects were
observed for the number of years ego lived in the present county or city. Ego being the only person in the household shows higher odds
both for case 2 and case 3 suggesting the likelihood of either ego or alter to evacuate. Proximity to large water body or ocean (twentyfive miles or less) also suggests that either the ego or alter do not evacuate if ego living in close proximity (within 25 miles of an ocean or
large water body). This should be given due consideration since individuals staying at close proximity needs to evacuate for safety.
However, previous evacuation experience and receiving evacuation order from emergency officials result in lower mean odds for
each of the cases compared to the base outcome (both ego and alter evacuate). This result provides a crucial insight on the fact that
individuals having previous experience and receiving an evacuation order can persuade their social partners to evacuate and show
synchronous evacuation behavior. These findings from Hurricane Sandy validates the findings from Hurricane Ivan (Hasan et al., 2010)
and have important implications. The key take-away from this result is that the more people who receive mandatory evacuation orders
from officials the more likely they will evacuate which can expedite the evacuation process. However, individuals having evacuation
experience prior to Sandy were more likely to evacuate compared to those without prior experience. This result contrasts with Hurricane
Ivan findings and depicts the importance of educating people in coastal regions who do not have previous experience about the necessity
of evacuation. The odds of case 3 (alter evacuates, ego does not) relative to the base case were 0.50 times lower for ego living in a singlefamily home.
7. Conclusions and key findings
In this study, the role of social ties on evacuation behavior is explored by using ego-centric social network data obtained from
Hurricane Sandy and by considering the hierarchical structure of the data i.e. friends or close contacts (alters) being nested within an
individual (ego). With the help of Hierarchical Generalized Linear Modeling (HGLM) technique, this study develops a multinomial
multilevel model of joint evacuation-decision outcome based at the dyadic (ego-alter tie) level. Estimation findings suggest that joint
evacuation decisions of closely connected individuals are significantly influenced by several underlying factors. The main contribution
of this study in the evacuation modeling literature is to report important dyadic or relational attributes being representative of the
characteristics of social ties and dominating the joint evacuation decision outcome. Some of the key insights based on the final model
specification are listed below:
 All else being equal, it is more likely that both the ego and alter are less likely to evacuate.
 Household size, location and proximity to large water body influence evacuation decision-making. People, staying in a single-person
household, are less likely to evacuate. Individuals staying close to large water body are more likely to evacuate.
 Previous evacuation experience and evacuation order from emergency officials significantly impact evacuation decision-making.
Individuals who experienced a major hurricane previously and receive evacuation order have a heightened level of risk perception and thus evacuate.
 Both ego and the alters (social partners) are more likely to evacuate if they contact each other daily and live in close geographic
proximity (less than five miles or fifteen minutes’ drive).

 If the topic of discussion between ego and alter is family matters in general, it has mixed effects on evacuation decisions at the dyadic
level. This is indicative of family relations in social networks being responsible for within-household negotiation and improvisation
on the decision to evacuate.
 Age, gender, marital status and income of alters significantly impact evacuation outcome at the dyadic level. Age has heterogeneous
effects on evacuation. For example, elderly egos are more likely to evacuate, whereas elderly alters are less likely to evacuate
This study provides meaningful implications for a clear understanding of evacuation decision-making behavior that can help
forecasters and emergency officials create targeted and efficient evacuation plans. Evacuation orders from officials make it more likely
that individuals will evacuate as do frequently contacted social ties. Evacuation rates would be higher and faster for closely connected
communities. Thus, strong social ties (more contact frequency) can serve the purpose of effective dissemination of warning information.
On the other hand, if friends live far apart, chances of evacuation are significantly reduced. Based on this, group-level evacuation
policies can be introduced for people residing in mandatory evacuation zones and increase the rate of overall evacuation. More consideration should be given to special population groups such as elderly people and senior citizens by providing them desired facilities
required for evacuation. Increased public education is needed for people residing in the evacuation zones who never experienced a
major hurricane.
This study incorporates a disaggregate approach to model evacuation decision-making i.e. how an individual and close contact both
behave during a major hurricane threat. However, a more aggregated approach would be to model evacuation decision-making at the
individual level by including network measures like density, homophily, heterogeneity and so on to explore network effects. Also, the
study is based on ego-centric social network data from the east coast i.e. New York and New Jersey during Hurricane Sandy. Future
studies could collect data from similar types of disasters to validate the results obtained in this study and check if the model is applicable
and transferable to other coastal areas in the United States. The survey did not obtain the actual content of the communication between
ego and alter with respect to the hurricane or evacuation, however, such crisis communication patterns can also be important predictors
of joint evacuation decisions.
