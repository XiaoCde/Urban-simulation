DEPARTMENT OF ENERGY AND ENVIRONMENT 
 
NOTICE OF FINAL RULEMAKING 
 
Flood Hazard Rules 
 
The Director of the Department of Energy and Environment (DOEE), pursuant to the authority set 
forth in the District Department of the Environment Establishment Act of 2005, effective February 
15, 2006 (D.C. Law 16-51; D.C. Official Code § 8-151.01 et seq.); the Water Pollution Control 
Act of 1984, effective March 16, 1985 (D.C. Law 5-188; D.C. Official Code § 8-103.01 et seq.); 
the Soil Erosion and Sedimentation Control Act of 1977, effective September 28, 1977 (D.C. Law 
2-23; 24 DCR 000792); the District of Columbia Applications Insurance Implementation Act, 
effective May 26, 1976 (D.C. Law 1-64; D.C. Official Code § 6-501 et seq.); and Mayor’s Order 
2006-61, dated June 14, 2006, hereby gives notice of his adoption of the following amendments 
to Chapter 31 (Flood Hazard Rules) of Title 20 (Environment) of the District of Columbia 
Municipal Regulations (DCMR).  
 
This rulemaking is intended to protect the public health, safety, and welfare of District residents 
and businesses in the event of significant flooding and reduces public and private losses due to 
flood conditions in flood hazard areas. The rule addresses deficiencies identified by the Federal 
Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) during a March 2016 audit of the District’s 
implementation of the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP). The rule facilitates the District 
remaining eligible to participate in NFIP and to receive federal financial relief in the event of a 
flood-related disaster. This rule also reflects revisions to the District’s building code and zoning 
regulations and includes new provisions to adapt to changing flood hazard conditions specified in 
Climate Ready DC (2016). The rule requires safer building practices in flood hazard areas to 
reduce injury, loss of life, and property damage from flooding, and will also help prepare the 
District’s residents and structures for projected higher sea levels and more frequent and intense 
storms due to climate change. 
 
A Notice of Proposed Rulemaking was published in the District of Columbia Register on July 19, 
2024, at 71 DCR 8695. A forty-five (45) day comment period ended September 2, 2024. DOEE 
received three (3) sets of comments which it carefully evaluated and considered; for the reasons 
described below, DOEE did not make any changes to the proposed rules in response to the 
comments received. DOEE summarizes the comments and its responses below.  
 
 
Rhode Island Avenue Metro Underpass Flood Hazard Area 
A commenter asked that DOEE provide a statutory basis for treating the Rhode Island Avenue 
area as a Special Flood Hazard Area.  
 
DOEE Response: The Flood Resilience Amendment Act of 2022, DC Law 24-151, 
effective August 16, 2022, D. C. Official Code § 6-501(b) gives the Mayor the authority to 
issue rules designating flood hazard areas in the District and requiring flood insurance as 
a condition of occupancy for the life of a new or substantially improved building in a flood 
hazard area.

The same commenter asked that DOEE clarify whether the recent construction of DC Water’s 
Northeast Boundary Tunnel (NEBT) project has been taken into consideration when delineating 
the Rhode Island Avenue area as a special flood hazard area.  
 
DOEE Response: On August 14, 2023, approximately 2 inches of rain fell in less than an 
hour at this location, flooding the underpass, stranding cars, and filling a pet daycare 
business with 6 feet of water. The flood killed 10 dogs and forced the business to close 
temporarily. At the time of the flood, DC Water’s NEBT was nearing completion, but not 
yet in operation. The Tunnel began operating on September 15, 2023, approximately a 
month after the flood.   
  
As described in the proposed rulemaking, DOEE defines the Rhode Island Avenue 
regulated area as the land that was below the elevation (94.5 ft NAVD88) of the area that 
was flooded on August 14, 2023, which is before the NEBT began to operate. However, 
DOEE has determined that the regulated area described in these regulations is still 
warranted for two reasons: 1) that elevation is a conservative estimate that was already 
allowing more risk than other similar flood hazard areas, and 2) while the operation of the 
NEBT may alleviate flooding in this area, it was not designed to solve flooding. 
 
Elaborating on the reasons above, the standard in American floodplain management is to 
regulate areas that are in the 100-year or 500-year floodplains. Statistically, the 100-year 
floodplain means the area that has a 1% annual chance of being flooded in a given year and 
the 500-year floodplain means the area that has a 0.2% annual chance of being flooded in 
a given year. According to best estimates from the National Weather Service and from DC 
Water, the amount of rain that fell over the Rhode Island Avenue area on August 14th, 
2023, was 2 inches over a 45-minute period,1 which equates to somewhere between a 10- 
and 25-year storm. Statistically, a 10-year storm has a 10% chance of occurring in a given 
year and a 25-year storm has a 4% chance of occurring in a given year. For comparison, a 
100-year storm with a 45 minute duration would produce between 2.3 and 3.2 inches of 
rain.2 If DOEE were to regulate an area that had a 1% annual chance of being flooded 
around Rhode Island Avenue, the regulated area would be larger, to account for more rain 
(between 2.3 and 3.2 inches compared to 2 inches) and the resulting higher flood waters. 
Prior to the operation of the NEBT, DOEE’s regulated area for Rhode Island Avenue 
already was allowing for more risk than other FEMA defined floodplains throughout the 
city. DOEE’s regulated area is even more conservative when considering that climate 
change is expected to increase the intensity and frequency of severe storms in the District. 
A 25-year storm with a 1 hour duration (a high estimate of what occurred on August 14, 
2023) results in 2.5 inches of rain according to existing design standards, but is likely to 
result in 2.7 inches of rain in the next 50 years according to Climate Ready DC’s 2024 
Projections Update.

Further, as noted, the NEBT was not designed to solve flooding. If the NEBT was able to 
completely drain 2 inches of rain over a 45-minute period, it would be reason to reconsider 
the boundary, as the currently defined elevation of 94.5 feet NAVD88 would now only be 
flooded with much heavier rains. However, DC Water’s own guidance is that the NEBT 
“will not eliminate flooding for a larger storm, but will mitigate the impact.”3 The main 
goal of the NEBT is to improve water quality by reducing combined sewer overflows into 
the Anacostia River. It also has an ancillary benefit of reducing interior flooding at some 
locations near the tunnel. At the time of the flood, DC Water estimates that the stormwater 
system in the Rhode Island Avenue area could drain all the water from a 5-year, 24-hour 
storm, which is approximately 4 inches of rain in a 24-hour period.4 With the NEBT now 
operational, DC Water estimates that the stormwater system capacity in this area is now 
capable of draining a 15-year 24-hour storm,5 which is approximately 5.5 inches of rain in 
a 24-hour period. However, that capacity is described over a 24-hour period. The 
stormwater system in this area cannot handle all 4 or 5.5 inches of rain within 45 minutes 
(the duration of the August 14, 2023 storm) because the sewer inlet openings are only so 
large. Effectively, the ability of the stormwater system and the NEBT to limit flooding 
depends on how quickly stormwater can be drained off the street, and less about total 
stormwater storage capacity of the NEBT.  While the NEBT may help reduce some 
flooding in the area, if there are especially intense storms (such as those that took place on 
June 25, 2012, June 26, 2012, August 10, 2022, and August 14, 2023), the flooded area is 
likely to be close to the same size as it would be before the NEBT was operational, though 
the waters will likely recede more quickly.  
 
The same commenter also asked DOEE to clarify the requirements for existing buildings within 
the Rhode Island Avenue area and whether the regulations would require modification to existing 
structures.  
 
DOEE Response: Finalization of the regulations will not render existing structures out of 
compliance.  The regulations apply when new work is being done.  If the work proposed 
is a substantial improvement of an existing structure (project cost is greater than fifty 
percent of the value of the existing structure), or if the work is new construction, then the 
project will be subject to the full requirements of the regulations. The regulations require 
that the entire building must be 1) elevated or 2) protected by a building-wide 
floodproofing system that can be deployed quickly.  If the proposed work is less than 
substantial improvement (such as tenant fit outs and any repair, replacement, or new 
installation of any mechanical, electrical, or plumbing systems such as furnace, electrical 
outlets, or water lines) and is below the Design Flood Elevation (DFE), the Flood Hazard 
Rules and construction code require that the utility systems and other materials be flood 
resistant or raised above the DFE.  A completely new, building-wide floodproofing system,

Technical Approach on Freeboard 
A commenter asked what approach DOEE took in assigning a freeboard value for Design Flood 
Elevations (DFEs).  
 
DOEE Response: DOEE uses a freeboard value above the Base Flood Elevation to add 
additional protection that accounts for future conditions with rising sea levels and increased 
frequency and intensity of storm events. The proposed standard is based on case studies in 
cities such as Norfolk, Virginia; Fort Collins, Colorado; Austin, Texas; and Seattle, 
Washington. The specific elevation levels align with the existing version of the DC 
Building Code. A FEMA study of historic flooding in Boulder County, Colorado found 
that adopting a 100-year flood elevation plus 2 feet of freeboard standard (like what these 
regulations propose) could have reduced losses due to flooding in that city by 38%.6 As of 
2015, a nationwide total of 192 local governments and 4 states had adopted the 2 feet of 
freeboard standard.7 For critical facilities, these regulations require that buildings meet a 
DFE of whichever is higher; the 500-year elevation plus 2 feet, or the 100-year elevation 
plus 4 feet. DOEE’s intention was to ensure that critical facilities, which house vulnerable 
occupants or critical functions, should be protected to a standard at least 2 feet higher than 
the standard for non-critical facilities. 
   
Definition of Mixed-Use 
A commenter stated that a developer might be able to evade constraints on residential building 
subgrade parking by adding minor retail elements to residential structures that could then qualify 
the building as mixed-use instead of residential.  
 
DOEE Response: Minor retail elements will not be enough to classify a residential building 
as a mixed-use building instead. While these regulations provide a generic explanation of 
what a mixed-use building is, the District’s Construction Code defines how to classify 
buildings, or portions thereof as mixed-use or residential. The District’s Construction Code 
has adopted the International Building Code (2015) and the International Residential Code 
(2015) that detail the construction and classification of those buildings and states that 
incidental uses shall be included in the building occupancies within which they are located.   
 
The Director of DOEE adopted these rules as final on June 10, 2025, and the rules shall take effect 
on the date of publication of this notice in the District of Columbia Register.

CHAPTER 31     FLOOD HAZARD RULES 
 
3100  
PURPOSE  
 
3100.1 
This chapter and the flood-resistant construction provisions of the District of 
Columbia Construction Codes (together referred to as the Floodplain Management 
Regulations) promote public health, safety, and general welfare, and minimize 
public and private losses due to flood conditions in flood hazard areas. 
 
3100.2 
This chapter establishes minimum requirements that are designed to: 
 
(a) 
Mitigate the impact of flooding on properties in the District; 
 
(b) 
Prevent or regulate the construction of flood barriers that will unnaturally 
divert floodwaters or may increase flood hazards to other lands;  
 
(c) 
Restrict or prohibit certain uses, activities, and development inside areas 
subject to flooding;  
 
(d) 
Require uses, activities, and developments that occur in flood-prone areas 
to be protected to prevent flood damage; and 
 
(e) 
Regulate uses, activities, and development that, acting alone or in 
combination with other existing or future uses, activities, and development, 
will cause unacceptable increases in flood heights, velocities, and 
frequencies that may affect other properties. 
 
3100.3 
The degree of flood protection required by this chapter is based on scientific and 
engineering considerations and is intended to minimize the degree and extent of 
flood damage. The regulations and the construction codes are designed to keep 
properties reasonably safe from flooding. Enforcement of these regulations and the 
construction codes does not imply that land outside the flood hazard areas, or that 
uses permitted inside the flood hazard areas, will be free from flooding or flood 
damage. Larger floods can and will occur. Flood heights may be increased by man-
made or natural causes.

3106  
PERMIT APPLICATION PROCEDURES AND REQUIREMENTS 
 
3106.1 
In any flood hazard area, a permit issued by DOB shall be required for all new 
construction, substantial improvement, or development. A permit for any 
development on a development site located wholly or partially inside a flood hazard 
area will not be issued by DOB unless DOEE has determined that the proposed 
work conforms to the Floodplain Management Regulations. 
  
3106.2 
Permit applicants shall provide the information required by the Floodplain 
Management Regulations in sufficient detail and clarity to determine whether the 
following conditions are met: 
 
(a)  
The proposal is designed and constructed with methods, practices and 
materials that minimize flood damage and that are in accordance with the 
Floodplain Management Regulations and the American Society of Civil 
Engineers Standard 24-14 (ASCE 24), Flood Resistant Design and 
Construction; 
 
(b)  
All utilities and facilities, such as sewer, gas, electrical, and water systems, 
are located and constructed to minimize or eliminate flood damage;  
 
(c)  
The proposed development provides adequate drainage to reduce exposure 
to flood hazards;  
 
(d)  
Any wet-floodproofed enclosure at a new construction or substantial 
improvement of residential or non-residential structures is: 
 
(1) 
Used only for parking of vehicles, building access, or storage; 
 
(2) 
Designed to automatically equalize hydrostatic flood forces on 
exterior walls by allowing for the entry and exit of floodwaters and 
is either: 
 
(A) 
Certified by a District registered professional engineer or 
architect; or  
 
(B) 
Meets the construction requirements listed in ASCE 24 
related to flood resistance; and

Above grade on at least one side.

3107 
DESIGN FLOOD ELEVATIONS (DFEs) 
 
3107.1 
DFEs must be identified for all development on a development site located wholly 
or partially inside a flood hazard area. Applicants may voluntarily design their 
project to be elevated or protected at a height above the DFEs required in this 
section. 
 
3107.2 
If an application includes a site plan, an applicant shall provide with the site plan 
cross sections that show the ground elevation and DFE at the site.  
 
3107.3 
Unless otherwise designated, the DFE shall be the higher of: 
 
(a) 
The base flood elevation plus a freeboard equal to two feet (2 ft.), or  
 
(b)  
The high flood elevation, in accordance with the most current FIS and FIRM 
published by FEMA and adopted by the District.  


===== [page 17] =====
 
17 
 
3107.4 
For critical facilities permitted wholly or partially inside flood hazard areas, the 
DFE shall be equivalent to that identified in § 3107.3 plus two feet (2 ft.).  
3107.5 
For any new construction or substantial improvement located on a development site 
wholly or partially inside a tidal shoreline buffer area, the DFE shall be equivalent 
to that identified in § 3107.3 plus three and four-tenths feet (3.4 ft.). 
3107.6 
If the DFE is not specified on the FIS or FIRM, the applicant shall, subject to DOEE 
approval, either: 
(a) 
Determine the DFE by obtaining, reviewing, and reasonably utilizing data 
available from a federal or state agency; or 
 
(b) 
Determine the DFE by using hydrologic and hydraulic engineering 
techniques, and as follows:  
 
(1) 
The determination shall be performed and sealed by a District-
registered professional engineer.  
 
(2) 
Studies, analyses, and computations shall be submitted in sufficient 
detail to allow review and approval by DOEE.  
 
(3) 
The accuracy of data submitted for the determination shall be the 
responsibility of the applicant. 
 
3107.7 
For the Zone X (shaded) flood hazard area mapped by Letter of Map Revision 
(LOMR) 15-03-2388P, where no high flood elevation is specified on the FIS and 
FIRM, the DFE shall be the elevation of the base flood elevation plus freeboard of 
two feet (2 ft.) if no high flood elevation data is available from a federal, state, or 
other source approved by DOEE. Requests for removal from this flood hazard area 
based on elevation shall be accompanied and justified by an H&H analysis that is 
prepared and sealed by a registered design professional and submitted to DOEE for 
review.  
 
3107.8 
For the Rhode Island Avenue Metro underpass area specified in § 3101.2(d), the 
DFE shall be the NAVD88 elevation of ninety-six and one-half feet (96.5 ft.).

3108.1 
Utilities and Finishes. The applicant shall apply the following minimum standards 
to all proposed development on a development site located wholly or partially

appliances; plumbing fixtures; duct systems; and other service equipment 


===== [page 18] =====
 
18 
 
shall be performed so that the systems are elevated above the DFE or 
designed to prevent water from entering or accumulating inside the

(b) 
New and replacement sanitary sewage systems and gas and oil supply

(1) 
To minimize or eliminate infiltration of flood waters into the

(2)

(f)

the following criteria: 
 
(a) 
There shall be at least two (2) flood openings on different sides of each

(b) 
For the substantial improvement of residential structures wholly or partially

(d)

(e)

(f) 
Interior partitions and load-bearing walls shall have openings to allow water

(a)

(b)  
Failure to grant the variance would result in exceptional hardship by 
rendering the lot undevelopable due to conditions such as unique physical 
and topographical conditions of the property; 
 
(c)  
Granting a variance will not result in increased flood heights, additional 
threats to public safety, or extraordinary public expense, or conflict with 
other existing laws; or regulations; or  
 
(d)  
The variance is the minimum necessary to afford relief considering the flood 
hazard.

Any repair, reconstruction, rehabilitation, alteration, addition or other improvement

Tidal shoreline buffer – land projected to be inundated by water by year 2080 due to changes in 
sea level as determined by best available science. The flood hazard area is the land inside the Tidal 
Shoreline Buffer layer depicted in the DOEE Flood Risk Portal (http://dcfloodrisk.org/), along 
with any subsequent revisions and amendments.  
 
Watercourse – A river, creek, stream, channel, or other topographic feature in, on, through, or 
over which water flows at least periodically. 
 
Wet floodproofing – floodproofing method that relies on the use of flood-damage-resistant 
materials and construction techniques to minimize flood damages to areas below the DFE of a 
structure intentionally allowed to flood.
