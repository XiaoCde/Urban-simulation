Abstract 
Floods are among the most destructive natural disasters, yet understanding of pedestrian 
movement during floods remains limited. This study presents the first experimental investigation of 
unidirectional pedestrian flow under no-water and three water depth conditions, revealing how water 
depth influences movement dynamics. Findings indicate that the impact of water depth on 
pedestrian speed and density is density-dependent. At low densities, water resistance limits 
pedestrian movement speed, whereas at high densities, pedestrian interactions become the primary 
constraint. Flow capacity decreases progressively with increasing water depth, by 9.3%, 14.6%, and 
26.8%, respectively. The nearest neighbor space is jointly influenced by both density and water 
depth. Increasing density compresses longitudinal spacing more than lateral spacing, while water 
condition further reduces forward personal space. This spatial transition is associated with gait 
adaptation: when step length exceeds shoulder width, the nearest neighbors concentrate on both 


===== [page 3] =====
2 
 
sides of the movement axis. The pedestrian speed–headway relationship exhibits a consistent two-
regime pattern—constrained and free—across all water depths. However, the step frequency–
headway relationship shows two regimes only when water depth is below the knee (<0.60 m). Below 
knee level, pedestrian speed regulation involves coordinated adjustments of both step length and 
frequency. Above knee level, speed changes are primarily achieved through step length adjustments. 
Free step length decreases abruptly under water conditions (~37.7%), while free step frequency 
declines monotonically with water depth (1.03–0.84 Hz). These results provide essential empirical 
data for flood evacuation modeling and support the development of effective flood risk mitigation 
strategies.

1. Introduction 
1.1 Background 
In the face of ongoing global warming, the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events 
have been steadily increasing, resulting in greater damage. Of these events, floods pose a widespread 
and serious risk to urban environments and societies across the globe. Data from the Global Disaster 
Data Platform [1] reveals that from 2003 to 2022, floods constituted 49.08% of all recorded natural 
disasters, impacted 44.69% of the total population affected by natural disasters, and were 
responsible for 21.54% of overall direct economic losses. With the rapid pace of urban development, 
the impact of flooding on cities has become increasingly pronounced [2]. Particularly during the 
rainy season, intense precipitation and the resulting floods can severely disrupt the operations of 
densely populated urban areas, often causing substantial economic damage and loss of life. 
During flood evacuations and rescue operations, both affected individuals and emergency 
responders often encounter situations requiring movement through floodwaters [3]. The interaction 
between pedestrians and flowing water introduces considerable uncertainty and risk into the 
evacuation process, potentially resulting in severe injuries or fatalities. Therefore, a thorough 
understanding of pedestrian movement characteristics in flood conditions is essential, highlighting 
the urgent need for research on pedestrian dynamics during flood evacuations. 
 
1.2 Literature Review 


===== [page 5] =====
4 
 
Assessing flood risk is a crucial component of monitoring and anticipating hazardous events, 
typically based on three core elements: flood hazard, the vulnerability of the built environment, and 
the potential consequences for affected populations [4]. Common approaches to flood risk 
assessment during disaster scenarios involve utilizing urban geographic information [5] to develop 
predictive flood models [6] and applying flood stability models to evaluate pedestrian risk levels 
and generate hazard maps for human exposure [7]. In addition to water depth, hydrodynamic factors 
such as flow variability, local perturbations, and the spatial configuration of buildings and districts 
significantly influence flood propagation and hazard intensity. Recent numerical and laboratory 
investigations have highlighted the role of urban morphology in shaping surface flooding patterns 
and flow exchanges between streets and residential blocks [8-13]. These findings underscore the 
importance of considering pure hydrodynamic behavior when assessing flood impacts on evacuation 
processes. Despite these advances, existing risk models often rely primarily on water depth as the 
dominant hazard indicator and typically consider only pedestrian location and density in risk 
estimation. Research focusing specifically on individual-level impacts remains limited. Bernardini 
et al. conducted a foundational study examining how pedestrian evacuation behavior influences 
flood risk assessment and emergency response planning [4], proposing risk indicators at both urban-
regional and outdoor spatial scales. Further, Bernardini et al. [14] explored the effects of unsteady 
water flow on pedestrian behavior, introducing a microscale, behavior-based approach to assessing 
flood risk in outdoor urban environments. Although these studies have significantly advanced flood 
risk assessment methodologies, they are largely constrained by their reliance on static evaluation 
criteria, which fail to capture the dynamic nature of pedestrian evacuation during flood events. 
Bridging this gap is essential for developing more comprehensive and effective strategies for flood 


===== [page 6] =====
5 
 
risk management. 
Ensuring the safety of individuals remains the primary objective of emergency evacuation 
procedures. In the field of urban flood research, real-time assessment of pedestrian evacuation 
capacity and flood hazard levels has become a key priority [13]. Recent studies have investigated 
the mechanisms of human instability during flooding, establishing discriminative criteria through 
water tank experiments conducted at multiple scales [15, 16], mechanical modeling [17, 18], or 
integrated approaches combining both methods [19], involving individuals with varying physical 
characteristics [20] and postures [16], along with different hydrodynamic conditions [21]. These 
studies have identified two primary physical mechanisms underlying human instability: moment 
instability (commonly referred to as toppling) and friction instability (commonly referred to as 
sliding) [22]. Subsequent research has derived threshold values for the onset of instability using 
mechanical models [19], complemented by experimental data to define critical combinations of 
water depth and flow velocity associated with human instability [16, 18, 23], or has proposed 
discriminant equations incorporating individual characteristics [20]. More recently, probabilistic 
approaches based on laboratory experiments have been developed to account for uncertainty in 
pedestrian stability, including scenarios in which individuals move either with or against the flow 
[24]. 
Pedestrian evacuation models designed for flood scenarios integrate flood characteristics with 
evacuation dynamics to assess how flood hazards influence pedestrian movement processes. These 
models are generally classified into macroscopic and microscopic categories. Macroscopic models, 
typically developed for urban-scale flooding and often based on Geographic Information Systems 
(GIS), account for the dynamic effects of flood propagation on accessible pedestrian and vehicular 


===== [page 7] =====
6 
 
routes, as well as shelter availability [25], to estimate evacuation times [26, 27]. They have also 
been used to explore optimal evacuation strategies [28] evacuation pathways [29-32], among other 
applications. However, by their nature, macroscopic models overlook detailed pedestrian movement 
behaviors, focusing instead on large-scale evacuation patterns. In contrast, microscopic models 
emphasize the fine-grained simulation of pedestrian movement and their interactions with 
floodwaters. By integrating hydrodynamic models with behavioral models of pedestrians, 
researchers have examined various aspects of flood evacuation, such as pedestrian decision-making 
[33], evacuation processes [34-37], and evacuation efficiency [38-40]. These studies have also 
contributed to the development of new flood hazard assessment methodologies [41]. Despite these 
advancements, a critical limitation persists: the lack of empirical data on pedestrian movement 
characteristics during flood events. Consequently, many current evacuation models are overly 
simplified and idealized, which limits their ability to accurately represent the complex interactions 
between pedestrians and flood dynamics. Overcoming this limitation is crucial for enhancing the 
realism and practical applicability of flood evacuation modeling. 
Understanding the movement dynamics of pedestrians in water is essential for developing 
effective flood evacuation models and forms a critical basis for formulating accurate evacuation 
strategies and rescue protocols. Researchers have conducted experiments measuring individual 
pedestrian speeds in controlled environments, such as water tanks [42, 43] and swimming pools 
[44], under varying water depths. These studies consistently demonstrate that pedestrian movement 
speed in water is significantly slower than on land. An exponential relationship has been established 
between pedestrian speed and the specific flood force per unit width (a function of water depth and 
flow velocity), indicating a pronounced reduction in speed as water depth increases [42-44]. 


===== [page 8] =====
7 
 
Moreover, pedestrians exhibit distinct biomechanical characteristics when moving through water 
[45]. Treadmill-based experiments have investigated the biomechanical responses of individuals 
across different age groups performing various locomotion modes at different water depths. 
Biomechanical investigations have further explored human motion in aquatic environments using 
wearable inertial measurement units (IMUs) and pressure sensors [46, 47]. Findings show that 
during the stance phase in water, EMG signals from the ankle, knee, and hip joints are markedly 
lower compared to those on land [48]. In contrast, walking in water leads to increased EMG activity 
[49] and elevated cardiorespiratory responses [50] with increasing walking speed. Furthermore, key 
gait parameters such as knee joint range of motion, ground reaction force magnitude, momentum, 
and impulse differ significantly from those observed during land-based walking [49, 51, 52] The 
interaction between water and pedestrians reduces joint moments during locomotion and alters joint 
coordination patterns [53]. However, most of these studies have focused on individual movement 
dynamics, with limited attention given to crowd-level motion characteristics in flood evacuation 
scenarios. Recently, Li et al. conducted experimental studies on crowd movement in a swimming 
pool with a water depth of 0.60 m [54] and examined pedestrian flow through bottlenecks [55]. 
These studies revealed significant differences in both macroscopic features (such as the relationships 
between pedestrian density, speed, and flow rate) and microscopic features (including pedestrian 
behavior and stepping patterns) of crowd movement in water compared to on-land conditions [54]. 
Additionally, evacuation efficiency at various exit widths was found to be considerably lower in 
water than on land [55]. Despite these advancements, the exact mechanisms by which water depth 
affects pedestrian dynamics remain poorly understood. In particular, quantitative investigations of 
the fundamental speed–density–flow relationships under flooded conditions remain scarce. 


===== [page 9] =====
8 
 
Although previous experiments at a water depth of 0.60 m provided initial insights into the speed–
density characteristics [54], the available dataset was limited to densities below 2.2 ped/m², leaving 
the high-density regime unexplored. As a result, a comprehensive fundamental diagram for 
unidirectional pedestrian flow in inundated environments has not yet been systematically 
established. A deeper understanding of the interaction between flood conditions and pedestrian 
behavior—particularly the influence of water on evacuation processes—is vital for enhancing public 
safety and maintaining social stability during flood events.  
 
1.3 Research objectives 
This study aims to address this gap by conducting controlled experimental trials to establish a 
comprehensive pedestrian motion database across multiple water depths and to elucidate the 
underlying mechanisms by which flooding influences crowd dynamics. The specific objectives are 
as follows: 
(1) to collect systematic pedestrian trajectory data under no-water conditions and three 
representative inundation depths, thereby establishing a foundational database for different flood 
scenarios; 
(2) to investigate the impact of water depth on the pedestrian speed–density–flow relationship 
and to quantify how hydrodynamic resistance interacts with pedestrian density to influence 
pedestrian speed dynamics; 
(3) to analyze changes in nearest-neighbor spatial distributions and to identify how water depth 
and density jointly regulate pedestrian spatial dependencies; 
(4) to examine the coupling patterns of step length and step frequency across water depths and 


===== [page 10] =====
9 
 
to uncover how hydrodynamic resistance alters the mechanisms of speed adjustment. 
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 describes the experimental setup, 
data extraction procedures, and analytical methods. Section 3 presents the experimental results, 
including pedestrian trajectories and behaviors, fundamental diagrams, nearest-neighbor spatial 
distributions, and headway and pedestrian speed. Section 4 discusses the findings and concludes 
this study. 
 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1 Experimental setup 
In July 2024, controlled unidirectional pedestrian flow experiments were conducted in the 
University of Science and Technology of China. Four water depth conditions were systematically 
tested:0.90 m (waist level), 0.60 m (above the knee), 0.35 m (below the knee), and 0 m (no water). 
Participants were recruited through online posters, and all volunteers were briefed in advance about 
the experimental procedures and potential risks involved. A total of 188 undergraduate student 
volunteers (101 males, 87 females) participated in the experiment, with an average age of 22.3 ± 
3.5 years, an average height of 1.70 ± 0.10 m (range: 1.51~1.90 m), and an average body mass of 
62.6 ± 12.0 kg. Each water depth condition was tested in a separate session: 114 participants in the 
0.90 m condition (first afternoon), 95 in the 0.60 m condition (second morning), 134 in the 0.35 m 
condition (second afternoon), and 89 in the 0 m condition (second evening). To ensure participant 
safety, six certified lifeguards were present throughout all experimental sessions, and on-site 
medical support was available.  
The water-based experiments were conducted in a controlled environment with still water (zero

an open area in front of a teaching building. Metal guardrails were installed to define the boundaries

the lower-left corner of the 8 m corridor. The left side of the experimental layout is the 3.5 m wide 
Holding Area, where participants waited before the experiment commenced. The global density in

3. Results 
3.1 Trajectories 
Fig. 3 illustrates pedestrian trajectories of three scenarios under different experiment conditions, 
where trajectory color represents instantaneous walking speed. The first column corresponds to 
scenario U-070-180-180 (low density), the second to U-180-180-180 (medium density), and the 
third to U-180-180-095 (high density). From top to bottom, each column represents no-water 
conditions, 0.35 m, 0.60 m, and 0.90 m water depth. Under low-density conditions, pedestrians in 
dry land environments exhibit significantly higher walking speeds compared to those in flooded 


===== [page 17] =====
16 
 
conditions, whereas at high density, the differences across water depths are less noticeable. 
Furthermore, in no-water environments, pedestrians tend to maintain a buffer zone from the channel 
boundaries, while in flooded conditions, trajectories often extend to or beyond the boundaries and 
display greater variability, indicating disorganized movement patterns. 
 
 
Fig. 3. Pedestrian trajectories of three scenarios under different experiment conditions, where

Fig. 4 displays the corresponding lateral position distributions, where the number of peaks 
corresponds to the number of pedestrian lanes. Under no-water conditions, two lanes are observed 
at low density, and three lanes emerge at medium and high densities. At a water depth of 0.35 m, 
the lane structure becomes less distinct, and at 0.90 m, the peaks are almost absent, suggesting the 
breakdown of lane formation. These observations indicate that hydrodynamic resistance impairs 
pedestrians’ ability to control body positioning and perceive spatial boundaries.

To provide a clearer representation of the interdependence among pedestrian density, speed, 
and pedestrian flow, we applied the binning method [62] for data processing. Fig. 6 presents the 
error-bar plots of the pedestrian speed–density (Fig. 6 (a)) and pedestrian flow–density (Fig. 6 (b)) 
relationships across different water depths. Overall, within the observed density range, both water 
and no-water conditions exhibit the characteristic decrease in pedestrian speed with increasing 
density. In the low-density regime (< 2.4 ped/m²), pedestrian speeds under water conditions are 
significantly lower than those under no-water conditions, although this difference gradually 
diminishes as density increases. When density exceeds 2.4 ped/m², the pedestrian speeds across all 
four experimental conditions converge. This trend indicates that at low densities, hydrodynamic 
resistance is the primary factor limiting pedestrian movement, whereas at higher densities, inter-
pedestrian interactions become the dominant influence, thereby reducing the relative impact of water. 
Under the three experimental water depths, the trend of decreasing pedestrian speed with 
increasing density was highly consistent. The pedestrian speed differences between adjacent water 
depths remained stable at 0.05 ± 0.024 m/s and 0.06 ± 0.027 m/s, respectively. This nearly parallel 
downward shift relationship indicates that water depth primarily results in a uniform reduction in 
overall walking speed, without significantly altering the shape of the pedestrian speed–density curve. 
In other words, under equivalent density conditions, the extent of pedestrian speed reduction 
remains largely the same, indicating that the influence of water depth on pedestrian speed in 


===== [page 21] =====
20 
 
unidirectional pedestrian flow is relatively limited. 
The differences in the pedestrian speed–density relationship are also reflected in the pedestrian 
flow–density relationship. Generally, under all experimental conditions, the pedestrian flow 
increases with rising density to a peak (commonly referred to as capacity), after which it declines. 
Under no-water conditions, the pedestrian flow remains at 2.05 ± 0.038 ped/(m·s) within the density 
range of 1.5–2.25 ped/m², demonstrating a strong passage capacity. In contrast, under the three 
experimental water depth conditions, the pedestrian flow stabilizes once the density reaches 
approximately 2.4 ped/m², and the maximum capacity decreases progressively with increasing water 
depth, measured at 1.86 ± 0.065 ped/(m·s), 1.75 ± 0.071 ped/(m·s), and 1.50 ± 0.153 ped/(m·s), 
respectively. These findings clearly indicate that increased water depth systematically reduces the 
overall passage efficiency of unidirectional pedestrian flow.

3.3 Spatial distribution of the nearest neighbors 
The analysis of the fundamental diagram reveals the overall pedestrian speed–density 
relationship and capacity differences in pedestrian flow under varying water depth conditions, 
providing a crucial basis for understanding the overall impact of water depth on pedestrian dynamics.

collective movement and does not precisely quantify the specific influence of relative positions on 
pedestrian motion. Therefore, beyond the analysis of the fundamental diagram, further investigation 
into individual-level spatial characteristics is necessary. The spatial distribution of pedestrians’ 
nearest neighbors represents a core manifestation of spatial dependence in collective movement [65] 
characterizing the spatial features of the interaction range where direct interpersonal interactions 
occur.

this case, congestion effects dominate the spatial behavior, and the influence of water depth on the 
nearest neighbor distribution becomes limited.

self-organizing regulatory mechanisms. A plausible explanation is that this spatial pattern is closely 
associated with pedestrians’ step length. During walking, individuals typically maintain sufficient

3.4 Headway and pedestrian speed 
Previous analyses of pedestrian speed characteristics based on individual local density and the 
spatial distribution of nearest neighbors have demonstrated that the presence of water causes a 
contraction of the space surrounding pedestrians, with the longitudinal space exhibiting greater 
sensitivity to density changes. It is important to note that although such analyses capture local 
congestion states and interpersonal proximity, they remain insufficient for fully characterizing how 
pedestrians dynamically respond to available space ahead in unidirectional pedestrian flows. 
Numerous studies have revealed a stronger, more direct, and stable correlation between pedestrian 
speed and headway—defined as the distance to the nearest pedestrian ahead—compared to the 
correlation with local density [59, 66]. In other words, during unidirectional movement, pedestrians 
primarily rely on their perception and assessment of the space ahead, rather than on lateral or 
rearward spatial conditions. Therefore, to better understand the mechanisms underlying pedestrian 
speed adjustment in water conditions, it is essential to further examine the relationship between 
headway and pedestrian speed. This will help elucidate how the leading pedestrian influences the 
behavior of the follower and provide empirical support for developing pedestrian flow models that 
accurately reflect movement characteristics in flooded environments. 
When collecting headway data, the first 90 pedestrians within the measurement area were 
selected as analysis subjects. Fig. 10 illustrates the relationship between pedestrian speed and 
headway under different water depth conditions. A binning method [62] was applied along both axes, 
using intervals of 0.1 m (headway) and 0.025 m/s (speed), respectively. The analysis reveals a clear 


===== [page 30] =====
29 
 
two-regime pattern in the relationship between pedestrian speed and headway: 
(1) Constrained regime: When the headway is small, pedestrian movement is restricted by the 
space ahead. A significant linear positive correlation between pedestrian speed and headway is 
observed during this phase. Pedestrians exhibit high sensitivity to changes in the space in front and 
actively adjust their walking speed to avoid potential collisions. 
(2) Free regime: When the headway exceeds a certain critical threshold, pedestrians are no 
longer directly influenced by the individual ahead. The pedestrian speed stabilizes and remains 
largely unaffected by further increases in headway, indicating that pedestrians have entered a free- 
pedestrian flow state.

4. Discussion 
a. 
b. 


===== [page 38] =====

Global warming has increased the frequency and severity of extreme weather events, posing 
growing risks to human life from floods. Floods damage infrastructure and often force pedestrians 
to walk through water during evacuations, increasing safety risks. Although current flood 
evacuation models have improved in hydrodynamic simulation and path planning, most still rely on 
simple water depth thresholds for risk assessment, with limited consideration of the interaction 
between flood conditions and pedestrian movement. Systematic investigations of pedestrian 
dynamics under varying water depths remain scarce. 
This study conducted controlled pedestrian flow experiments under no-water conditions and 
three water depths (0.35 m: below knee; 0.60 m: above knee; 0.90 m: waist level), collecting 
dynamic crowd data. It represents the first empirical analysis comparing crowd movement 
characteristics across multiple water depth conditions. This section discusses the key findings, 
critically examines the study’s limitations, and suggests directions for future research. 
 
4.1 Density-dependent hydrodynamic effects on speed 
This study observed that water has a significant density-dependent effect on the pedestrian 
speed–density relationship of unidirectional pedestrian flow. At low densities, water resistance is 
the primary factor limiting pedestrian speed, causing reductions of up to 45%. However, its 
influence decreases as density increases. When density reaches or exceeds 2.4 ped/m², pedestrian 
speeds converge across experimental conditions, and interactions between pedestrians become the 
main cause of deceleration. This density-dependent transition reflects a shift in the dominant control 
mechanism of movement. At low densities, pedestrians have sufficient headway and move close to 
their preferred walking speed. Under such conditions, hydrodynamic drag—proportional to relative 


===== [page 39] =====
38 
 
velocity and wetted body area [12]—directly increases biomechanical workload and reduces 
pedestrian attainable speed. In contrast, at high densities, extremely limited headway forces 
pedestrians into short-step, low-speed movement constrained by collision avoidance. Because 
hydrodynamic drag decreases rapidly with speed, its relative contribution becomes secondary 
compared to spatial blockage imposed by preceding pedestrians.  
However, the impact of water depth on the pedestrian speed–density curve is relatively minor, 
with stable speed differences of 0.05 ± 0.024 m/s and 0.06 ± 0.027 m/s between adjacent depths 
across the observed range. The nearly parallel downward shift of the speed–density curves suggests 
that increasing water depth acts primarily as an approximately uniform resistive load rather than 
altering the intrinsic density–interaction mechanism. This limited impact can be largely attributed 
to the collective hydrodynamic shielding effect (or wake effect) within the group. In a continuous 
unidirectional flow, pedestrians closely follow one another; the leading individuals effectively 
displace the static water, creating a localized wake zone [20]. Consequently, the trailing pedestrians 
experience significantly diminished hydrodynamic resistance compared to walking in isolation. 
Furthermore, in unidirectional flow, pedestrians move in an ordered manner. The collective pacing 
behavior and spatial alignment help maintain group momentum, thereby limiting the marginal speed 
penalty (0.05–0.06 m/s) imposed by each incremental increase in water depth. 
 
4.2. Practical Implications for Evacuation Modeling and Management 
These findings are of both theoretical importance and practical relevance. In micro-evacuation 
model development, the results demonstrate that water resistance significantly influences pedestrian 
speed in a density-dependent manner. Therefore, integrating a density-adjusted water resistance 


===== [page 40] =====
39 
 
term into model parameterization can enhance the realism of simulations across different crowd 
densities. Furthermore, the observed variations in step length and step frequency across water depths 
provide a quantitative foundation for modeling pedestrian speed regulation, thereby improving 
model adaptability to multi-water-depth scenarios. 
From the perspective of evacuation strategy formulation, the consistent relationship between 
nearest neighbor spatial distribution and step length reveals systematic differences in longitudinal 
and lateral space usage under varying water depths. This provides a scientific basis for designing 
crowd control strategies, determining passage widths, and setting safety distances in flood-prone 
areas such as subways, tunnels, and underground commercial zones. The differences in pedestrian 
speed regulation mechanisms suggest that, under deep water conditions, reliance on step frequency 
adjustments should be minimized, with greater emphasis placed on managing step length or 
regulating crowd density to maintain evacuation efficiency. At the engineering and emergency 
management level, these findings can guide the design of wading evacuation routes, passage 
capacity evaluations, and predictions of rescue team movement speeds. 
 
4.3. Limitations and Future Work 
However, the limitations of this study need to be acknowledged. First, the experiments were 
conducted under controlled still water conditions (zero background flow velocity) to isolate the 
effect of water depth and did not consider variables such as water flow speed or pedestrian 
psychological states. The experimental setting was idealized; in real-world flood scenarios, flowing 
water exerts additional hydrodynamic forces that can significantly compromise pedestrian stability, 
especially at greater depths. Second, due to logistical constraints, the participant groups across the 


===== [page 41] =====
40 
 
four experimental conditions were not consistent, and all were university students, which limits the 
generalizability of the findings. 
Future research should incorporate a wider range of environmental conditions, including 
different water flow velocities and spatial layouts, to identify more universal patterns of pedestrian 
movement. Enhancing the representativeness and diversity of participant samples will further 
improve the understanding of behavioral complexity. Crucially, while this study focused on 
movement dynamics in static water, investigating stability thresholds and hydrodynamic-induced 
imbalance under varying flow velocities represents an essential direction for future research to fully 
assess flood evacuation risks. 
 
5. Conclusion 
This study established pedestrian speed–density–flow relationships under different water 
depths, demonstrating how water depth influences crowd spatial distribution. It also identified a 
two-regime pattern in the headway–pedestrian speed relationship and analyzed how water resistance 
modifies the pedestrian speed regulation mechanism through changes in step length and step 
frequency. The main conclusions are as follows: 
(1). Water has a significant density-dependent effect on the pedestrian speed–density 
relationship of unidirectional pedestrian flow. At low densities, water resistance is the primary factor 
limiting pedestrian speed, causing reductions of up to 45%. However, its influence decreases as 
density increases. When density reaches or exceeds 2.4 ped/m², pedestrian speeds converge across 
experimental conditions, and interactions between pedestrians become the main cause of 
deceleration. The impact of water depth on the pedestrian speed–density curve is relatively minor, 


===== [page 42] =====
41 
 
with stable speed differences of 0.05 ± 0.024 m/s and 0.06 ± 0.027 m/s between adjacent depths 
across the observed range. However, pedestrian flow capacity declines progressively with 
increasing water depth, decreasing by 9.3%, 14.6%, and 26.8%, respectively, compared to the no-
water condition (2.05 ± 0.038 ped/(m·s)). 
(2). Both water depth and density influence the spatial distribution of nearest neighbors. At low 
densities, the distribution is elliptical along the movement direction, indicating a preference for 
forward personal space. As density increases, the distribution contracts, inviolable personal space 
shrinks, especially in the longitudinal direction. Greater water depths further compress forward 
spacing, resulting in more circular distributions and reducing the occupied area by 55–60%. Under 
high-density conditions, congestion becomes the dominant factor, and the influence of water depth 
weakens. This transition correlates with step length: when step length exceeds shoulder width, 
neighbors concentrate on both sides of the movement axis; as step length approaches shoulder width, 
the distribution becomes nearly circular. 
(3). The pedestrian speed–headway relationships exhibited a two-regime pattern—constrained 
and free—across all water depths, reflecting the stable speed adjustment mechanism in 
unidirectional pedestrian flow. Gait analysis reveals that water depth significantly alters the speed 
regulation pathway: under shallow water conditions, pedestrians can still adjust both step length and 
step frequency in response to changes in headway. However, when the water depth exceeds knee 
height (≥0.60 m), the sensitivity of step frequency to headway diminishes, and speed regulation 
gradually shifts to rely primarily on changes in stride length. Further comparison shows that the 
water environment significantly impacts pedestrian gait, leading to a sharp reduction in free step 
length (approximately a 37.7% decrease), while free step frequency monotonically decreases with 


===== [page 43] =====
42 
 
increasing water depth.
