ABSTRACT 
Background: Satellite observations following flooding coupled with electronic health data collected through 
syndromic surveillance systems (SyS) may be useful in efficiently characterizing and responding to health risks 
associated with flooding. 
Results: There was a 10% (95% Confidence Interval (CI): 1%–19%) increase in asthma related ED visits and 22% 
(95% CI: 5%–41%) increase in insect bite related ED visits in the flooded ZCTAs compared to non-flooded ZCTAs 
during the flood period. One month following the floods, diarrhea related ED visits were increased by 15% (95% 
CI: 4%–27%) for flooded ZCTAs and children and adolescents from flooded ZCTAs had elevated risk for dehydration related ED visits. During the protracted period (2–3 months after the flood period), the risk for asthma, 
insect bite, and diarrhea related ED visits were elevated among the flooded ZCTAs. Effect modification by reported age, ethnicity and race was observed. 
Conclusion: Combining satellite observations with SyS data can be helpful in characterizing the location and 
timing of environmentally mediated adverse health outcomes, which may be useful for refining disaster resilience measures to mitigate health outcomes following flooding. 
1. Introduction 
Extreme precipitation caused by rain, snow, or hail often leads to 
localized flash flooding and riverine flooding (Du et al., 2010; National 
Weather Service, 2005). A range of health outcomes have been found to 
increase during and following such flooding events (Ahern et al., 2005; 
Du et al., 2010; Paterson et al., 2018; D. D. Saulnier et al., 2017). The 
direct or immediate health related impacts of flooding include 
drowning, injuries, and hypothermia (Ahern et al., 2005; Du et al., 2010; 
Paterson et al., 2018; D. D. Saulnier et al., 2017). In the United States, 
the majority of flood-related drowning incidents occur in motor vehicles 
(Ashley and Ashley, 2008). Water contamination, crowding of people in 
shelters, and poor sanitation following flooding events increase the 
chances for fecal-oral transmission and may lead to an increase in 
gastroenteritis and other diarrheal diseases (Ahern et al., 2005; Paterson 
et al., 2018; D. D. Saulnier et al., 2017). The presence of stagnant flood 
waters may also contribute to increased transmission of vector-borne 
diseases such as dengue, malaria, and chikungunya in countries where 
such diseases are endemic (Ahern et al., 2005; Du et al., 2010; Paterson 
et al., 2018). The lack of access to medical resources contributes to 
exacerbation of chronic health conditions such as asthma, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes (Paterson et al., 2018; D. D. Saulnier et al., 
2017). Mental stress due to loss of life and property can lead to anxiety, 
depression, and Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) among the 
affected population (Ahern et al., 2005; D. D. Saulnier et al., 2017; 
Walker-Springett et al., 2017). Flooding has also been shown to be

The number of global flood events occurring each year has increased 
between 1985 and 2019 (Jonkman, 2005; Kettner and Brakenridge, 
2021). Within the United States, the number of billion-dollar weather 
and climate disasters related to flooding has increased between 1985 
and 2020 (Smith, 2020). Also, the frequency and intensity of heavy 
precipitation events have increased in most parts of the United States 
since 1901 (Easterling et al., 2017). The percentage of land area that 
experiences extreme one-day rainfall has increased from 10% to 18% 
between 1985 and 2015 (NOAA, 2020). Tropical cyclone precipitation is 
projected to increase by 14% with a 2 ◦C increase in global mean temperature (Knutson et al., 2020), and the frequency and intensity of heavy 
precipitation events are expected to increase over the 21st century 
(Easterling et al., 2017). These projected increases in heavy rainfall may 
lead to increases in local flooding over the 21st century (Seneviratne 
et al., 2012). 
Given the health consequences of previous flooding events and 
anticipated increases in heavy precipitation contributing to more 
frequent floods, it is essential to advance methods for monitoring 
flooding-related health outcomes. With advances in remote sensing and 
satellite image processing, inundation products are readily available 
within days of flooding (Galantowicz John et al., 2017; Kettner and 
Brakenridge, 2021; Shen et al., 2019) to help delineate neighborhoods 
that were exposed to floodwaters. Such products coupled with near-real 
time electronic health data collected through syndromic surveillance 
systems (SyS) can be used to efficiently characterize health risks associated with flooding. SyS has been shown to be useful in detecting the 
surge in emergency department (ED) visits and pressure on healthcare 
services in areas outside or surrounding the disaster areas following 
Hurricane Harvey and Irma (Wiedeman, 2020; William, 2020). It has 
been used in monitoring flood-related health impacts such as carbon 
monoxide (CO) exposure, injury, and gastrointestinal complaints 
following Hurricanes Florence, Harvey, and Sandy (Chen et al., 2013; 
Tanz et al., 2019; William, 2020). SyS was also useful in studying 
medication needs following Hurricanes Florence and Katrina (Jhung 
et al., 2007; Tanz et al., 2019); however, in each of these previous 
studies, only temporal variation in ED visits within an area broadly 
defined as affected (or evacuation-specific medication demand) was 
used to identify flood-related health impacts. In this study, we aim to 
improve estimations of flood-related health risks by refining estimates of 
exposure to floodwaters following Tropical Storm Imelda using satellite 
observations to quantify inundation in each ZIP Code and study the

Imelda was a short-lived storm that made landfall on September 17, 
2019 where southeastern Texas experienced rainfall totals greater than 
30 inches between September 16–22, with the highest recorded rainfall 
of 44.29 inches over Fannett in Jefferson County, Texas (Latto and Berg, 
2019). Fannett also received 31 inches in a 12-h period. This heavy 
precipitation makes Tropical Storm Imelda the fifth-wettest tropical 
cyclone in the contiguous United States (Latto and Berg, 2019). The 
heavy rainfall precipitated flooding over Jefferson, Orange, Hardin, 
Jasper, Newton, Galveston, Harris, Liberty, and Montgomery counties 
(Fig. 1). Five people died due to the flooding, and 10,878 homes were 
estimated to be flooded as of September 30, 2019 (Latto and Berg, 
2019). The event was recognized as a major disaster by the Federal 
Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) with estimated damages 
totaling USD 70 million (FEMA, 2019). 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Remotely-observed flooding and flood period 
The inundation that occurred following the landfall of Tropical 
Storm Imelda has been mapped using earth observations captured by 
two active remote sensing satellites, the Moderate Resolution Imaging 
Spectroradiometer (MODIS) and Sentinel 1-A between September 18 
and 21, 2019 (Chini et al., 2017; Shen et al., 2019), and the final 
maximum observed flood raster with ground resolution of 125 m has 
been provided by the Dartmouth Flood Observatory (Kettner and 
Brakenridge, 2021). The extent of the inundation data covers 328 Zip 
Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTA) in Texas. The percentage of land area 
inundated for each ZCTA was calculated using the inundation data. The 
mean of the percentage of land area inundated was 1.9% (SD = 4.8%). 
Based on the fraction of land area flooded, ZCTAs were split into three 
categories: non-flooded, moderately flooded (>0%–2%), and highly 
flooded (>2%–36%) (Fig. 1). The threshold separating the two flooded 
categories was selected by using the median value of the fraction of land 
area flooded for all the ZCTAs that were flooded as per the inundation 
data. 
Near-real time coarse resolution flood maps provided by AER 
FloodScan (Galantowicz et al., 2018) along with stream gauge flood 
maps (United States Geological Survey(USGS), 2020) were used to find 
the number of days ZCTAs within the study region were inundated. 
Flooding at multiple locations was reported as per stream gauge measurements on September 19. From the AER flood maps, which provides 
gridded estimates of daily flood fraction at 22 km resolution, it was 
observed that parts of the region were inundated on September 20, 
2019, with major inundation observed on September 22, and by October 
2, none of the regions in the study area were inundated; therefore, the 
flood period was defined as September 19 - October 1, 2019. 
2.2. Syndromic surveillance data 
ED visits that occurred between June 1 and December 30, 2019 
collected through the SyS of the Houston Health Department (HHD) and 
partners have been used in this study (Fig. 2). The data was gathered by 
the HHD from 85 facilities, and included a total of 3,251,700 ED visits of 
patients whose address ZIP Codes fall within our study area. Each ED 
visit record contained patient’s self-reported home address ZIP Code, 
age, race (White, Black/African American, Asian, American Indian, 
Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, Arab, Middle Eastern or North African, and unknown), sex (male or female), ethnicity (Hispanic, NonHispanic, and unknown), discharge diagnosis, chief complaint, and 
provider diagnosis. While SyS data include demographic information, 
they contain no unique person-identifier. The patient’s ZIP Codes in the 
ED visit records were mapped to ZCTAs using the UDS Mapper cross 
walk (Uniform Data System, 2019), and the mapped ZCTAs were then 
used to assign an inundation category to each ED visit record. The demographic distribution of the ED visits is provided in Supplementary 
Table 1. Records with one or more of eight health outcomes: CO 
poisoning, diarrhea, dehydration, other heat-related illness, insect bite, 
cardiovascular diseases, pregnancy/delivery/pregnancy loss, and 
asthma, shown to be directly or indirectly associated with flooding 
(Ramesh et al., 2021; Dellinger et al., 1996; Greenough et al., 2008; 
Paterson et al., 2018; Weinberger et al., 2021; Xiao et al., 2019) were 
considered for further analysis. Utilizing the chief complaint and 
discharge diagnosis field, the records were assigned to the cause-specific 
health outcomes by applying Electronic Surveillance System for the 
Early Notification of Community-Based Epidemics (ESSENCE) definitions (CDC, 2020) (R code provided in Supplementary Materials). The 
ESSENCE definitions were used for assigning the cause-specific health 
outcomes, and the numbers of records that overlap between ED visits 
belonging to each outcome are provided in Supplementary Tables 2 and 
3. As there were only eight records matching CO poisoning-related visits 
during the flood period, we did not consider this health outcome for 
further analysis. For pregnancy-related visits, 186 out of 146,248 were 
not assigned due to sex reported as male. To study the effects of flooding 
alone, the week before the beginning of the floods (September 12–18) (n 
= 111,246) was considered a washout period and ED visits occurring on 
this week were removed from the analysis, to remove potential effects of 
the preparation phase (Florida Department of Health, 2015). Further

2.3. Study design 
By using a control period in addition to a control series, the study 
follows a controlled before after study design (Lopez, ´ 2018) where the 
change in ED visits between the control period and the flood or 
post-flood periods among the flooded ZCTAs is compared to the change 
in ED visits among the non-flooded ZCTAs. The ED visits from the 
non-flooded ZCTAs were assigned to a control group, and the ED visits 
from flooded ZCTAs (the moderately and highly flooded together) were 
considered cases (Fig. 2). The period before the start of the flooding was 
treated as the control period (June 1 - September 12) to analyze the 
average rate of change in ED visits during the flood period, acute 
post-flood phase i.e., a month after the flood period (October 2 - 
November 1) and protracted post-flood phase i.e., two to three months 
after the flood (November 2 - December 30). The number of ED visits 
related to heat-related illness from the flooded ZCTAs during the protracted post-flood phase was less than 15, therefore were not analyzed 
for the protracted period (Supplementary Table 4). The AER FloodScan 
maps also indicated minor flooding in some regions of the study area 
during June 7–9, therefore a sensitivity analysis was completed by 
removing this period and one month following this period (June 10 - 
July 9) from the control period. 
2.4. Statistical analysis 
Modified Poisson regression with robust standard error estimations 
has been used previously to assess health outcomes associated with 
environmental events (Bhaskaran et al., 2013; Florida Department of 
Health, 2015; D. Saulnier et al., 2018b) was used to estimate the rate 
ratios (RR) for each of the seven cause-specific ED visits among the 
flooded ZCTAs with respect to the non-flooded ZCTAs after adjusting for 
available individual-level characteristics (age, race, ethnicity, sex), day 
of the week, and difference from control period. The equation for estimating rate ratios for any one of the seven cause-specific ED visits (e.g., 
diarrhea) can be represented as:

“ZCTA_flood” represents the flood category of patient’s ZCTA (reference 
non-flooded), and “period” indicates control (reference), flood, or either 
of the two post-flood periods. The exponent of the estimate β1 is the rate 
ratio comparing ED visits from flooded ZCTA to non-flooded ZCTA 
during control period and the exponent of β2 is the ratio of rates of ED 
visits during flood/post-flood periods and control period among the nonflooded ZCTAs. So, the exponent of the interaction term β3 provides the 
rate ratio for flooded ZCTAs compared to non-flooded ZCTAs during 
flood/post-flood period in comparison to corresponding difference between the flooded and non-flooded ZCTAs during the control period. 
Total records per day from corresponding patient’s ZCTA were used as 
an offset in the model to account for the changes in total ED visits each 
day. To understand the change in the effect of flooding over these causespecific ED visits with respect to the patient’s sex, ethnicity, race, and 
age group, stratified rate ratios were also estimated with adjustment for 
the other factors mentioned above. Due to low numbers in some reported categories, reported race was further collapsed into White, 
Black/African-American, and other for this stratified analysis. Additionally, effect modification was evaluated by including an interaction 
term in the main model for each of the demographic factors. For this, 
male, non-Hispanic, and White was used as the referent category for sex, 
ethnicity, and race, respectively. For the age stratified models, different 
age categories were used for each cause-specific ED visit as the age 
distribution of ED visits among each cause-specific ED visit is different 
(Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 4). The rate ratio corresponding to change 
in ED visits with respect to one percentage increase in ZCTA flooded was 
also estimated in a separate model by substituting the proportion of 
ZCTA flood (continuous variable) in place of the categorical “ZCTA_flood” variable in the above equation.

3. Results 
The demographic distribution of ED visits across each period and 
each category of flooding is presented in Supplementary Table 4. With 
respect to the baseline (control period), the average rate of six cause 
specific ED visits (i.e., asthma, insect bite, cardiovascular diseases, 
dehydration, diarrhea, heat related illness) among the flooded ZCTAs 
was greater than the average rate of corresponding visits among the nonflooded ZCTAs (Supplementary Table 5). The below results focus on the 
additional change in rate ratios during the flood period and post-flood 
periods (Equation (1), β3), accounting for these differences in the control period (Equation (1), β1). 
3.1. Cause-specific ED visits during flooding 
During the flood period, ED visits related to insect bites and asthma 
increased by 22% (95% CI: 5%–41%) and 10% (95% CI: 1%–19%), 
respectively, in the flooded ZCTAs compared to the non-flooded ZCTAs 
(Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 6). The increase in ED visits related to 
insect bites occurred in the highly flooded ZCTAs (36% [95% CI: 14%– 
61%]), but not the moderately flooded ZCTAs (6% [95% CI: -12%– 
27%]) (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 7). From the stratum specific estimates, it was found that the association between flooding and ED visits 
related to insect bites was strongest among the patients older than 17 
years of age (RR = 1.64; 95% CI: 1.19–2.25) (Fig. 3) and effect modification was significant when compared to patients aged 6–17 (p-value 
= 0.007) (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 8). The stratum specific rate ratio 
for ED visits related to insect bites showed elevated risk among age 
group 0–5 years of age (RR = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.05–1.54), female (RR =
1.31; 95% CI: 1.10–1.56), and White (RR = 1.23; 95% CI: 1.03–1.46) 
patients (Fig. 3); however, effect modification by age, race, sex, or 
ethnicity was not significant (Supplementary Table 8). The increased 
risk for asthma was more pronounced among female patients (RR =
1.15; 95% CI: 1.03–1.28) and non-Hispanic patients (RR = 1.15; 95% CI: 
1.04–1.26) (Fig. 3) but effect modification was not statistically significant (Supplementary Table 8). Cardiovascular related ED visits were 
decreased by 10% (1%–19%) among Hispanic patients and effect 
modification was significant (p-value = 0.009) (Fig. 3; Supplementary 
Table 8). Other cause-specific ED visits (diarrhea, dehydration, heatrelated illness, and pregnancy related ED visits) did not show overall 
or stratum specific increases or decreases during the flood period among 
the flooded ZCTAs compared to the non-flooded ZCTAs (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 6); however, significant effect modification by age 
was detected for dehydration, with the rate ratio higher among patients 
of age 18–50 compared to patients older than 64 (p-value = 0.04) 
(Supplementary Table 8). 
Using percentage of ZCTA flooded as a continuous variable, it was 
observed that on average, for one percentage increase in ZCTA flooded, 
ED visits related to asthma and insect bite were increased by 2.2% 
(0.5%–3.9%) and 4.1% (0.9%–7.4%) during this period (Supplementary 
Table 9). 
3.2. Cause-specific ED visits one month following flooding 
Comparing the difference in the average rate of ED visits between the 
control period and the acute post-flood phase (the month after flooding), 
ED visits related to diarrhea were increased by 15% (95% CI: 4%–27%) 
among the flooded ZCTAs with respect to non-flooded ZCTAs (Fig. 4). 
This increase was observed among both moderately flooded as well as 
highly flooded ZCTAs (Fig. 4). ED visits related to diarrheal symptoms 
were increased among children (0–5 years) (RR = 1.16; 95% CI: 
1.02–1.32), females (RR = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.10–1.42) and the risk was 
also elevated among White (RR = 1.16; 95% CI: 1.03–1.31), and nonHispanic patients (RR = 1.19; 95% CI: 1.04–1.37) (Fig. 4). No effect 
modification was observed by age, race, or sex for the above ED visits 
(Supplementary Table 8). Comparing the moderate and highly flooded 
ZCTAs separately to the non-flooded ZCTAs showed that pregnancyrelated ED visits were increased by 10% (95% CI: 4%–17%) in the 
highly flooded ZCTAs while asthma was increased by 9% (95% CI: 
0.2%–18%) among the moderately flooded ZCTAs (Fig. 4). ED visits 
related to asthma among men showed a slight increase of 9% (95% CI: 
1%–17%) in flooded ZCTAs compared to the non-flooded ZCTAs (Fig. 4). 
Cardiovascular disease-related ED visits were heightened (RR = 1.11; 
95% CI: 1.03–1.20) among patients of age group 18–50 years (Fig. 4). 
Risk for insect bite related ED visits was elevated only among residents 
from the highly flooded ZCTAs (RR = 1.12; 95% CI: 1.00–1.24). The 
association between flooding and insect bite-related ED visits was 
strongest among adults older than 17 years of age (RR = 1.42; 95% CI: 
1.15–1.74) (Fig. 4). The elevated risk was also statistically significant 
among persons identifying as non-Hispanic (RR = 1.17; 95% CI: 
1.03–1.34) but not for those identifying as Hispanic (RR = 0.94; 95% CI: 
0.79–1.13)(Fig. 4). Dehydration-related ED visits during the acute postflood phase among patients of age 0–5 and 6–17 from the flooded ZCTAs 
were increased by 37% (95% CI: 8%–73%) and 45% (95% CI: 9%–92%), 
respectively. Dehydration related ED visits was also increased by 31% 
(95% CI: 1%–69%) among non-White and non-Black/African-American 
patients from the flooded ZCTAs compared to the non-flooded ZCTAs; 
but this was not statistically different from that of White patients (pvalue = 0.17) (Fig. 4).Effect modification by age was observed for ED 
visits related to insect bite, cardiovascular diseases, and dehydration. 
The risk for dehydration was heightened among patients of age 0–5 and 
6–17 compared to patients of age 18–50 (p-values ≤ 0.023), while risk

3.3. Cause-specific ED visits two months following acute post-flood phase 
During the protracted post-flood phase (2 months following the acute 
post-flood phase: November 2 - December 30), overall ED visits related 
to insect bite, diarrhea, and asthma were increased in the flooded ZCTAs 
compared to the non-flooded ZCTAs (Fig. 5). The increase in insect bite 
related ED visits was 38% (95% CI: 13%–69%) among age group 6–17 
years of age, 30% (95% CI: 9%–54%) among non-Hispanic, 27% (95% 
CI: 7%–52%) among male patients, 19% (95% CI: 4%–37%) among 
White patients and 30% (2%–67%) among patients recorded as 
belonging to non-White and non-Black/African-American race groups 
(Fig. 5). No significant effect modification was observed (Supplementary 
Table 8). The overall increase in ED visits related to insect bite was more 
pronounced among the moderately flooded ZCTAs 29% (95% CI: 12%– 
49%) compared to the highly flooded ZCTAs (Fig. 5). The risk for 
diarrhea related ED visits were elevated for patients identifying as nonHispanic (RR = 1.24; 95% CI: 1.08–1.43), White (RR = 1.13; 95% CI: 
1.01–1.27), female (RR = 1.18; 95% CI: 1.04–1.33), and of age group 
18–64 years of age (RR = 1.23; 95% CI: 1.03–1.47); risk for asthma was 
elevated among both patients Hispanic (1.09; 95% CI: 1.01–1.17) and 
non-Hispanic (RR = 1.13; 95% CI: 1.06–1.20), patients identifying as 
White (RR = 1.13; 95% CI: 1.07–1.21), males (RR = 1.15; 95% CI: 
1.07–1.23), and patients age groups 6–17 (RR = 1.14; 95% CI: 
1.07–1.21) and 0–5 (RR = 1.09; 95% CI: 1.00–1.20)(Fig. 5).Effect 
modification by age was observed for asthma (p-value = 0.02) while 
effect modification by race, sex and ethnicity were non-significant for 
both asthma and diarrhea related ED visits. The overall as well as 
stratified RRs for dehydration, cardiovascular, and pregnancy related 
ED visits did not show any increase or decrease during this protracted 
phase except for a 15% (95% CI: 2%–26%) decline in ED visits related to 
dehydration among patients older than 64 years of age (Fig. 5). Effect 
modification by age was significant for dehydration (p-value = 0.04), 
with increased risk among patients of age 6–17 compared to patients of 
age 18 - 15 (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table 8). The risk for ED visits related 
to cardiovascular disease was heightened among patients recorded as 
Non-Hispanic compared to Hispanic (p-value = 0.041), and the risk for 
ED visits related to dehydration was heightened among patients reported as non-White and non-Black/African-American compared to 
White (p-value = 0.032). On average, one percentage increase in ZCTA 
flooded was associated with 3% (1%–5.1%) increase in ED visits related 
to insect bite during the protracted phase (Supplementary Table 9). 
The results of the sensitivity analysis performed by removing control 
ED visits that occurred during flooding in June 2019 and one month 
after are presented in Supplementary Figure 1. The rate ratios for causespecific ED visits between flooded and non-flooded ZCTAs were similar 
to that of the main analysis and did not show any significant changes in 
ED visits other than those presented in the main analysis. 
4. Discussion 
In this study we have shown the usefulness of combining SyS with 
earth observations for detection of health risks that might escalate after 
flooding. The near-real time availability of both SyS data and earth 
observations can aid in recovery measures, targeting populations who 
would likely experience elevated health risks during and following 
flooding. The analysis of demographic-stratified risks associated with 
flooding measured at ZCTA level and the investigation of effect modification of this risk by the patient’s demographics is a unique addition of 
this study to the literature. 
We found that ED visits related to pregnancy, insect bites, diarrhea, 
dehydration, cardiovascular diseases, and asthma were elevated during 
or following the landfall of Tropical Storm Imelda. Other studies that 
used health records to understand flooding related health outcomes 
reported similar increases in diarrhea, insect bite, and pregnancy complications. (Ramesh et al., 2021; Hetherington et al., 2021; Liu et al., 
2016; D. Saulnier et al., 2018a; Xiao et al., 2019). ED visits for these 
causes were elevated among at least one of the strata during the month 
following the floods while only asthma and insect bite were elevated 
during the floods, and the protracted phase was marked with increased 
visits for asthma, insect bites, and diarrhea. No differences in 
heat-related illness was found during and following flooding in the 
present study (Supplementary Table 6). Few studies that have analyzed 
the association between flooding and heat-related illness; however, one 
study pointed out reports on heat-related illness among relief workers 
involved in sandbagging activities after the 1993 Midwest floods (Dellinger et al., 1996). 
The strongest association between flooding and the different causespecific ED visits was reflected among ED visits related to insect bites 
during the flood period (Fig. 3). We observed an overall increase in insect bite-related ED visits during the protracted phase which coincides 
with the findings of another study that showed an 120% increase in

arthropod bites one month after the impact of Hurricane Floyd in North 
Carolina (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC and others), 
2000). Increases in ED visits related to insect bites have also been reported after the flooding caused by Hurricane Hugo in North Carolina 
and Hurricane Harvey in Texas (Ramesh et al., 2021; Bandino et al., 
2015). The potential reason for the increased cases of insect bite after 
flooding could be due to movement of insects away from damaged 
habitat and/or fleeing their natural hosts such as birds and rodents 
(Bandino et al., 2015; Dayrit et al., 2018; Tempark et al., 2013). In case 
of Imelda the floods inundated 961 sq km of land area and damaged 10, 
878 homes in Texas which could have caused significant changes in 
insect habitats (Latto and Berg, 2019). In addition, cleanup activities 
bring humans closer to affected insect habitats such as downed trees, 
which may also increase the probability of getting bitten by insects after 
flooding. This follows our finding where during the acute phase, the 
increase in ED visits related to insect bite was greater among adults older 
than 17 years (compared to age groups 6–17 and 0–5 years of age), who 
are more likely to be involved in cleanup activities. 
We found an overall increase in ED visits for diarrheal disease during 
the month following flooding as well as during the protracted phase. The 
risk was elevated particularly among females and people aged 18–64 
during the protracted period. In coherence with this result, a study done 
in Huaihua, China analyzing dysentery cases over a six-year period 
showed that dysentery was elevated for three weeks following flooding 
and was higher in females and people aged 15–64 (Liu et al., 2016). In 
contrast, the risk for diarrhea was found to be similar among males and 
females following the floods caused by heavy rains of 2016 in Anhui, 
China (N. Zhang et al., 2019). In a review of studies published to date, 
76% of studies found a positive association between flooding and diarrheal disease, and none reported a negative association (Levy et al., 
2016). However, there is heterogeneity across studies. For example, a 
pooled estimate calculated from 14 articles estimated a central RR for 
diarrheal diseases during and following flooding as 1.64 (95% CI: 
0.928–2.88) (Kraay et al., 2020). Some of the common reasons for association pointed out in the literature are contamination of drinking 
water, failure of treatment plants and supply lines, transport of pathogens from excreta in soil to surface waters, consumption of contaminated food, direct contact with polluted flood waters, lack of resources 
for hygienic sanitation practices in shelters or evacuated places, inundated latrines, inundated wells, and crowding at shelters (Kraay et al., 
2020; Phung et al., 2017; D. Saulnier et al., 2018a; N. Zhang et al., 
2019). Following Imelda, wastewater treatment plants and other critical 
sanitary sewer infrastructure were damaged in the New Port district in 
Harris county, which could have contributed to risk for diarrheal outcomes (Newport Municipal Utility District, 2021). Also, Imelda caused 
large scale power outages, which could have affected the operation of 
water treatment plants in the study area (Entergy Newsroom, 2019). 
Evidence of drinking water contamination by Shigella, salmonella, 
cholera, Escherichia coli, and rotavirus following flooding has been 
presented in various studies (CDC, 2012; Levy et al., 2016; F. Zhang 
et al., 2016). Dose response relationships between flooding and diarrheal outcomes has also been found to be positive with respect to increase in depth of flood water, direct contact with flood water, and levels 
of water insecurity (Kraay et al., 2020; Rosinger, 2018). The biological 
pathway between flooding and diarrheal diseases is discussed elaborately by Levy et al. (2016). 
Disruption of access to clean drinking water following flooding may 
also lead to increased dehydration outcomes among the flooded population. A study done after the historic 2014 Bolivia floods showed 21 
times higher odds for dehydration among children (aged 2–12) with 
medium to high household water insecurity compared to low water 
insecurity (Rosinger, 2018). In the current study, though we did not 
detect increased overall risk for dehydration related ED visits, we 
observed an elevated risk among children (ages 0–5) and adolescents 
(ages 6–17) which was also significantly greater than the risk among 
adults (ages 18–50). Increased inpatient hospitalization for dehydration 
was also reported two weeks following Hurricane Sandy in comparison 
to the same period in the four previous years, but in this case, the majority of the increase was reflected among patients older than 65 years of 
age (Swerdel et al., 2016). 
Pregnancy-related ED visits were increased by 10% during the month 
following flooding and the increase was reflected only among the highly 
flooded ZCTAs. As the ESSENCE definitions used for filtering these records cannot distinguish between pregnancy, pregnancy loss, and delivery but filters all records of patients who were pregnant during the 
emergency visit, the increase in this group of ED visits may portray 
increased health-seeking behavior for pregnant people from the highly 
flooded ZCTAs. One of the potential mediators for increased pregnancyrelated ED visits is inadequate prenatal care during and following such 
flood-related events due to disruption in health services. For example, a 
medical center in Chambers County was evacuated due to flooding of the 
facility following Imelda (Latto and Berg, 2019). The floods also inundated many roads in the study region interrupting ground transportation 
(Latto and Berg, 2019). Studies have shown increased inadequacy in 
prenatal care following Hurricanes Katrina and Michael (Harville et al., 
2010; Pan et al., 2021), with delay in prenatal care initiation as a partial 
mediator for the association between hurricane and small for gestational 
age (Pan et al., 2021). Increases in ED visits related to pregnancy complications following Hurricanes Harvey, Katrina, and Sandy have also 
been noted in previous studies (Ramesh et al., 2021; Oni et al., 2015; 
Xiao et al., 2019). Studies have showed increases in low birth weight 
following flooding of the Red River, North Dakota in 1997 and 2009 
(Hilmert et al., 2016; Tong et al., 2011) with the study on 2009 floods 
showing significant decreases in birth weight with increasing proximity 
of the maternal residence to the floods. Though there is not a clear 
explanation about the pathway of association between flooding and 
pregnancy complications, accidental injury has been pointed out as a 
potential reason for threatened abortion while mental stress and 
depression may exacerbate gestational diabetes and pregnancy-induced 
hypertension (PIH), or induce preterm labor (Oni et al., 2015; Tong 
et al., 2011; Xiao et al., 2019). Changes in mental health status following 
tropical storm Imelda was reported in the Texas Flood Registry, where 
30% of survey respondents reported mental or emotional changes due to 
the impact of the storm (CEHI, 2020). 
Increases in ED visits for cardiovascular disease (CVD) related outcomes or symptoms also are related to mental and psychosocial stress 
induced by flooding (Becquart et al., 2019; Vanasse et al., 2016). 
Decreased access to medication and support systems during as well as 
following flooding may also contribute to increases in CVD-related 
outcomes (Becquart et al., 2019). We observed an 11% increase in 
cardiovascular disease related ED visits after the flood period among 
adults (ages 18–50) and, similar to our findings, Weinberger et al. 
(2021) have reported an overall 10% (2–19%) increase in cardiovascular disease-related ED visits following landfall of Hurricane Sandy in 
the state of New York. Studies that analyzed changes in CVD-related 
health visits between flooded and non-flooded or, pre-flood and 
post-flood populations have also shown increased CVD visits (Li et al., 
2015; Vanasse et al., 2016). While we did not see an increase in ED visits 
related to CVD in patients older than 65 years, CVD hospitalizations 
were increased in Orleans Parish, Louisiana on the sixth day after the 
landfall of Hurricane Katrina in patients 65 years of age and older 
(Becquart et al., 2019). 
Asthma-related ED visits were elevated in the moderately flooded 
ZCTAs compared to the non-flooded ZCTAs during both the acute and 
protracted post-flood phase. During the protracted post-flood period the 
risks among adolescents (ages 6–17) were significantly greater than 
adults. Association between flooding and respiratory syndrome as well 
as mold growth and respiratory symptoms among children and adolescents after Hurricane Katrina has been reported (Rath et al., 2007). One 
of the potential pathways explaining the association between flooding 
and asthma is growth of flood-related molds or home dampness (Azuma 
et al., 2014; D’Amato et al., 2020; Quinn et al., 1994). Increased mold

growth in flooded houses compared to non-flooded houses has been 
documented in several studies (Azuma et al., 2014; Rose and 
Akpinar-Elci, 2013; Zock et al., 2002); and the Institute of Medicine 
(IOM) report on health impacts of indoor dampness affirms suggestive 
evidence for association between dampness and asthma symptoms in 
sensitized persons (Institute of Medicine (IOM), 2004). As Imelda led to 
flooding of over 10,000 houses, several of the flooded houses may have 
experienced mold growth. Adding evidence to this, outreach teams have 
reported helping in mold removal in flooded houses in Jefferson, 
Chambers and Orange counties following Imelda (All Hands and Hearts, 
2019). Atmospheric molds and pollens outdoors are other potential 
pathways for exacerbation of asthma following flooding (D’Amato et al., 
2020). 
Risk ratios for asthma and insect bites were higher in moderately 
versus highly flooded areas in post flood periods (Supplementary Table 
7). Different re-habitation patterns based on flooding severity, the more 
immediate need for remediation and repair in highly flooded areas, or 
differences in flooding leading to intermediate causes that take longer to 
manifest in moderately flooded versus highly flooded areas are possible 
reasons for these results; however the data available did not allow for 
exploration of these potential explanations. In stratified results 
(Figs. 3–5), some of the risk ratio point estimates for patients identifying 
as White or patients identifying as non-Hispanic were higher than for 
patients identifying as Hispanic, Black or African American, or the 
combined group including patients identifying as Asian, American Indian, Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, Arab, Middle Eastern or North 
African. However, statistical tests for effect modification by race or 
ethnicity were largely non-significant. As the majority of the ED visits 
(62%) were of patients identifying as White, we had greater power to 
detect an effect of flooding for patients identifying as White, as indicated 
by the differences in confidence intervals in Figs. 3–5. These results 
suggest additional studies to determine whether there are meaningful 
differences and if so, whether they could reflect differences in evacuation and healthcare seeking behaviors, among other potential 
explanations. 
Though this study was able to delineate health risks following the 
flooding caused by the Tropical Storm Imelda with the help of remotely 
observed flooding and ED visits from the SyS, the study has some limitations. First, the method for filtering cause-specific ED visits using 
definitions does not exactly match the process in the ESSENCE system, 
which relies on proprietary compilers to process the raw text before 
categorizing records into syndromes and subsyndromes. In addition, 
inaccuracies in definitions for capturing cause-specific ED visits exist 
(Kumar and White, 2018). Also, our exposure estimation method does 
not allow for daily resolved estimates of flooding within affected ZCTAs, 
as ZCTAs are classified as flooded using the maximum observed inundation product, whereas flood waters would have likely risen and 
receded at different timepoints across the affected ZCTAs. Using daily 
flood maps instead of the maximum observed flood map would be 
helpful to overcome this limitation (Galantowicz John et al., 2017). Due 
to constraints in data availability, the control period used in this study 
did not incorporate the months of flood and post-flood period (September–December) in the previous years. As a result, this analysis was 
controlled for weekly trends, not for monthly or seasonal trends. 
5. Conclusion 
This study shows that flooding after heavy rains precipitated by 
Tropical Storm Imelda was associated with increased ED visits related to 
asthma and insect bites during the flood period and increased ED visits 
related to pregnancy, dehydration, asthma, insect bites, cardiovascular 
diseases, and diarrheal outcomes during post-flood periods. Remotelysensed inundation products merged with SyS data were helpful in 
defining flood exposure within residential ZCTAs and evaluation of 
health risks during and following the floods. Further analysis by demographic characteristics of patients allowed for estimation of 
differential risks experienced across age, race, and ethnicity categories. 
Current remotely-sensed inundation products are available within days 
following flooding (Galantowicz et al., 2018; Shen et al., 2019) and may 
be helpful in identification of the affected communities quickly, and 
merging this data within SyS will help to identify anomalies in ED visits 
in near real-time, which may aid disaster management authorities in 
mitigating health outcomes associated with flooding by data-driven 
deployment of medical resources and services.
