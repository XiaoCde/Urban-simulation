A B S T R A C T
Driving into flood water is the leading cause of death during a flood in the UK yet represents an 
unexplored area of research both nationally and internationally. This paper reports on the find­
ings of two quantitative studies. The first a nationwide survey (N = 185), which sought to 
determine driver opinions, attitudes and understanding regarding road flooding, including 
perception of water depth and water speed. This was followed by experiments to determine the 
relative saliency of dynamic versus static forms of 20 common UK road signs through eye tracking 
and memory recall tasks (N = 44). This study examines driver's misconceptions of road flooding, 
lack of adherence to road flooding signage and tests the relative effectiveness of dynamic signage 
and its relationship to driving experience. The study highlights that traditional flood signage is 
ineffective – prompting poorer memory recall, whilst dynamic signage demonstrated higher 
memory recall across all signage tested, with dynamic flooding signage being significantly more 
effective than the static equivalent for across all demographics.
1. Introduction
Climate change is leading to greater incidents of flooding globally (Gissing et al., 2023; Hiramoto et al., 2025), causing changes in 
storm patterns (Suarez et al., 2005) and rainfall intensity (Evans et al., 2024) throughout the UK, affecting river levels and flows 
(Hannaford & Marsh, 2008; Prosdocimi et al., 2014; Robins & Lewis, 2019). Whilst there has been a significant focus on the effects of 
flooding to housing stock (Sayers et al., 2018), the effect on transportation infrastructure and systems is given far less focus and re­
quires addressing (Suarez et al., 2005).
Driving through flood water is the leading cause of death during a flood in the UK (Environment Agency, 2019) and high income 
countries worldwide particularly Australia and the US (Evans et al., 2024; Gissing et al., 2023; Panakkal & Padgett, 2024). Just 30 cm 
of water can float a small car with only 50 cm required to float a large 4 × 4 (Kramer et al., 2016; Martínez-Gomariz et al., 2017; 
Pregnolato et al., 2017). Once a vehicle begins to float, there is the possibility that it will be carried downstream (Evans et al., 2024;

and drowning of occupants (Drobot et al., 2007; Evans et al., 2024; Shah et al., 2018; Versini et al., 2010). Despite this, the dangers of 
driving through flood water are not widely recognised amongst the public. Key reasons cited for drivers entering flood water are a lack 
of awareness of the dangers (Franklin et al., 2014) or that existing warnings are not taken seriously (Drobot et al., 2007; Shah et al., 
2018). In the UK both possibilities are likely, due to the absence of flood-related information in the UK Highway Code (HR Wallingford, 
Flood Hazard Research Centre and Analysts, R., and P, 2006; The Department for Transport, 2020), and a lack of focus in government 
led UK road safety campaigns (HR Wallingford, Flood Hazard Research Centre and Analysts, R., and P, 2006), which typically feature 
more prominent issues such as drink driving, the wearing of seatbelts and awareness of motorcycles. This is in contrast with other 
countries such as the USA and Australia, who specifically target road flooding through learner handbooks (Hamilton et al., 2023) and 
driver awareness campaigns (Drobot et al., 2007; Gissing et al., 2016). With the absence of driver instruction and awareness cam­
paigns, the sole strategy implemented in the UK to minimise road flood incidents is through the use of road signage for which there has 
been very little research into the effectiveness especially within a UK context.
Existing literature concerning road flooding signage (Ahmed et al., 2020; Higgins et al., 2012) and drivers' responses to road 
flooding is predominantly from an Australian (Gissing et al., 2016; Haynes et al., 2017), or North American (Drobot et al., 2007; 
Higgins et al., 2012) perspective, with a literature review noting a distinct lack of any European studies (Ahmed et al., 2018). While 
there is an distinct absence of UK-specific literature on the effectiveness of flooding signage, Australian (Ahmed et al., 2020; Gissing 
et al., 2016) and Malaysian (Shah et al., 2018) researchers have reported existing road signage to be ineffective. This paper seeks to 
address this literature gap by exploring drivers' reactions to flooding situations and the current forms of flooding signage in the UK to 
understand their adherence (or lack of) to road signage. The study also considers the potential for dynamic forms of signage to increase 
adherence, by comparing the effectiveness of a range of dynamic and static road signs. The research is presented through two studies as 
part of a wider project involving field trials of a prototype dynamic sign and flooding detection system at a well-known national road 
flooding hotspot (Smith, 2018).
1.1. Key hypotheses
These two studies sought to test three key hypotheses:
First, we hypothesised that demographic factors play a key role in the decisions around whether to enter flood water, with male 
drivers often found more likely to be involved than females to enter flood water (Ahmed et al., 2020; Diakakis & Deligiannakis, 2013; 
Hamilton et al., 2018; Haynes et al., 2017; Smith, 2018). Other demographic factors include location familiarity, with drivers residing 
in rural areas (Diakakis & Deligiannakis, 2013; Gissing et al., 2019) being more willing to enter flooded locations, and the type, size 
and ownership mode of vehicle driven. Drivers of 4 × 4's are reportedly more likely to enter flood water (Ahmed et al., 2020; Coles, 
2008; Gissing et al., 2016; Hamilton et al., 2018; Haynes et al., 2017).
Secondly, we questioned whether drivers are less likely to react to flooding ‘warning’ signage than other mandatory forms of 
signage such as speed limits. In the UK, warning signage is typically triangular, whilst signage that gives orders and is considered 
mandatory consists is circular, with rectangular forms for information and directional signage (The Department for Transport, 2020).
However, there is a wide spectrum of warning signage, giving advance warning of a variety of road features, such as junctions, and 
potential hazards such as flooding, wildlife, or potential rock falls. Whilst flooding signage is given as a warning rather than an order, 
the consequences of not heeding the warning could have similar risk outcomes to disregarding mandatory speed and stop signs for 
example.
Our third hypothesis predicts that dynamic forms of signage utilising LEDs should be more effective at evoking adherence than 
traditional static screen-printed aluminium signage. It is proposed that dynamic signage could be considered more visually salient

2. Methodology
A range of quantitative methods were employed as highlighted in fig. 1. The first study consists of an online survey that sought to 
measure existing understanding, attitudes, adherence toward road flooding and signage, and drivers' willingness to drive through flood 
water. This was complemented by a second study that utilised eye tracking experiments and memory recall tests to determine the 
effectiveness of signage, comparing static signage to comparable dynamic versions.
2.1. Experiment 1 Online Questionnaire
An electronic questionnaire was developed via Qualtrics, an online platform and was advertised through a variety of social media 
channels. The objective of the questionnaire was to examine the perception and attitudes of drivers regarding static and dynamic traffic 
signage and road flooding scenarios. The survey was structured in four parts: demographic questions, perceptions of static and dy­
namic flood signage, perceptions of flooded roads, and attitudes to different flooded road scenarios displayed in photographs and video 
clips. Quantitative data was analysed using SPSS, while qualitative data was analysed in NVivo following a content analysis approach. 
This involved systematically examining participants' open-ended responses to identify recurring themes and patterns, enabling 
qualitative insights to be transformed into quantifiable data. This research was approved by the Ethics Committee at Nottingham Trent 
University (ID 1627983). The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to the ethical 
guidelines of Nottingham Trent University for research integrity and health and safety. All participants were informed about the aim 
and procedure of the study and were asked to provide consent before taking part in the study. No deception was involved, and par­
ticipants were debriefed after their participation.
2.2. Participants
In total, 414 respondents undertook the questionnaire, which was reduced to 185 responses (93 males, 92 females) after removing 
incomplete and non-UK responses. Respondents had an average age of 40.6 years (SD = 15.9), ranging from 19 to 81 years old, 
Appendix 1 shows our data representativeness compared to DVLA license holder data for the same period. These respondents had an 
average of 13.9 (SD 4.74) years' driving experience since passing their test and drove between 5001 and 10,000 miles per year, or 
101–200 miles per week reporting a greater proportion average of 52% of leisure miles travelled. The majority (83.7%) stated that they 
fully owned their car, whilst 10.8% reported driving a leased/ PCP car, followed by 2.1%, 1.8% and 1.6% who reported using company 
cars, hire cars, or cars belonging to a family member. Participants were asked the type of vehicle they most frequently drive; the highest 
percentage (25.95%) reported a supermini such as a Ford Fiesta or similar while the least represented vehicle was a luxury saloon such 
as a Mercedes S class or similar (0.54%). SUV/4 × 4 drivers made up 14% of the sample.
Using a 5-point Likert rating scale, participants were asked how frequently they drive in the following areas (Table 1).

2.4. Experiment 2 method - eye tracking and memory response
The first study examines demographic factors such as age and driving experience and their relationship with willingness to go 
through the flood and dynamic flood signage. However, due to the survey nature this is limited to observation of static images. 
Therefore, the second study showed participants driving perspective videos encompassing signage to test their responses in a more 
realistic setting using video and eye tracking followed up by a memory recall activity. Additionally, we created a fully operational 
prototype of dynamic flood signage (fig. 6), which was deployed with a water level and flow rate sensing bollard along a flood-prone 
road to assess its efficacy in capturing the attention of drivers. We incorporated this signage into our video footage alongside a breadth 
of conventional road signs in both their static and dynamic forms to explore the survey findings and evaluated the drivers' responses. 
The second experiment also sought to assess the attentional differences of drivers in response to static and dynamic traffic signs to test 
the hypothesis that drivers would react and respond to dynamic signage more rapidly and consistently than static forms.
2.4.1. Design
An experiment was conducted to measure the effect of driving experience (Experienced versus Novice) on gaze behaviour and recall 
ability between the type of road sign (Static versus Dynamic). A mixed design was used with driving experience as a between groups 
factor and type of road sign as a within groups factor. This research was approved by the Ethics Committee at Nottingham Trent 
University (ID 1627983). The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to the ethical 
guidelines of Nottingham Trent University for research integrity and health and safety. All participants were informed about the aim 
and procedure of the study and were asked to provide consent before taking part in the study. No deception was involved, and par­
ticipants were debriefed after their participation.
2.4.2. Participants
A sample of forty-four drivers comprising of an equal split of male and female participants were assigned into one of two groups. 
This sample size is beyond the 40 threshold recommended in the literature for studies concerning AOI's (Hoogerbrugge et al., 2025). 
The first group consisted of twenty novice drivers, with 3 years or less of driving experience (mean age: 23 years, mean experience: 1.5 
years), whilst the other twenty-four participants were classed as experienced drivers with more than 3 years of driving experience, 
(mean age: 36.7 years old, mean experience: 11 years). All participants were recruited through internal advertisement at Nottingham 
Trent University.

2.4.3. Apparatus and stimuli
Participants were told that they would be watching video clips depicting real on-road footage and would be asked to answer some 
questions at the end, with no false task given. The stimuli consisted of 20 recorded videos clips, depicting matched pairs of 10 common 
road signs (fig. 6) edited from hours of footage captured through routine driving. This footage was filmed using a windscreen mounted 
Go-Pro camera to depict the point of view of the driver and edited using Adobe Premier Pro software. Each video clip was cropped to 
last approximately 50 s, with the 20 clips being split into 10 pairs containing a matched static and dynamic variant of each sign for 
comparison. Each participant was shown all 20 video clips in a randomised sequence. Care was taken to ensure that each sign pair was 
viewable for the same duration.
The stimuli were presented on a computer screen measuring 48.3 × 30.5 cm connected to a SensoMotoric Instruments' Remote Eye- 
tracking Device, sampling at 500 Hz (SMI RED 500). Eye tracking data was collected to provide an insight into participants' attention to 
signage during the presentation of the videos. However, to assess whether participants sufficiently processed the signs shown, they 
were presented a questionnaire immediately after the video presentation on a Lenovo ThinkPad X 260 laptop that assessed their level 
of memory recall of the target signs. The memory test conducted in Qualtrics contained 20 arrays, each containing 4 sign images of 
which one had appeared in the preceding eye tracking phase of the study, while the other 3 signs were distracters. The position of the 
correct sign within the array was randomised across trials. The use of this combined eye tracking and recall approach address criticisms 
in the literature of a sole recall approach, negating potential subconscious reactions to signs (Crundall & Underwood, 2001; Jamson & 
Mrozek, 2017).

3. Results
3.1. Study 1 results
The primary objective in the first section of this study is to examine the direction and strength of associations between key variables 
rather than to establish causal relationships. Correlation analysis is therefore employed as an appropriate exploratory technique to 
identify potential relationships that may warrant further investigation Pearson correlations were undertaken to investigate the re­
lationships between the demographic variables and drivers' willingness to drive through a flooded road or a ford. The average score for 
willingness to drive through flood waters was (M: 93.64) across the whole sample, ranging from 48 to 119, out of a potential score of 
120 derived from the questions posed for the three scenarios described in section 2.1.2. The correlations shown in full in Appendix 2 
show a positive relationship between variables of age (r = 0.342, N = 183, p < .001), driving license period (r = 0.276, N = 185, p <
.001) and likelihood of driving through floodwaters. As the age and driving license period increases, the probability of driving though 
water increases too, suggesting that experienced and older drivers are more willing to go through water compared to novice drivers. No 
meaningful relationships were found between demographic variables of sex, miles driven, leisure mileage, ownership of the car, type of 
the car, prior experience of driving through flood water or driven locations (urban, rural, suburban, motorways) with willingness to 
drive through flood waters (all P > .05).
For the STOP sign, drivers were asked to select one response from three potential actions. Of the total sample, 87.57% of drivers 
reported adherence and selected “I would come to a complete stop”, whilst 10.27% selected “I would slow down but proceed if they thought 
it safe to do so” (i.e., treating the sign more as a ‘Give Way’ sign). The remaining 2.16% of respondents selected “I would turn around to 
find another way if it is easy to do so”, which suggests that these participants misunderstood either the sign or the question.
For the 30-mph sign, drivers were given four responses to choose from. Of the total sample, 48.65% of the sample reported that they 
“would not drive above this speed”, 35.14% stated they “would drive at this speed” (i.e., viewing the speed sign as a target speed rather 
than a maximum limit). Of the remaining sample, 12.43% stated they would “reduce my speed to this level if a speed camera is present” 
while 3.78% stated that they would “treat the sign as an advisory speed limit”. These results for the speed sign suggest a slightly lower 
compliance than the STOP sign but are close in respect of overall compliance. Whilst these figures for the mandatory static signs don't 
suggest 100% adherence particularly in relation to the speed sign, it does suggest a relatively high level of compliance amongst the 
sample, with only 12.4–16.2% demonstrating violating behaviour.
A comparison of responses to the three flood signs, suggested that the dynamic (M = 3.20, SD = 0.78) and hybrid flood signs (M =
3.29, SD = 0.67) in comparison to the static flood sign (M = 2.87, SD = 0.69) elicited a far higher incidence of drivers electing to turn 
around and a lower incidence of decisions based on the actions of other drivers. A one-way within subjects ANOVA was conducted to 
compare the effect of sign type on driver adherence in static, dynamic and hybrid sign scenarios. The result showed that mean sign 
scores differed significantly across these three scenarios, F (2, 368) = 30.354, p < .001, η2
p = 0.142. Furthermore, a post hoc pairwise 
comparison using the Bonferroni correction indicated a significant difference between the dynamic and hybrid signs versus the static 
sign (p < .001), but no significant differences were found between the dynamic and the hybrid sign (p > .001). Whilst both the static 
and dynamic signs represent identical information in a warning-sign format, the dynamic sign is reportedly treated with more 
adherence than the static sign. The hybrid sign also ostensibly receives more credibility from respondents, despite it being informa­
tional in shape.
These results shown in fig. 7 align with the inferential analysis in table 2, which examined the relationships between the three types 
of flood signage and the willingness to drive through flood water. There is a statistically significant positive relationship between the 
static flood signage and willingness to drive through flood water (r = 0.316, p < .001), suggesting that where signage is present static 
flood signage is the least effective at reducing the willingness to drive through floodwater.
Though our general measure of willingness to drive through flood waters correlated with willingness to ignore a static flood sign, 
this was not the case with the dynamic and hybrid signs. This suggests that only the dynamic and hybrid signs moderate pre-existing 
tendencies to drive through water.

Participants were asked when approaching a flooded road or ford on a 30mph versus a 50mph road what speed they would aim for 10 
m before the water and what speed they would aim for when in the water by entering a numerical value in an open box for which no 
suggestions were given, the results of which are shown below in fig. 8.
The hypothesis was that drivers would adjust their speed by a similar relative amount on both roads rather than to an absolute 
figure suggesting that on a 50mph road they would drive more rapidly through water than on a 30mph road. However, fig. 8 suggests 
this not to be the case. A 2 × 2 within subject Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was conducted on participants' speed responses across the 
variables of speed limit (30 mph vs. 50mph) and point in the drive (on approach vs. driving through the flood water) on participants 
speed ratings. This identified a significant main effect of timing (F (1,180) =199.906, MSe = 19.559, p < .001) with participants rating 
their speed as significantly higher before the flood (M = 13.03 mph) than during the flood (M = 8.38 mph). Furthermore, a significant 
interaction was found between road speed and timing (F (1,180) =199.906, MSe = 0.000, p < .001). However, no significant difference 
(P > .05) is seen between scenarios of 30mph road (Mean before water = 13.03, Mean during water = 8.38) versus 50mph roads (Mean 
before water = 12.85, Mean during water = 8.20).

3.2. Study 2 results
The eye tracking data was initially processed and classified using SMI BeGaze (Version 3.6.52), and then analysed in RStudio 
(2024.09 Build 394) using the lme4 (Bates et al., 2015), lmeTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017) and report (Makowski et al., 2023) packages. 
Plots were created using the ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) package. A series of descriptive and inferential analyses were conducted for Sign 
Type (static and dynamic) as a within groups measure and Driver Group (novices and experienced) as between groups factors for four 
dependent variables: fixation count, fixation dwell time, time to first fixation and memory recall. We consider fixation count as the 
proportion of times that participants attended to the parts of the scene defined as an AOI (i.e., the sign). The fixation duration time 
concerns the amount of time in which drivers held their focus on a particular AOI for an extended period of time, calculated using SMI 
BeGaze's event detection algorithm for fixation classification. Time to First Fixation referred to the difference between the onset of an 
AOI (e.g., the appearance of a sign) and the moment when the participant first fixates on that AOI, indicating how quickly attention 
was directed to that specific region of interest. Finally, the memory recall test measured the number of correct signs participants were 
able to recall. By combining these eye tracking measures with the recall scores, we were able to assess whether drivers could process 
the different sign types and how long each sign drew their attention for.
3.2.1. Number of fixations on sign AOI
A Chi square test of independence found there was a significant difference between the number of times participants fixated on 
Dynamic and Static signs (Х2 = 42.46, df = 9, p < .001), with a higher number of fixations on Static signs.
Chi square Goodness of fit tests were conducted for each of the individual Sign types, revealing that there were significant dif­
ferences for the Speed limit 50 (Х2 = 15.87, df = 1, p < .001), Flooding (Х2 = 4.91, df = 1, p = .03) and National Speed (Х2 = 17.31, df 
= 1, p < .001) signs. (See Fig. 11).
A follow up Chi square test of independence found there was a significant difference between the number of times participants 
fixated on Dynamic and Static signs for each Driver Group (Х2 = 45.84, df = 27, p = .013). However, post hoc comparisons found no 
significant differences between the number of fixations for each Driver Experience Group on each Sign type (see Fig. 12).
3.2.2. Fixation duration
Fig. 13 shows the average fixation duration for each Driver Group on the different Sign Types, calculated from 745 total fixations 
during the period when the sign AOI was active during each trial. Due to the non-normal distribution of the data, fixation duration was 
z-scored and scores above 3 standard deviations away from the mean were removed as outliers, leaving 651 fixations for analysis.
A 2 × 2 mixed design ANOVA with Driver Group as a between subjects' factor and Sign Type as a within subjects' factor found no 
significant differences between average fixation duration for the effect of Sign Type (p = .9), Driver Group (p = .71), nor any inter­
action (p = .4).
To analyse whether the length of time the sign was visible had any effect on dwell time of participants, a linear mixed effects model 
with Driver Group and Sign Type and AOI Length as main effects was fitted, and Participants and Trial Number as random effects. This 
model was compared to a null model where all variables were fitted as random effects and was found to explain a significantly greater 
proportion of the variance (Null Model AIC = 8955.2.1, BIC = 8991.0; Mixed Effects Model AIC = 8951.5, BIC = 9000.8; p = .02). 
However, there were still no significant differences (see fig. 13) between average fixation durations for Driver Type (p = .92) or Sign 
Type (p = .42), nor any interaction effects (Sign Type x Driver Group: p = .387, Driver Group x AOI Length, p = .74, Sign Type x AOI Length: 
p = .266, Full Interaction: p = .3).

4. Discussion
Hypothesis one raised mixed findings in the demographic's response to flood waters. The study found that young drivers are less 
likely to drive through flood waters. This finding agrees in part with findings from accident fatality data in a Greek (Diakakis & 
Deligiannakis, 2013) and an Australian study (Ahmed et al., 2020), but is contrary to the findings of a US study which found that 
18–35 year drivers were more likely to take such risks (Drobot et al., 2007). Whether this is due to cultural differences, a difference in 
methodology or a change in time with the US study being significantly older is unclear. However, research shows that the US typically 
has a lower road safety record than Europe with far higher road fatalities across all variables, population size, vehicle numbers and 
distance travelled (Luoma and Sivak 2014). There was also a lack of a significance in respect to sex, vehicle type, location and response 
to flood, which was surprising as it was expected that male respondents (Ahmed et al., 2020; Diakakis & Deligiannakis, 2013; Hamilton 
et al., 2018; Haynes et al., 2017; Smith, 2018), drivers of 4 × 4 vehicles (Ahmed et al., 2020; Coles, 2008; Gissing et al., 2016; Haynes 
et al., 2017) and those from rural areas (Diakakis & Deligiannakis, 2013; Gissing et al., 2019) would be more likely to drive through 
flood water but this was not found to be the case in our UK study, as neither category showed significance. This difference could be due 
to the predominately US (Coles, 2008) and Australian focus (Ahmed et al., 2020; Gissing et al., 2016; Gissing et al., 2019; Hamilton 
et al., 2018; Haynes et al., 2017) of existing studies, that represent larger geographical and in particular rural distances and both 
countries having a higher proportion of 4 × 4 type vehicles (International Energy Agency, 2020; Martin, 2023) than the UK. The Greek 
study (Diakakis & Deligiannakis, 2013) analysed data from a 40-year period, which affects the ability to compare the findings with the 
more recent studies. The findings related to hypothesis one suggests that no specific UK driver groups need to be targeted, but that 
general awareness is needed across the range of UK drivers regardless of demographics.
Hypothesis two questioned whether drivers are less likely to react to flooding ‘warning’ signage than other mandatory forms of 
signage such as speed limits. The survey found this was the case, with a stark lack of compliance for the static Flood ‘warning sign’ 
when compared to the static ‘mandatory’ signage tested. This lack of adherence amongst drivers is acknowledged in the international 
literature concerning static flood signage (Becker et al., 2015; Gissing et al., 2016; Higgins et al., 2012; Rae et al., 2016) and could be 
attributed to static flood signs remaining deployed and visible beyond a flood state when the risk has passed, an issue that can be 
readily addressed in dynamic signage.
Hypothesis Three predicted that drivers would report greater willingness to believe in the dangers presaged by dynamic signs 
compared to a static sign, which was supported in the survey findings. Driver responses suggest that they would be more likely to seek 
an alternative route when they encounter a dynamic or hybrid flooding sign when compared to a static flooding sign. Furthermore,

fixations compared to the dynamic. These were motorway gantry signs that are positioned much higher up than the other standard 
post-based road signs. Further research exploring impact of motorway gantry signs specifically to investigate this difference would be 
useful, considering the positioning, nature of use for variable speed signs and impact of speed and road type to determine whether 
dynamic signs are less effective at higher speeds.
The key finding, however, is that dynamic signage is more memorable with higher recall, despite being fixated upon less. Dynamic 
signs were found to be no more distracting than static signs, there was no difference in the fixation duration, but drivers were better 
able to process the dynamic signs as evidenced through their recall despite fixating on them later than static signs. This suggests that 
dynamic signs are a preferable way to communicate information to drivers, having less of an impact on driver's visual search patterns. 
Particularly the dynamic flooding sign, which had higher recall and attracted a greater number of fixations than the static flood sign. 
This is potentially because static flood signs are placed on roads that are prone to flooding being left in situ 24/7, even when the road is 
no longer flooded (Shah et al., 2018), resulting in drivers distrusting and disregarding their information in most cases. However, 
dynamic flood signs such as the one developed for this study can be actively controlled so that they only illuminate when there is an 
actual presence of a flood, and as such are more salient and reliable to drivers.
Combined with the results from Study 1 where drivers reportedly disregarded static flood signs, these results suggest that using 
Dynamic Flood warnings signs can have a greater impact for drawing the attention of drivers to flooding and thereby lessen the dangers 
of driving through flood water.
5. Limitations and further work
All research conducted in this study assumed optimal clear daylight conditions for the comparison of signage. However, as noted in 
the literature the highest percentage of fatal incidents due to flooding occurred in poor visibility in night, twilight conditions or heavy 
rain conditions (Ahmed et al., 2020; Diakakis & Deligiannakis, 2013; Gissing et al., 2023; Haynes et al., 2017). These are circumstances 
that are likely to be significantly improved by dynamic forms of signage and therefore this should be the focus of future studies. 
Furthermore, as noted there were differences in fixations for higher speed limit signage, particularly motorway gantry signs, which 
would benefit from further research considering sign positioning and consideration of the speed effect which could be conducted more 
effectively within a driving simulator, but was outside the scope of this study.
6. Conclusions
This paper presents new insights in respect to drivers' knowledge, understanding and response to road flooding in the UK an area of 
under explored research internationally. Study one built on the existing international literature by demonstrating a lack of under­
standing amongst the public of the specific risks associated with road flooding. Whilst most respondents highlighted correct depths and 
water entry speed for flooded roads, there was a disconnect in the critical importance of the water flow rate, which is a key factor in 
fatal incidents. Furthermore, the driver's perceived ability to pass through flood water were typically based on perceived rather than 
measured water depth and other driver's reactions to flood, rather than a judgment on the effective water speed or flow rate that could 
be identified through movement or ripples in the water. The survey also found the respondents' ability to differentiate differences in 
water depth against roadside objects was also compromised, which is particularly concerning in instances of flash or pluvial flooding 
that occur in places without water depth gauges like Fords and Bridges do. Existing static flood signage is shown to be ineffective and 
not fit for purpose, often being dismissed by drivers agreeing with the internation literature (Activa, 2014; Ahmed et al., 2020; Gissing 
et al., 2016; Hamilton et al., 2016). It is suspected that the nature of deployment of static flood signage and how timely such signage is 
covered or removed after a flooding event creates mistrust. Due to the limitations of its implementation, typically being displayed 
either permanently in flood prone locations or through the use of temporary or flip-over ‘static’ signage as recognised in the literature 
(Higgins et al., 2012; Shah et al., 2018) and anecdotally through our own research when speaking with drivers. This mistrust of static 
signage could explain the higher willingness amongst respondents in this study to drive through flood water when presented with a 
static compared to dynamic and hybrid signage. Our research suggests that dynamic flood signage addresses these limitations and is 
the preferable and more effective option, being borne out in both the results of the survey, eye tracking and memory recall test. 
Furthermore, dynamic signage is shown to have higher recall across all sign types tested in study 2, compared to their static 
equivalents.
The results of the survey, eye-tracking and memory recall studies clearly demonstrate that the installation of dynamic signage in
