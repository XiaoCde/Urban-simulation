ABSTRACT
Topography and dynamics of real networks' enable network agents to alter their functional behavior.
Theoretical and analytical advancements in network science have furthered our understanding of the effects
of social network characteristics on peer influence and engagement. Influence of social network occurs when
network players alter their decisions and behavior based on others’ influence in the social network which is
evident in different disciplines. Crisis communication networks have significant impact during disasters as people often spread pertinent information in social media due to presence of limited access to conventional information sources. Existing researches in social science and sociology indicate that social networks benefit
spreading warning message and disseminate information about an imminent risk, nevertheless, the existing
studies do not provide enough understanding on how to quantify such influences and how this map into
decision‐making during emergency evacuations. This study provides a robust review of the studies exploring
how individuals are socially influenced, both on‐line and off‐line, while communicating risk during extreme
weather events such as hurricanes. The scope of this review primarily includes studies that look into how
our social networks influence the way we decide to evacuate and how crisis information spread from one agent
to another agent in a network. The insights and findings obtained through this comprehensive review will be
useful to diverse set of stakeholders such as emergency managers, planners, policy makers and practitioners.
These include identifying and implementing targeted strategies for different groups of people in similar crisis
events based on their social network properties, interactions, and activities.

1. Background and motivations
The methodological and analytical advances in social network
research have extended the understanding of the effects of the personal network of individuals on normative social influence and social
engagement. Information from the social network provides better
insights into the thought, skills, values, goals, attitudes, habits, and
relational environment of an individual. Real social networks '
dynamic topology enables their agents to change their practical behavior. Through applying network models and observing the complexities
in the ways network players communicate and change their behavior,
the evolutionary mechanisms of being responsible for the growth of
such networks gained better understanding. In order to develop novel
network modeling techniques, extensive research efforts are required
to understand the basic properties of such networks, replicating similar
properties based on empirical evidence, and effectively design such
networks.
An individuals’ beliefs, emotions, thoughts, opinions, or behaviors
are often affected by others in the social network which is defined as
social influence. In a plentitude of several disciplines such as conformity, socialization, emergency decisions, peer pressure, obedience,
leadership, persuasion, sales, and marketing sectors are often influenced by social factors. Recent studies have shown that individuals
are more biased by their friends as compared to strangers. For an
example, 68% of consumers consult their close friends and family
members before home electronics purchase (Burke, 2003). Social
influence on individuals’ behavior has been the emphasis of travel
behavior and evacuation modeling for a long time. The influence of
an individual’s social network on evacuation decision‐making and crisis
communications is an evolving concept in their respective literature; which
is also the core idea of this study. The overall decision‐making of an individual depends on his/her interaction to the society which is significantly defined by his/her personal constraints. These interactions
with society are determined by an individual's social network. Hence,
the social network has immense influences on the resulting interdependent behavior of the network agents. This study provides an overall
understanding of social network influence on underlying evacuation
behavior and related crisis communications that is currently available
in the evacuation‐modeling literature.
Different types of natural and man‐made disasters are recurrently
experienced by individuals and communities all over the world
(Nelson, 2013). Over the past three decades, disasters triggered by natural hazards alone have perpetrated billions of dollars in property
damage and killed 2.5 million people (Emergency Events Database,
0000; Klomp and Valckx, 2014; Roy et al., 2020). Although large‐
scale disasters (2010 Haiti earthquake and 2009 Italy earthquake for
example), have received substantial media coverage, small‐scale local
disasters such as floods, typhoons, hurricanes, tornadoes, wildfires and
mudslides (among others) also need to be acknowledged that killed
thousands of people as well (Hoyois et al., 2007). On Increasing
National Resilience, the National Academies Committee works
towards Hazards and Disaster which recognized disaster resilience as
a national authoritative at different levels (individual, local, interstate,
and national). Enhanced policy requirements by the government have
been suggested by this imperative that would enable people to rely less
on governmental support while responding to such disasters (Cutter
et al., 2013). Disaster resilience received more emphasis related to
functioning and operations of physical infrastructure systems (Cutter
et al., 2013), however, this should include more social factors and considerations (Sadri et al., 2016b).
To promote disaster resilience minimizing the adverse impacts of
disasters, communities need to be proactive with sufficient preparation
(Diffenbaugh and Field, 2013; Guikema, 2009; Kennedy, 2002;
Mortula et al., 2020; Press and Hamilton, 1999). Information spreading effectively establishes the basics to increase the awareness to every
individual in a community (Cutter and Finch, 2008; Helbing, 2013;
Vespignani, 2009). Organized planning, collection, systems, and delivery techniques are required for this awareness before approaching to
the desired people using various social media and communication
resources. Online social media (such as Facebook, Twitter, YouTube
among others), unlike traditional ones, can serve as suitable ways to
spread information during crisis. Recent researches have recognized
the importance and need to analyze effectively, obtain and use of
the comprehensive and huge information of these online information
sources provide (Ahmed et al., 2020b; Lazer et al., 2009). In many
experimental studies, examples of such practices related to emergency
response (Bagrow et al., 2011; Hughes and Palen, 2009; Li and Rao,
2010; Van Hentenryck, 2013; Wang et al., 2014; Watts et al., 2013)
and crisis informatics (Caragea et al., 2011; Earle et al., 2012;
Freeman, 2011; Guy et al., 2010; Pickard et al., 2011; Sakaki et al.,
2010; Skinner, 2013; Ukkusuri et al., 2014) can be found. Additionally, connectivity and activity through social media permit researchers
to examine and forecast what occurs in the actual world through social
network magnification (Korolov et al., 2015; Kryvasheyeu et al.,
2016).
People often learn about weather status through conventional
sources like television or radio and non‐conventional sources like
internet or social media, hence crisis communication networks play
a prominent role during emergency situations. Many researchers identified different organizations, news media, peers and government
agencies as emergency warning sources (Drabek, 1969). From social
science research, the literature exhibits that the socio‐psychological
variables help to translate and map disaster warning into decision‐
making (Gladwin et al., 2007; Lindell et al., 2005; Lindell and
Prater, 2007a, 2007b). Emergency evacuation studies have shown
how evacuation scenarios are strong affected by internet, media, peer
influence, local agencies and organizations (Lindell et al., 2005). For
an example, people in New Jersey (NJ) and New York (NY) had better
communication experience in social media as compared to traditional
sources during Sandy (Kaufman et al., 2012). Such online communications persisted during the recovery phases of the hurricane even in the
areas with power outage (Midtown Manhattan), based on the continuous distribution of tweets observed throughout the affected areas
(Floating Sheep, 2012). During Sandy, twitter data analysis explored
that evacuees were more likely to rely on social media for weather‐
related information rather that other information sources (Sadri
et al., 2016a). Overall, social media (e.g. twitter), survey, and census
data have been used to understand the dynamics of social influence
on evacuation decision making. Twitter data can be collected using
twitter application programing interface (API) and ego‐centric network survey data by using Personal Network Research Design (PNRD)
approach.

2. Social capital theory
2.1. Classical definition
As far as the literature related to the theory of social capital (linkage among the society members’) is concerned, it has been demonstrated in two related, however distinct ways. One way to approach
social capital is to see it as the degree to which a person participates
in various informal relationships (Hudson et al., 2020; Putnam,
1995, 2001). Based on this, social capital refers to the mechanism
how community members communicate one another and provides
the overall pattern of the organizational life and public health of that
society. This may include engaging in recreational, political or neighborhood activities, participating in groups for the environment, etc.
Another way of approaching social capital is to deliberate it as the
resources that individuals are able to only exchange through relationships with other people such as information, ideas, support, etc. as
opposed to human (education) or physical (technology) capital. This
definition calls for considering the structure of a given social network
that can explain the resource flow mechanism within the network
based on network density and size, frequency of contact, length of relationship etc. (Burt, 1984, 1985, 2000; Malual and Mazur, 2020). Coleman showed how social capital helps to generate human capital in U.S.
high schools and revealed that increased community involvement of
school parents can help reduce student dropouts while ensuring quality (Coleman, 1988). The study explained different aspects of social
network structures and how they can facilitate functioning of network
actors. Aldrich (2016) provided the evidence that civil society has the
ability to alter state policies in advanced industrial democracies (a) by
pushing siting authorities to choose localities with diminished social
capital as host communities for public odds in a dense local network
settings and (b) by obliging responsible organizations to retreat from
ordinary compelling policy instruments in managing conflict and
developing new policies and tools in the case of a stronger civil society
(Aldrich, 2016). In 2012, Aldrich empirically explored how social capital can help shaping strong and resilient neighborhood and communities where they can make more proactive decisions and absorb shocks
rapidly in post‐disaster scenarios (Aldrich, 2012). The study was based
on several forms of natural hazards such as tsunami (Indian Ocean,
2004), earthquake (Kobe, 1995 and Tokyo, 1923) and hurricane
(Katrina, 2005). The study also revealed that people with weaker
social relationships struggled more because of insufficient interactions
with groups that are more organized. Three distinct forms of capital
were introduced in this study that include: bridging (between networks), bonding (within network), and linking (across vertical
gradients).
2.2. Network capital as opposed to social capital
A more network viewpoint of social capital can be found in classical studies that employed personal network research design (PNRD)
approach to retrieve network information by interviewing people
and establishing their relationships (Ahmed et al., 2020a; Borgatti
and Halgin, 2011; Burt, 1984, 1985, 2000; Halgin and Borgatti,
2012). The General Social Survey (GSS) was conducted for the first
time in 1972 by measuring Americans ' core communication networks
(Burt, 1985; Marsden, 1987) and several studies used GSS to measure
discussion networks (Brashears, 2011; Wellman, 1979). The original
questions from GSS have been used by many scholars due to its flexibility to adapt to various contexts and shape the relationships of people and their close contacts (Bailey and Marsden, 1999; Burt, 1984;
Carrasco et al., 2013; Kowald et al., 2010; van den Berg et al.,
2009). However, Burt proposed the upper limit of the network size
to be five based on his experiments that accounts for increased measurement precision and reduced measurement bias while accounting
for severe time constraint (Burt, 1984). Miller and Simon also found
that five to seven data “chunks” that can be comfortably retained at
once in human memory (Miller, 1956; Simon, 1974). On the other
hand, Granovettor stressed more on the collective potential of loose
relationships and proposed that occasional and informal relations were
capable of linking diverse set of individuals allowing scope to access
unique opportunities (Granovetter, 1973). But this is applicable more
from a complete network perspective when two or more dense clusters
are connected by weak ties which could potentially link them and serving as bridge in between. This is not within the scope of an ego‐centric
network approach where the core discussion network of individuals is
obtained and the behavior associated with the network members is
analyzed. However, this issue, to some extent, could potentially be
resolved by measuring the social capital according to Putnam's definition (Putnam, 1995, 2001) which is more aggregated in terms collecting relevant data as compared to a network approach discussed above.
In this regard, Grootaert et al. suggested that the conceptual debates
on social capital is hard to resolve and provided a list of well‐
defined core questions to obtain numerical data on numerous extents
of social capital as a section of a bigger household survey (Grootaert
et al., 2003).
3. Behavioral modeling of hurricane evacuations
3.1. Overall hurricane evacuation
In the coastal areas with high population and inadequate road network, evacuation scenarios turn to a complex phenomenon (Cepeda
et al., 2010; Dow and Cutter, 2002). The need for effective management of evacuation and for stakeholder coordination have been identified for such areas (Gladwin et al., 2007; Lindell et al., 2005).
Evacuation preparedness organizations usually identify the alternative
evacuation routes in the event of short‐notice disasters like the hurricanes according to the predicted trajectory of the hurricane before the
evacuation process and evacuees receive formal routing suggestions
(Kim and Kang, 2010). Evacuation orders must give traffic clearance
time to get through bottlenecks such as bridges or traffic‐limited roads.
Nevertheless, evacuees often postpone departure and wind up leaving
together and displaying coordination in terms of their evacuation
behavior along similar routes (Wolshon, 2002). Previous studies analyzed various influential factors such as evacuee and household characteristics (type and location), path and hurricane alert mechanism,
etc. to illustrate how evacuation patterns can change as influenced
by such factors(Baker, 1991; Carnegie and Deka, 2010; Ehrhart
et al., 2012; Gladwin et al., 2001, 2007; Lindell et al., 2005; Lindell
and Prater, 2007b; Urbina and Wolshon, 2003).
3.2. Evacuation decision making
From the hurricane evacuation behavioral perspective, the key
challenge is to predict the evacuation demand i.e. identifying households who are likely to evacuate. Although studies on evacuations during hurricane is major, however studies on household decisions to
evacuate is limited. One interesting finding by Dash and Gladwin
revealed households supposed to evacuate end up not evacuating
whereas the reverse is true as estimated by emergency mangers
(Dash and Gladwin, 2007). This is why evacuation decision making
is a complex process during an active hurricane threat and is influenced by several underlying factors (Drabek, 1992; Gladwin et al.,
2007). In this regard, several other studies could be referred (Baker,
1979, 1991; Cross, 1979; Dow and Cutter, 1998; Drabek, 1999;
Drabek and Boggs, 1968; Fischer et al., 1995). Dash and Gladwin have
identified causal factors such as household location, age, sex, race and
ethnicity, earning, elderly people and children in the household and
past experience among others to have a significant impact on the conA.M. Sadri et al. Transportation Research Interdisciplinary Perspectives 9 (2021) 100325

duct of evacuation decision‐making (Dash and Gladwin, 2007). The
Huang et al. (Huang et al., 2016) statistical meta‐analysis (SMA) determined, however, coherent with Baker (Baker, 1991), that demographic characteristics normally had weak and inconsistent
interrelations with evacuation.
In terms of statistical modeling, Gladwin and Peacock represented
variables influencing evacuation. As example, household size, senior
personnel and infants in the household, households located in zones
of evacuation, are notable (Peacock et al., 1997). Another study used
intensity of storms in developing their model with example scenarios
storms and found such intensities strongly influencing evacuation decisions (Whitehead et al., 2000). However, some studies developed
behavioral models without considering the existence of heterogeneity
among different households (Fu and Wilmot, 2004, 2006)(Solis et al.,

decision making behavior and the study revealed that evacuation decisions are strongly correlated with geographic characteristics, agencies
and peer influence, evacuation orders from the officials, warning information, along with other demographics (Lindell et al., 2005). But, such
correlation analysis does not account for the combined effect of the
influencing factors on the decision to evacuate.
The ways people perceive different risks also influence household
level evacuation decisions. Mileti and Sorenson mentioned that individuals convert immaterial risk concepts into concrete adapted valuations of risk for them, their relatives, and their ménages through the
personalization process (Mileti and Sorensen, 1987). All the existing
models of evacuation decision making did not consider for the heterogeneity which remains from person to person due to variances in the
risk discernment’s production. In this context, Hasan et al. (2010)
established a random parameters logit model of household‐level
decision‐making with Hurricane Ivan data (Hasan et al., 2010). This
research explored dissimilarities of spatial location, obligation to work
commitments, order of evacuation, infants in households to understand evacuation behavior and decision making. Nevertheless, this
revealed evacuation decision making behavior at the household level
without any sign of communal influence on the evacuation decision
by accounting preliminary household and demographic criterions
(Hasan et al., 2010).
3.3. Evacuation timing decision
There are many studies on the departure time of evacuation wherever the most focus is to derive empirical distributions while
not the inclusion of various potent factors. The analysis by Lindell and
Prater may be said for a close review on evacuation temporal
order studies (Lindell and Prater, 2007a). However, few efforts have
been produced in literature as regards behavioral studies relating to
choices on evacuation timing (Kolen and Helsloot, 2012). In order to
explain evacuation timing behavior, Sorensen included path analysis
along with a sequential choice sets that are in par with the development of hurricane predictions (Sorensen, 1991). In the research, the
connection between time of departure and several important factors
was regarded as an ordinary least square regression (OLS). Fu and Wilmot created a sequential logit selection model that identifies whether
or not to evacuate for each family when examining the circumstances
around a hurricane. (Fu and Wilmot, 2004). Hazard‐based evacuation
model was developed to interpret evacuation decision‐making behavior in addition with evacuation timing decisions (Fu and Wilmot,
2006). A major hypothesis of the established pattern is that the decision on evacuation and on time for evacuation are taken concurrently
and all the comparable influential factors are affected by both choices.
But this interesting assumption for the hazard model may not be true,
as the variables influencing those two choices may differ, although
they are linked to each other. The model also includes homes that have
not evacuated, when the relevant observations are considered to be
censored correctly, overestimating amount of families evacuated.
The above mentioned temporal models (Fu and Wilmot, 2004,
2006; Lindell and Prater, 2007a; Sorensen, 1991) are mostly focused
on environmental, social and demographic aspects. Another hazard‐
based approach modeled finishing point of a duration given such durations continued for some time. (Hasan et al., 2013). In case of hurricane evacuation, hazard‐based strategy could capture the expiry of
the time span between receiving warning information and actual evacuations. This research offers useful insights into the time dynamics of
the decision making method of evacuation in the homes. In addition,
the modeling framework included random parameters to capture the
heterogeneous risk reaction. The main focus of this article was to
gather information from Hurricane Ivan's information on the causes
that affect the decision on the evacuation timing.
3.4. Evacuation mobilization time
Observing cyclone Frances evacuation behavior and the evacuees,
Dixit et al. modeled evacuation mobilization time i.e. time elapsing
between evacuation order being received and actual evacuations.
(Dixit et al., 2008). In this study, the authors explained effects of a past
hurricane in a subsequent hurricane impacting time of mobilization by simultaneously estimating two models which was also
described as the time elapsed between the receipt of the alert and the
time of departure (Sorensen, 1991). In certain other research, the time
to mobilize was referred to as an “evacuation delay” (Heath et al.,
2001; Stopher et al., 2005; Voght, 1991). Dixit et al. (Dixit et al.,
2008) explored such time to mobilize to explain perceptions of danger
structurally which can predict the overall amount of evacuees and time
of departure. Sadri et al. recently launched random parameters for
understanding this time gap between the evacuation choice and actual
leaving time from the house or evacuation area if an evacuation alert is
relevant (Sadri et al., 2013a).
3.5. Evacuation destination choice
In a specific transport network, Lindell and Prater referred to the
point as the nearest location where the evacuee is coming outside of
the hazard zone. They also examined the accommodation type and
the city or the town evacuees stayed before coming back to their
homes (Lindell and Prater, 2007a). Besides, Barrett et al. defined the
ultimate evacuation destination as the locations which evacuees considered safe on their own or locations which were suggested in the
evacuation plans. (Barrett et al., 2000). Southworth acknowledged
that evacuees show dispersive nature and are affected by various factors in the selection of their destinations: location of friends and family
members, speed of risk among others (Southworth, 1991). Mei (2002)
and Modali (2005) did not find any credible trip distribution models
other than the OREMS package, the Emergency Management System
from Oak Ridge. In order to distribute evacuation trips, one study used
both gravity‐based approach along with an intervening opportunity
technique where gravity‐based approach turned out to be more efficient (Wilmot et al., 2006). But such approaches do not apply to disaggregated areas, and additional calibration is required in such cases.
Cheng et al. (2008) have created two distinct multinomial (MNL) logits, one for families and friends, and another for the selection of motel
or hotel / motel in different zones.
Places of families and friends were among favored evacuation destinations with highest popularity, hotels were among the second most
popular (Smith and McCarty, 2009). For other possible hazards like
nuclear plants, public shelter is required and, such shelters supply critical resources despite limited availability (Mileti et al., 1992). Among
the people who evacuate, low income people consider public shelters
as evacuation destination (Mileti et al., 1992; Moore et al., 1963). A
recent research (Mesa‐Arango et al., 2012) has introduced a model
of type of evacuation destinations at the household‐level based on data
from Ivan (2004) to account for differences of use among the diverse

3.6. Evacuation routing strategy
A number of study could be cited with regard to emergency planning and network level assessment. As an example, the relative exactness of various types of trip generation was distinguished by Wilmot
and Mei (Wilmot and Mei, 2004) for evacuation. Another research
expounded and guided the formation of hurricane evacuation dynamic
models by Barrett et al. (Barrett et al., 2000). In order to prevent excessive traffic delays and densities in evacuations using trip chain simulation, one study provided a comprehensive framework and solution to
such issues (Murray‐Tuite and Mahmassani, 2004). The effect of the
events on the completion time of an evacuation of a big metropolitan
region was assessed by Robinson et al. (Robinson et al., 2009). The
study by Wolshon et al. (2005a, 2005b)) is concentrating on fields
for consideration in order to implement an effective evacuation plan.
Dixit et al. (2011) have used microscopic modelling for external checks
on the entrance and exit of cars into the evacuation network, and
implemented a “network breathing” method to enhance the general
outflow. In order to determine the best arrangements to decrease
chances of gridlocks in major hurricanes through efficiently distributed demand, one study introduced cell‐based traffic assignment
model (Liu et al., 2006). They presumed that only the beginning time
could be regulated to evacuate each staged area.
With regard to evacuation routing strategy, a network‐based model
of traffic flow was proposed to define ideal lane‐dependent plans of
evacuation for more rigorous highway topology with the main concept
of reducing traffic delay at intersection during evacuation (Cova and
Johnson, 2003). Two separate models were proposed to respond during disturbance to account for extreme uncertainty and dynamics of
such networks (Shen et al., 2008). While one framework provided
time‐dependent traffic control strategies that vary with the transportation networks, where shortest routes were chosen by taking into
account of possible delays owing to congestive conditions. In a
multi‐agent simulation, one study contrasted between two distinct
routing methods (Lämmel and Flötteröd, 2009). While the proposed
cooperative technique produces significantly greater evacuees as
opposed to the non‐cooperative counterpart. Another study examined
the route choice conduct of evacuees and found that evacuation efficiency subsequently degrades. They implemented an information feedback induced approach to increase efficiency in the ideal scenario
(Chiu and Mirchandani, 2008). The proposed routing framework
was calibrated using the preferential strategy indicated and it was
not designed to replicate real evacuation scenarios, rather explaining
how such scenarios deviate from ideal routing strategies.
One study claimed that it is possible to control and predict the likelihood of detouring from a path that is congested (Robinson and
Khattak, 2010). Analysis of stated preferences shows that riders preferred alternative path if there are delays on the planned path longer
than anticipated when ATIS data is accessible on alternative paths.
The survey aimed to provide sufficient behavioral information with
non‐representative experimental data. Therefore, they decided that
before using the results to replicate actual scenarios, a demographic
survey has to be obtained. Research conducted in nine districts /
parishes during hurricanes Katrina and Rita by Wu et al. (2012) also
focused on household evacuation logistics. The study jointly considered route and destination choice and urged the necessity of developing evacuation routing strategies. A follow‐up study reported that in
most cases route choice is based on shortest and/or most common
route assumptions. (Murray‐Tuite et al., 2012b). Sadri et al. have
recently proposed a random parameters multinomial logit framework
explaining evacuation routing strategies using Hurricane Ivan data in
2004 during evacuation scenario (Sadri et al., 2013b). The model recognized and discussed several key factors affecting the routing performance of evacuees, among three major alternatives: selection of the
usual route, following recommended emergency routes and possibly
bypassing or diverting routes. A recent study explored the selection
of major bridges by random logit‐based approach for Miami Beach
evacuees (Sadri et al., 2015b).
3.7. Evacuation mode choice
Moreover, several studies deal in specific with evacuation modes of
transportation. During Hurricane Lili, Kang et al. (2007) likened their
real behaviors to their reported (two years before) hurricane evacuation reaction. The respondents showed that their real conduct for evacuation, data sources, transportation methods for evacuation, amount
of cars carried and kinds of evacuation shelters were exactly as
expected. Lindell and Prater have disclosed that those capable of taking private cars, both from the citizens and management agencies, are
still widely presumed to take these cars. They also proposed to take
into account the vehicles which pull boat trailers in the evacuation
of hurricanes (Lindell and Prater, 2007a).
The necessities of captive riders’ and people needing special accommodation depending on families, neighbors, friends or transit services
were explored by Renne et al. (2009, 2008)). The potent preferences
for private cars (84%) were identified by Deka and Carnegie (2010)
on the basis of specified preference study information and the selection of other methods depending on their familiarity with the options
and the lack of access to personal vehicles. Using information from
hurricane Katrina / Rita, Wu et al. (2012), verified that the most
prevalent way to evacuate families is to take their own cars. According
to them, an exception is that elderly evacuees are less probable to have
a licensed car and are therefore more dependent on carpooling than on
public transport. Only 11% of evacuees did not take their own vehicles, 71% of whom drove with someone else, and 28% used another
type of transport.
During Hurricane Gustav, about ninety six percent of those who
evacuated took own van or car (Wilmot and Gudishala, 2013). A minor
portion took transit (0.8%)or did carpool (2.8%). Murray‐Tuite et al.
(Murray‐Tuite et al., 2012a, 2012b) created a concurrent optimization
model which included various transport modes and discovered that
the findings of the optimization model did not always correspond to
the expected conduct of the evacuees. Another research established
higher inclination to family togetherness while evacuating with lower
number of cars, while inclination towards individual belongings were
observed if more vehicles were used(Murray‐Tuite et al., 2012a). An
interesting study examined the special features of visitors who have
an impact on their feeling and mental reactions to the warning notice
of the hurricane (Villegas et al., 2013). The process of transport did not
affect perceptions of danger and fear except for visitors in surveyed
coastal locations. Lindell et al. noted that the bulk of evacuees
(90%) traveled by their own cars, while 9% were riding with colleagues during the hurricane Lili, and less than 1% used government
transport (Lindell et al., 2011). The average amount of cars per family,
according to their assessment, was close to 2.
A structure for incorporating both gathering of households and
choice of evacuation mode into a model in a no‐notice emergency
was proposed to study impacts of such problems and evacuation effectiveness (Liu et al., 2014). As such, a decision tree‐based method by
introducing the option of evacuation mode in cases without notice.
This framework considered socio‐demographics such as gender,
elderly people in family, distance and type of commuting, driving permit ownership, private vehicle accessibility, vehicular ownership as
potential factors while predicting evacuation modal split including
ride share, riding alone, taxi and transit. Another comprehensive evacuation study suggested that evacuation modal split is influenced by

4. Social influence in evacuation modeling
While a major hurricane approaches, people tend to communicate
their families, friends or neighbors in the community to prepare for the
hurricane. As such, evacuation decisions are influenced at different
levels: personal, family or community. Besides, the demographic factors as well as human race, religion and societal classification also
influence the evacuation decisions (Sadri et al., 2017b, 2018b,
2016b). Predictions of evacuation demand start from the decision to
evacuate followed by evacuation timing; evacuation mobilization
time; evacuation destination; evacuation mode and evacuation route.
One study acknowledged the influence of social networks as one the
three socio‐psychological processes combined with individual characteristics to influence the critical occurrence of the reason of why people sometimes decide not to evacuate from a dangerous situation (Riad
et al., 1999). According to this study, how people perceive risk and
find resources are also two key aspects. Along with individual risk discernment, influence of social networks affects evacuation decisions
although an individual is responsible ultimately for his/her personal
decisions (Riad et al., 1999). Several studies on hurricane evacuation
(Baker, 1991, 1995; Dash and Gladwin, 2007; Miller, 2012) found several contributors such as personal and household‐level criteria, ordering evacuation notice, and perception of risks, while the most
influential factor is the personal risk perception that shapes evacuation
decision making. Hasan and Ukkusuri (2011) found that the influence
of social network characteristics on how people perceive risk is not
comprehensively explored in existing studies. However, it is possible
that the risk observation of an individual can be communally subjective as evacuation resolutions spread from person to person thorough
the social network configuration. Gladwin et al. (2007) revealed that,
personal social networks based on families, relatives, friends, neighbors, and co‐workers impact evacuation decisions and information
propagation. The research also outlined the steps needed to promote
work in social sciences on the high priority problems in the hurricane
prediction and warning system by appropriate agencies and
organizations.
Existing studies also indicate that social relationships influence the
spreading of extreme event warning (i.e., extent, information contents
and sources, and amount of warning to the audience) and the ways to
adapt (Clifford, 1956; Messias et al., 2012). One study found higher
possibility of receiving information on evacuation situations if having
stronger connections and relationships in the neighborhood (Perry,
1979). Sometimes authorized information during warning phase provide ambiguous messages that are generally established through other
available information sources such as individual’s social network
(Mileti and Beck, 1975; Yore and Walker, 2020). It is usually found
that the role of family relationships has more impact in compared to
community relationships for making evacuation decisions (Perry,
1979). Nevertheless, logical that when the strength of family relationships is less, community connections can perform corresponding purpose regarding a behavioral model of evacuation decision making.
Individual social network parameters can be the effective tool to
predict emergency evacuation patterns. As an example, it is explored
in evacuation literature that people who normally evacuate during disaster have a large and interactive social network and vice versa
(Drabek and Boggs, 1968). Existing researches on hurricane evacuation from social science also recommend that households of African
American usually obtain more unified family relationship and greater
social networks and more community interactions compared to Caucasian communities, as a result have a faster emergency warning information dissemination (Perry, 1979). Existing research also explored
that indigenous groups (i.e. African American households) keenly
comprise their elderly people as part of family and result in higher
evacuations (Quarantelli, 1985). A contagion‐based evacuation decision framework was proposed to illustrate the neighborhood or peer
pressure on evacuation decisions based on network science principles
(Hasan and Ukkusuri, 2011). This study suggested that individual
social relationship is considered as the unification of family affairs
(such as parents, siblings and relatives) and other social connections
(such as neighbors, colleagues, and friends).
Another research identified that planning through social networks
in groups of extremely vulnerable individuals such as inhabitants in
mobile homes can be a significant evacuation readiness identification
(Kusenbach and Taylor, 2012). Hasan and Ukkusuri (Hasan and
Ukkusuri, 2011) proposed that the perception of risk of a person can
spread within the social network from person to person. Their research
created a model of contagion and used a simulation‐based strategy to
know the function of distinct patterns of community mixing to explain
the decision making of information cascade and evacuation. Sadri
et al. (2015a) has recently studied the resemblance of social partners'
evacuation decision choices based on ego‐centric social network of
individuals. Their results indicate that, along with predominant effects
of socio‐demographic variables, characteristics of relationships including intervals of interactions, conversation contexts and distance or
proximity substantially effect joint evacuation decisions. In this study,
Hurricane Sandy's initial information is used within a logit‐based
framework to explain decision‐making of evacuation conduct at both
individual and dyadic level (social tie). Another research by Sadri
et al. (2017b) disclosed that evacuation choice behavior of individuals
in vulnerable areas is considerably affected by household and
individual‐level aspects along with the structure of networks of social
relations. This research also makes significant contributions to the literature linked to the logistics of hurricane evacuation by defining the
impact/preface of social networks on decision‐making during the scenario of hurricane Sandy evacuation based on a carefully designed
household‐level network survey in the aftermath of Hurricane Sandy
that included southeastern United States (NY and NJ).
5. Social influence in crisis communications
Computing for disasters, a new research area, can potentially benefit the overall preparedness and community resilience by collecting
data and transforming data into usable and secured forms specifically
for the emergency officials (Consortium, 2012; Herrmann‐Lunecke and
Villagra, 2020). This requires examining multi‐disciplinary areas and
addressing challenges related to scope, scale, complexity and uncertainty. While previous studies suggested that 'systems approaches' will
provide more success instead of individually exploring the new
advances such as wireless communication, data fusion, embedded sensors, pattern recognition and so on (Association, 2003; Mason, 2006;
Sircar et al., 2009). Computing for disaster has the ability to increase
the number of lives saved, improved quality of life for the injured people, accelerated economic recovery, creating new job sectors, etc.
There are two major sources of big data: dedicated sensor networks
(for example, earthquake detection using seismometers) and multi‐
purpose sensor networks (for example, social media such as Twitter
using smartphones), both of them have demonstrated their usefulness
in disasters such as the Tohoku Earthquake (Pu and Kitsuregawa,
2013). According to this report, large‐scale data could potentially benefit several inter‐related phases in disasters starting from preparedness
then warning to recovery.
In recent times, Microblog service providers such as Twitter, Facebook, etc. are serving as potential source for handling relevant information during major crisis or emergencies. Social media has the

7. Summary and conclusions
To explore how human social networks can be leveraged so that
people make better and more strategic joint decisions during a crisis
is the primary objective of this study. The prevalence of social networks in recent times influence people’s decision making in so many
ways. The proposed review work contributes towards exploring novel
methodologies available in the literature for analyzing social network
data related to extreme weather events and provides a holistic understanding of the role of social and information networks on the underlying travel behavior of people in terms of reacting to a major disaster
such as hurricanes. Given the potential availability of social network
datasets in near future and the lack of appropriate methodologies to
connect social influence with travel behavior, this study makes significant contribution to the synthesis of complex contagion in evacuation
decision‐making and crisis communication dynamics. The empirical
models reviewed in this study could benefit practitioners and emergency officials to derive evacuation demand based on social network
data. While the overarching goal of this review is to provide a holistic
understanding of social network influence on travel behavior during
extreme weather events, the role of social and information networks
needs to be assessed at different stages of a disaster such as emergency
preparedness, evacuation, and recovery by adopting a holistic interdisciplinary approach to minimize the overall impact caused by a given
disaster. Resiliency cannot be ensured to a reasonable extent unless
all these interconnected phases are considered altogether.
This review study also makes significant contribution in terms of
policy implications. By identifying the key social network effects on
individuals travel decisions, policy makers would be able to make
strategies for targeted groups or communities instead of implementing
individual‐level policies. In addition, as compared to traditional policy
making strategy based on observed data, public communication officials can provide real time guidance based on real time data. The discussion of findings presented this review also provide important
implications in making communities more resilient and proactive in
terms of how individuals react to certain types of information and
make decisions. With the recent advancement in information technologies, real time information update and social media applications, these
methodologies can become useful in transportation analysis in near
future. Transportation engineers and traffic managers can be benefited
by using the key findings of this review study to predict the social network influence on evacuation decisions, evacuation and mobilization
time, destination, mode and route choice in future evacuation scenarios. The evacuation demand predicted from the empirical models
would also help emergency managers and officials to find out more
efficient techniques to control the evacuating traffic in future hurricanes. In sum, this research aims to serve as a key to demonstrate
the usefulness of the theoretical and methodological approaches of
social network analysis for an in‐depth understanding of how personal
network connections impact evacuation decision‐making.
Although previous evacuation research analyzed the dynamics of
the actions people take during major hurricanes, including the impact
of social networks and communication media on the effectiveness of
evacuation choice, quantitative studies of how social network effects
on evacuation decisions are limited. Evacuation decisions are, in some
cases, based solely on personal responsibilities and desires. However,
sometimes individuals, especially acquaintances, may affect them.
Existing researches in sociology recommend that social networks influence the objective of spreading warning message through information
dissemination about an imminent risk and people having higher social
contacts are assumed to obtain more warning information. Nevertheless, the experimental literature is inadequate about the evaluation
process of warning messages obtained from social networks into
decision‐making during evacuation. The quantitative models reviewed
here will better predict evacuation demand by practitioners and emergency authorities and make effective preparations during a major hurricane. It may also be useful for researchers and other stakeholders to
explore other essential aspects of evacuation behaviors such as destination selection, departure time, choice of route, modal split, and so
on. It is found that individuals with different sets of constraints and
characteristics react differently to a risk of hurricane. Important conclusions are drawn from relevant studies to tailor and support the complex nature of evacuation decisions. These insights and implications
are particularly meaningful for effective information dissemination
and real time crisis management.
