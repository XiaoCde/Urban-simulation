ABSTRACT
With coastal populations experiencing the growing threat of hurricanes as a consequence of global climate
change, models for predicting how household evacuation behavior may diffuse over time and space are needed
for emergency management. This study models the effects of social influence on household evacuation behavior
in anticipation of a hurricane event. An agent-based model was developed in this study to simulate: 1) a homeworkplace social network between households residing in the Florida Keys, 2) the communication of a hurricane
evacuation order among socially linked households, and 3) the resulting spatio-temporal diffusion of household
evacuation behavior. Data sources informing model implementation include U.S. Census block group data,
business databases, and statistics from hurricane evacuation surveys. Simulated model results from the model
were validated with empirical traffic records observed at a Florida Keys monitoring station during evacuation
from Hurricane Georges in 1998. This model builds upon previous research using agent-based models to simulate hurricane evacuation by incorporating multiple data sources and validating results with empirical traffic
patterns. Such an empirically-grounded model facilitates locally relevant exploration of evacuation behavior to
support the development of more effective evacuation plans and preparedness for future hurricane events.
1. Introduction
The arrival of Hurricanes Harvey, Irma, and Maria in 2017 was a
stark reminder of the danger of these extreme events and their increased risk with global climate change. Meanwhile, growing populations along the Atlantic U.S. coast have become vulnerable to hurricanes, because a large number of households cannot be protected by
staying in their homes or in shelters and must therefore evacuate to
survive (Barrett, Ran, & Pillai, 2000; Urbina & Wolshon, 2003). In response to a hurricane evacuation order issued by local government,
households must decide whether to evacuate, and their choices tend to
vary over space and time (Baker, 1991; Dow & Cutter, 2000). Indeed, a
household's decision-making about evacuation is not an easily predictable but rather a complex process (Gladwin, Lazo, Morrow,
Peacock, & Willoughby, 2007). Modeling such a complex process for
different households is thus critical to improving understanding and
planning ahead for evacuation from future hurricanes and other natural
disasters.
1.1. Study aim
The aim of this study is to simulate the hurricane evacuation process
using a model that accounts for social influence on household decisions
of whether to evacuate. In particular, this research explores the question: How might social influence affect household decisions to evacuate
from a hurricane, and how do these decisions affect the resulting
traffic? Taking a novel and empirically grounded approach to model
development, this study contributes to research on hurricane evacuation by generating a home-workplace social network to mediate social
influence, embedding this network in a spatially explicit agent-based
model (ABM) to simulate the diffusion of household evacuation behavior, and using empirical data to calibrate model elements and validate
simulated evacuation traffic. This model provides a virtual platform for
experimentation with alternative strategies for emergency management
planning.

1.2. Background
Evacuation is a response to an emergency situation that generates
distinct travel demand patterns in terms of who will evacuate and when
they will depart (Pel, Bliemer, & Hoogendoorn, 2012). Evacuation decision-making is primarily determined by risk perception, as contextualized by the level of the threat, the vulnerability of the household,
and the content of the message issuing a warning or evacuation order
(Lindell & Perry, 2004). Evacuation behavior has been linked to a
variety of factors, such as the intensity and proximity of the disaster, the
structure of the traffic network, and demographic characteristics of
households (Baker, 1991; Chen & Zhan, 2008; Hobeika & Jamei, 1985;
Sheffi, Mahmassani, & Powell, 1982; Stern & Sinuany-Stern, 1989;
Wilmot & Mei, 2004).
Studies examining demographic and socio-economic characteristics
of households as determinants of evacuation behavior often assume that
households make decisions independently from one another (Baker,
1991; Chen, Meaker, & Zhan, 2006; Wilmot & Mei, 2004). However,
evidence suggests that social influence between households also plays
an important role in affecting whether households decide to evacuate
(Horney, 2009; Lindell, Lu, & Prater, 2005). Such social influence includes communication of risk (Lindell & Perry, 2004) and knowledge of
others' behavior (Horney, 2009). In their review of activity-based simulation models for emergency situations, Henson and Goulias (2006)
underscored the need for models that acccount for interactions between
households and also called for simulation at finer scales of spatiotemporal resolution. By simulating social influence on evacuation in a
rich spatio-temporal context, the present study thereby adds to an extensive body of research using evacuation models.
Two types of models, statistical and computational models, have
been used to predict travel demand patterns associated with hurricane
evacuation. Statistical models use methods such as logistic regression to
understand contributing factors to evacuation decision, often based on
empirical data collected through surveys. For instance, Fu and Wilmot
(2004) estimated a binary logit model for Hurricane Andrew and
identified statistically significant factors including: the distance to the
storm, the forward speed of the hurricane, time-of-day (morning,
afternoon, and night), the presence of an evacuation order, possibility
of flooding, and housing type. These statistical models can then be
extended from the survey sample to to the entire study area to predict
which households will evacuate and thereby create travel demand.
In contrast to statistical models, computational models estimate the
travel demand by simulating households' decision-making process,
based on established psychological and/or behavioral theories, such as
risk perception, social learning, and innovation diffusion. As a class of
computational models, ABMs have been increasingly used to simulate
households' decision-making processes about evacuation (Chen et al.,
2006; Cova & Johnson, 2002; Widener, Horner, & Metcalf, 2013;
Zhang, Chan, & Ukkusuri, 2009). Cova and Johnson (2002) developed a
microscopic traffic model to simulate neighborhood evacuation that
could be used not only to locate bottlenecks, but also to identify which
evacuees were delayed at a given bottleneck. Chen et al. (2006) estimated the clearance time for evacuees along the Florida Keys' transport
network and the number of evacuees needing accommodation if the
main evacuation route were not available.
An agent-based modeling approach was taken in this study to incorporate household characteristics and social interactions among
households as influences on the decision to evacuate from a hurricane.
This bottom-up approach to dynamic modeling allows for simulation of
individuals as heterogeneous agents that interact and make decisions
that affect patterns of collective behavior (Grimm & Railsback, 2005).
When appropriately calibrated and validated, ABMs can provide simulated predictions under plausible scenarios to support evacuation
planning (Crooks & Wise, 2013).
Some recent ABMs of evacuation have simulated social influence on
evacuation in such a way that households decide to evacuate in part
because of knowledge about their friends' evacuation behavior
(Madireddy, Kumara, Medeiros, & Shankar, 2015; Widener et al.,
2013). These models use computer algorithms to generate abstract social networks, such as random, small-world and scale-free networks
(Hasan & Ukkusuri, 2011; Madireddy et al., 2015; Widener et al.,
2013). An advantage of these algorithms is that little information is
required to construct the social networks. However, these abstractions
can limit model utility for local evacuation planning.
This study addresses three main challenges faced by these social
network ABMs (Hasan & Ukkusuri, 2011; Madireddy et al., 2015;
Widener et al., 2013). First, actual social networks among households
are generally unknown and thus difficult to construct accurately.
Second, the model outcomes are often difficult to validate because they
use abstract networks or lack reference data about evacuation dynamics. Third, geospatial location is often excluded from these models,
which instead simulate diffusion dynamics among households in an
abstracted topological or social space. This study addresses these
challenges by developing a home-workplace social network using information on both residential and workplace locations, by validating
model results with observed traffic during evacuation, and by rendering
the model spatially explicit for the geographical context of the Florida
Keys.
In this study, households are modeled as individual agents that
make decisions about whether to evacuate after learning about an
evacuation order from mass media or through their social network.
These socially influenced decisions then manifest in spatial and temporal traffic patterns associated with the simulated evacuation rate.
These spatio-temporal patterns are needed to infer where people are
more likely to evacuate so as to coordinate evacuation traffic and avoid
congestion. This simulation study contributes to previous ABM research
on evacuation of populations at risk from Hurricane Georges in the
Florida Keys (Chen et al., 2006; Chen & Zhan, 2008). The present study
differs from this previous research in accounting for social influence
among households and heterogeneity in households' evacuation tendencies.
1.3. Study area
The Florida Keys was chosen as the study area to provide a geographic context for the model that is particularly vulnerable to hurricanes (Blake, Rappaport, & Landsea, 2007). Frequently visiting hurricanes expose the population of the Florida Keys to a high risk of impact
that is exacerbated by its situation as a low-lying area with access to
only one arterial road for evacuation. Even as it is uniquely constrained,
this linear transportation route facilitates demonstration of how to
apply the model algorithms and interpret model results. The Florida
Keys thus provides a relatively simple and yet relevant case study for a
model that involves diffusion of evacuation behavior in a home-workplace social network. This modeling framework is capable of being
extended to other vulnerable areas by accounting for other types of
transportation networks and urban populations informed by GIS data.
This area is a chain of islands extending southwest from the
southern tip of the Florida Peninsula (Fig. 1). More than 32% of its
population is concentrated at its southernmost tip in the city of Key
West (Fig. 1c), which is the farthest away from the outlet to the
mainland. Since there are no shelters safe for hurricanes with Category
3 or higher, residents of the Florida Keys must evacuate to the mainland
to ensure their safety (Chen et al., 2006). The United States Highway 1
(U.S.1), extending easterly 112 miles from Key West, is a single arterial
road connecting islands together and with the mainland. In a hurricane
event, it is the only viable evacuation route to the mainland. The single
evacuation route along a linear transportation network is thus vulnerable to becoming a bottleneck of acute traffic congestion during evacuation.
A practical consideration for this study area was the availability of
traffic data during hurricane evacuation that could be used for model

2. Model design
The model design consists of three interrelated components, including a social contact network among households, a diffusion process
of evacuation order, and a diffusion process of evacuation behavior.
After the evacuation order is issued by authorities in response to an
incoming hurricane, households being informed first would discuss it
with their social links, leading to communication of the hurricane
evacuation order. As shown in Fig. 2, household a is first aware of the
evacuation order and then spreads this information to social links b, c,
d, and e through the social network. After being informed about the
evacuation order, each household starts to make a decision on evacuation. The decision-making process is influenced not only by a
household's own characteristic evacuation tendency, but also by the
evacuation behavior of linked households in its social contact network.
It is expected that a household would decide to evacuate if most of its
social links have evacuated, due to peer pressure associated with social
contagion (Widener et al., 2013; Hasan & Ukkusuri, 2011). For example, once household a observes the evacuation of household b,
household a may decide to evacuate as well. The evacuation of both
household a and b (indicated by red shading) may further motivate
household c and d to evacuate. In such a way, evacuation behavior
diffuses from one household to another through the social network.
Fig. 3 illustrates how the social network is conceptualized as a
home-workplace social network, which has been demonstrated as an
improvement upon residential-based networks alone for modeling
contagion (Bian et al., 2012). In this study, social links to both residential neighbors and workplace colleagues are assumed to be sufficient to influence a household's decision on hurricane evacuation. The
assumption that co-workers are part of social networks that influence
household evacuation behavior is based upon findings from two lines of
research. First, previous research has found that friendship plays an
important role in evacuation behavior (Hasan & Ukkusuri, 2011;
Messias, Barrington, & Lacy, 2012). Second, many friendships are
formed among workplace colleagues (De Souza Briggs, 2007; Due,
Holstein, Lund, Modvig, & Avlund, 1999). Moreover, this assumption
has also been applied in other models simulating evacuation behavior
(Lindell, 2008; Murray-Tuite, Schweitzer, & Morrison, 2012).
As illustrated in Fig. 3, two households can be socially linked in the
model under either of two conditions: 1) both households reside in the
same neighborhood (such as a Census block group); or 2) at least one
member from each household works at a common workplace, i.e., colleague relationship. The resulting network follows a basic small-world
structure (Watts & Strogatz, 1998), where most of social links are highly
localized around households, but a few links can reach distant households due to working at same place. Further, since where people live and
work can be estimated from Census data, the home-workplace social
network may be considered more plausible than an abstract network.

Specifically, a population of 79,547 individuals was synthesized in the
study area and grouped into 35,064 households, according to U.S.
Census block group data from 2000 (Census Bureau, 2000). Two sets of
information were extracted from the Census data: individual-level data
and household-level data (Table 1). Using algorithms developed by Mao
and Bian (2010), table cross-referencing and Monte–Carlo simulation
were performed to assign attribute values to individuals and households
for each Census block group, so that the synthesized distributions fit
real demographic distributions. Then, synthetic individuals were filled
into a synthetic household based on its household type, size, and
number of children and disabilities. To further allocate synthetic
households to spatial location, i.e., (x, y) coordinates, residential land
parcel data were obtained from the Florida Geographic Data Library
and then synthetic households were randomly assigned to those parcels
of the same Census block group and same household type. A residential
parcel could contain more than one household.
Individuals were assigned to workplaces based on their household
location (as origins) and estimated travel distance to work (travel time
× speed of travel mode in Census data). Workplace destinations were
determined from a set of 6459 business locations in the study area,
including schools, that were obtained from a commercial database
(ReferenceUSA, 2003). Each worker was assigned to a business location
in which: 1) the industrial code matched that of the worker's occupation; 2) the distance by road to the worker's household matched the
estimated travel distance to work from Census data; 3) the maximum
number of employees for the business was not yet reached. Unlike
workers, school-aged individuals in each household were assigned to a
nearest school as their workplace. These algorithms for synthesizing
households and workplaces were previously validated by Mao and Bian
(2010) using the Census Transportation Planning Products (CTPP) data.
Because the synthesized population has the same statistical distribution
as the actual population, it is considered broadly representative of the
empirically observed reality. Therefore, while the synthetic population
necessarily differs from the actual population, its correspondence with
empirical data provides a plausible basis for the simulation experiments
conducted with the model.
3.2. Construction of home-workplace social network among households
Each synthetic household was assigned a total number of social links
n drawn from a Poisson distribution with a parameter λ that indicates
the average number of links in the network. Among the n links, a
uniformly distributed random integer number k was then generated
between 0 and n to determine how many workplace colleagues would
be linked to the household. To do so, k colleagues of household members were selected at random from their synthetic workplaces. Each
household was then linked to the households of those k colleagues. The
rest of n-k social links were randomly wired to other households in the
same block group as their residential neighbors. These procedures were
repeated for every household, which completed a home-workplace social network of the study area. This empirically based method of social
network simulation is distinct from the abstract networks simulated in
other social network ABMs of evacuation (Hasan & Ukkusuri, 2011;
Madireddy et al., 2015; Widener et al., 2013).
3.3. Diffusion of evacuation order
Because hurricane evacuation warnings are generally given at least
a day in advance of its expected landfall, the model was simulated for a
24-h period using an hourly time step. In the baseline case, the evacuation order is issued at the beginning of the simulation.
In the model, the evacuation order diffuses through two channels:
the mass media and the social network among households. Once an
evacuation order is issued by authorities, it is broadcast through mass
media, such as television, radio and newspaper, and delivered to a
given household at a certain probability, named media coverage rate.
The baseline media coverage rate was set at 1.1% in the model, indicating that a given broadcast would be expected to reach 1.1% of
households per hour. Although this value represents a theoretical rather
than empirical assumption, it was determined to be both a plausible and
conservative estimate after conducting sensitivity analysis using a range
of media coverage rates and broadcast frequencies (Yang, 2013).
If a household was informed about the evacuation order, it would
discuss with other households in its social network with a probability
referred to as discussion rate d. Through discussion, households unaware of evacuation order were informed and then would discuss with
their social contacts, leading to a cascaded diffusion of the evacuation
order through the social network. The initial discussion rate d(0) is set
to 80% when a household first receives the message and diminishes
with the passage of time, as formulated in Eq. 1 (Deffuant, Huet, &

3.4. Diffusion of evacuation behavior
In this study, the diffusion of evacuation behavior among households was modeled with a classic threshold model (Granovetter, 1978),
which has also been applied in other hurricane evacuation models
(Hasan & Ukkusuri, 2011; Widener et al., 2013). Using the threshold
model, a household would decide to evacuate if it learns that at least a
threshold fraction of links in its social network are evacuating. Support
for this heuristic can be found in the empirical correlation between the
decision to evacuate and the influence of peers (Lindell et al., 2005). As
illustrated in Fig. 4, once a household i receives the evacuation order,
either from the mass media or their social network, this household is
considered informed. Following a discussion with their social links,
informed household i will evaluate the proportion pi of social links that
have already evacuated or decided to do so. If pi is higher than their
evacuation threshold Ti, the household i would decide to evacuate;
otherwise, household i would stay and continue the discussion and
evaluation in the next time step.
The value of threshold Ti can vary from 0 to 100% over households
due to different risk perception and household characteristics (Hasan &
Ukkusuri, 2011). Different households have different thresholds, which
are assigned on the basis of empirical evacuation surveys as described
in the following section.
3.5. Model calibration
Surveys conducted after major hurricane events were used to
estimate households' evacuation thresholds as part of a model calibration process. First, a logistic regression was run on the basis of
survey data collected by Irwin and Hurlbert (1995) to identify
household characteristics contributing to evacuation from Hurricane
Andrew in 1992.2 The dependent variable for this logistic regression
model indicated whether each of 466 households in southwestern
Louisiana evacuated from Hurricane Andrew, following the analysis
by Wilmot and Mei (2004). Independent variables included household type (mobile home, single family house, multi-family building,
and others), ownership (owned or rented), size of household members, marital status, age, race, gender of householder, and presence
of children. In addition, a binary independent variable was included
to indicate whether a mandatory evacuation order was issued or not.
The significant results from this logistic regression model are shown
in Table 2, with a corresponding R2 of 35%. For example, as a reflection of the vulnerability context associated with a household's
risk perception, living in a mobile home has a significant and strong
positive effect on the likelihood of evacuation. In contrast, less vulnerable households living in a single family home were less likely to
evacuate.
Second, the results of this logistic regression model were applied to
estimate the evacuation tendency of every household in the Florida
Keys as a value ranging from 0 to 100%, as presented in Table 3.
Overall, the model predicted a 53% evacuation rate of households in
the Florida Keys, which is consistent with the observed rate during
Hurricane Georges (Dash, 2002; Dash & Morrow, 2001).
Based on the predicted evacuation tendency from this regression
model, each household in the agent-based model was assigned an
evacuation threshold, assuming that households with lower tendency
to evacuation would have higher thresholds. The disribution of
evacuation threshold assignments for the Florida Keys presented in
Table 3 was estimated using the regression model with Census data
for the Florida Keys. The estimated proportions were partially validated with results of evacuation surveys for Hurricane Isabel
(Horney, 2009) and Hurricane Georges (Dash, 2002). On one hand,
the survey of 2003 Hurricane Isabel indicated 12.5% of residents in
the affected area of North Carolina chose to evacuate even though
none of their neighbors did (Horney, 2009). To approximate this
observed rate, households with the highest evacuation tendency
(> 0.9) were assigned a threshold value of zero; these accounted for
15.2% of households in the Florida Keys study area. On the other
hand, the survey of Hurricane Georges showed about 35% of respondents would “only consider what is best for them no matter what
authorities say” (Dash, 2002), and those households were reluctant
to evacuate. To approximate this rate, households with an extremely
low evacuation tendency (< 0.4) were either designated as stayers or
assigned extremely high thresholds (0.8 and 1.0). As indicated in

4. Results and discussion
4.1. Social influence on temporal diffusion of evacuation behavior
To assess the effects of social influence on evacuation behavior, a
sensitivity analysis was undertaken for the model in which the average
number of links λ per household was varied from 2 to 8 with an increment of 1 to construct different network scenarios, as shown in
Fig. 5. This range was chosen based on statistical results from the
General Social Survey (Davis, Smith, & Marsden, 2005), and to be
consistent with the assumption that given the limited time and emergency situation, households would only contact those trustworthy
friends and neighbors who are closely connected to them. Since only a
small number of trusted contacts would be consulted by each household, limiting the most connected of these network structures to 8
average contacts may be considered as allowing a generous upper
bound.
Fig. 5 shows the simulated number of households evacuating each
hour under these different network structures (λ from 2 to 8). For each
social network structure, an evacuation curve was plotted to depict
temporal patterns of evacuation behavior in terms of the number of new
households evacuating over a 24-h period. Each evacuation curve depicted in Fig. 5 represents an average across 50 model realizations for
each social network structure so as to reduce the effect of stochastic
variation on the overall evacuation trend. These results show that when
households have more social links, evacuation behavior diffuses faster
with a higher peak and earlier peak time. For example, diffusion in the
social network with an average of 8 links peaked 4 h earlier than the
social network with an average of 2 links. The corresponding peak size
was almost doubled from 1800 to 3500 households. This pattern arises
because more social links facilitate the spread of the evacuation order,
triggering more rapid social contagion of evacuation behavior.
The overall evacuation rate, derived from the area under the curves
shown in Fig. 5, also improved as the average number of social links
increased. The overall evacuation rate ranged from 43.6% to 54.5%
under different social networks. The network structure with an average
of 4 social links produced an evacuation rate of 53.2% (95% CI: [52.6,
54.3]) was the closest to the observed evacuation rate of 53% from the
survey (Dash, 2002). Thus, this social network was selected to further
explore the spatial dynamics of evacuation behavior and simulate
evacuation traffic for evaluation.
4.2. Spatial-temporal patterns of evacuation behavior
To illustrate the spatial spread of evacuation, a series of maps was
created to display evacuation intensity over time and location, as shown
in Figs. 6 and 7. The evacuation intensity was estimated with a kernel
density function as the number of newly households to evacuate during
a 4-h interval in a 50 m cell location. The cell size was set to approximate the average size of land use parcels. All results were averaged
from 50 model realizations to reduce the effect of stochastic variation
on the results of each simulation run.
The map in Fig. 6 presents the evacuation intensity in the most
populous area, the city of Key West. A full view of entire Florida Keys is
shown in Fig. 7. Evacuation intensity was classified into 5 categories:
extremely low (0–50 households per km2
), low (50–200), moderate
(200–500), high (500–1000), extremely high (> 1000). After the evacuation order was issued at Hour 1, the evacuation behavior was at a
low to moderate level for the first 4 h (Fig. 6a). The evacuation intensity
was under 50 households per km2 for most areas in the city. There were
only three small spots with high evacuation intensity (> 500), all of
which were in the central and eastern part of Key West. These hot spots
emerged in areas with high household density and households that had
a very low evacuation threshold.
As time proceeded, the evacuation peaked between Hour 5 and 8
(Fig. 6b). Evacuation intensity was above 200 households per km2 in
most areas, and the maximum evacuation intensity exceeded 3000 in
the central Key West. As more households decided to evacuate, they
imposed increasing peer pressure to their social contacts and motivated
them to follow the same behavior. During the third 4-h period (Fig. 6c),
the intensity of evacuation dropped quickly to a low level (50–200
households per km2
). The most active areas moved from the central city
to the western side of the city, where the intensity remained at a
moderate level. This map suggested that after 8 h there were still many
households left in the western city having not yet decided to evacuate.
Fig. 6d-f shows that evacuation intensity gradually receded during the
last 12 h of simulation, due to three reasons: 1) the pool of households
to evacuate was depleting; 2) the discussion rate between households
was approaching zero; 3) households leftover had extremely high
thresholds and strong will to stay rather than evacuate.

along the evacuation route for people from Key West. An ability to
maintain the flow of traffic in Marathon thus determines the overall
efficiency of evacuation for the entire Florida Keys.

4.3. Model validation
As an imitation of the real, a simulation model will never be valid in
an absolute sense. Nonetheless, model validation is an important kind
of model analysis, given that a model's utility arises in part from the
authenticity with which it mimics that reality. Here, the model validation process involves a comparison of simulated and empirically
observed evacuation traffic over time. Different data were used for
validation than for calibration of the evacuation threshold assignments.
Efforts to validate the model were undertaken using empirical traffic
data for evacuation from Hurricane Georges in the Florida Keys. In this
process, the simulated spatial-temporal dynamics of household evacuation behavior were first input into the TRANSIMS (TRansportation
ANalysis SIMulation System) software package (Barrett et al., 2001).
The simulated traffic pattern was then compared to empirical observations of evacuation traffic recorded at a traffic monitoring station
during the evacuation from Hurricane Georges (FDOT, 2012). Evacuation of residents from the Florida Keys occurred primarily over one
day (FDOT, 2012), in line with the 24-h time horizon used for simulation.
Three evacuation orders were issued on September 23, 1998 for
different parts of the Florida Keys. The orders were given during daytime hours in part because most households prefer diurnal travel. The
first evacuation order was issued between 6:00 and 7:00 am for the
Lower Keys; the second evacuation order was issued at 11:00 am for the
Middle Keys; and the third evacuation order was issued at 4:00 pm for
the Upper Keys (Dash & Morrow, 2001). These evacuation orders were
input into the model for the affected areas, and the resulting simulated
hourly traffic was compared to the recorded traffic at the entrance to
the mainland (Fig. 8). In general, the simulation results align with the
observed traffic in terms of both timeline and magnitude. After the
evacuation order was issued, the traffic rose fast after some delay
caused by discussion or packing. The simulated evacuation traffic
peaked at 1:00 pm, approximately the same time as the recorded traffic
peaks. The peak size of simulated traffic was also similar to that of the
recorded traffic volume, within a range of 1500–1700 vehicles per
hour. The simulated peak was close to the designed capacity, 1600
vehicles per hour, of a two-lane road. These results indicate that traffic
congestion might occur on the two-lane part of the Florida Keys road
network. From this comparison of recorded and simulated traffic patterns during evacuation, the reasonableness of the model is established
as a platform for predicting evacuation traffic and supporting policy
design.
4.4. Model extensions
Having established a correspondence between the simulated and
observed evacuation patterns for the case of Hurricane Georges, the
model was used as a virtual platform for further experimentation with
alternative warning strategies and transportation planning to inform
local evacuation policy during future hurricane events (Yang, 2013).
Simulation of door-to-door notices, for example, produced greater
evacuation rates in the model than could be achieved from a single
order issued as a mass media broadcast. Inclusion of the bus as a public
transit option was also tested and demonstrated an increased evacuation rate. These experiments show that encouraging residents and
tourists to evacuate by bus not only alleviates congestion but also ensures that those who lack access to private cars can evacuate safely.
While these model extensions are particularly relevant to the study
area of the Florida Keys, the model itself may be adapted to other
coastal areas that are vulnerable to hurricanes. Insights from such
adaptation to other geographic contexts would reveal relative effects of
more complex transport networks and different population characteristics on simulated evacuation behavior. Using empirical data to ground
the development of a locally relevant simulation model facilitates the
exploration of alternative scenarios and identification of effective plans
for future evacuation needs. Furthermore, the approach presented
herein may also be extended to model evacuation from areas impacted
by other natural disasters.
4.5. Model limitations
Although any model is inherently a simplification of reality, there
are at least two limitations of this study that could be addressed in
future research. First, this research drew upon survey data from
Hurricane Andrew to parameterize evacuation thresholds for the model
due to the lack of comparable surveys conducted in the Florida Keys
after Hurricane Georges. While the survey of Hurricane Andrew in
southwestern Louisiana provided a reasonable proxy due to the demographic characteristics of the affected population, such calibration
would be improved if similarly detailed survey data were available for
the Florida Keys.
Second, the island-chain of the Florida Keys extends more than one
hundred miles. When a hurricane hits this area, its impact varies in
different parts of the Keys, and so do the risks perceived by residents. It
is likely that residents in the area where the hurricane will land would
perceive much higher risks than those who live far away from the
landfall. This research assumed that all residents, no matter where they
reside, perceived the same level of risk toward an incoming hurricane,
and overlooked the spatial heterogeneity of risk among residents.
Extensions of this research include consideration of alternative scenarios for the location of a hurricane landing, whether in the Lower,
Middle, or Upper Keys areas. For these location-specific landing scenarios, the distance between the residence and the location of hurricane
landfall could be used to estimate the level of perceived risk that is
incorporated into the model of household decision toward evacuation.
5. Conclusions
This study enriches the research on hurricane evacuation by integrating a home-workplace social network into an agent-based simulation model so as to experiment with social influence on household
evacuation behavior. The spatial diffusion process of evacuation behavior was emphasized and explicitly represented for the geographical
context of the Florida Keys. The model was empirically grounded in
several ways: construction of the social network incorporated information about residential and workplace locations; household evacuation thresholds were estimated using data from hurricane evacuation surveys; and resulting traffic patterns were validated with
observations from traffic records. The model enables iterative
