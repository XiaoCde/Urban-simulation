ABSTRACT
Under the growing threat of flood disasters exacerbated by climate change, understanding human 
movement in water-inundated environments has become crucial for enhancing evacuation safety 
and urban resilience. Pedestrian crowds exhibit complex, self-organized behaviors that arise from 
simple local interactions; however, how these behaviors adapt to water-related constraints remains insufficiently understood. This study presents the first systematic experimental investigation into single-file pedestrian flow under varying water depths (0.35 m, 0.60 m, and 0.90 m), 
with a no-water scenario serving as the baseline. High-resolution trajectory data are utilized to 
explore how water depth influences the emergence of collective patterns and adaptive behavioral 
responses. These findings reveal that increasing water depth alters macroscopic flow regimes 
(velocity–density-flow relationships), including a shift in optimal density and the emergence of 
distinct interaction phases governed by hydrodynamic resistance, water–human contact, and 
interpersonal spacing. At the microscopic level, step length, step frequency, and walking velocity 
exhibit a two-phase organization relative to headway, indicating consistent coordination patterns 
across all water conditions. To investigate the underlying adaptation mechanism, this study introduces the concept of anticipated headway, which captures how pedestrians self-regulate 
through spatial anticipation and temporal delay. This regulatory behavior remains consistent 
(~1.41 s) across all water depths, suggesting a robust self-organizing mechanism embedded in 
human locomotion, even under environmental disturbances. These findings offer empirical evidence on how complex collective dynamics emerge and adapt in perturbed environments, 
providing new insights into self-organization, behavioral coordination, and model calibration for 
crowd systems under flood scenarios.

between 2004 and 2024, floods accounted for 43.34 % of all natural disasters globally, and 37.93 % of the total population impacted by 
natural disasters. The persistently high frequency of flood events has led to severe casualties and substantial economic losses. Amid

Consequently, pedestrian movement under waterlogged conditions becomes a critical factor influencing both evacuation efficiency [5,7] and personal safety [8,9]. microscopic behavioral adjustments. Particular attention is given to the velocity–density relationship, variations in gait parameters, 
and the mechanisms of speed adaptation. Moreover, the concept of “anticipated headway” is introduced to refine the description of 
pedestrian speed regulation processes, emphasizing how individuals dynamically coordinate their movements based on local cues and 
delayed perception, which is indicative of a decentralized control mechanism typical of self-organized systems.

2. Literature review
2.1. Pedestrian evacuation under flooding condition
Numerical simulation remains the dominant approach for investigating pedestrian evacuation under flood conditions. Existing 
evacuation models can generally be categorized into two types: microscopic and macroscopic models. Microscopic models are typically 
grounded in behavioral frameworks and are often integrated with two-dimensional hydrodynamic models—such as computational 
fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations [6] or shallow water equations [16,17]—to simulate evacuation processes at the building scale, 
particularly in confined spaces like subway stations [6,18]. These behavioral models are further enhanced through various strategies, 
including the incorporation of fluid-dynamic equations to represent crowd dynamics [19], the quantification of flood-related parameters on pedestrian movement [6], and the specification of variations in evacuation speed [20] and evacuation status [21] among 
individuals with differing characteristics. In contrast, macroscopic models are employed at the urban scale, where individual behaviors 
are abstracted. These models emphasize aspects such as shelter selection [22], evacuation route planning [23,24], and the choice of 
evacuation modes [22]. Both modeling approaches contribute significantly to emergency response planning, evacuation management, 
and flood risk assessment. However, despite notable advancements, many studies continue to rely on theoretical assumptions or 
estimated parameters, with limited systematic experimental validation. Notably, there remains a significant gap in the quantitative 
understanding of pedestrian movement under varying water depths.
To address these limitations, some scholars have conducted empirical studies on individual movement under different water depths 
using open channel setups and other experimental techniques. Findings demonstrate that pedestrian walking speed is considerably 
reduced in flooded conditions compared to no-water condition. A distinct correlation has been identified between walking speed and 
the specific flood force per unit width—a parameter determined jointly by water depth and flow velocity [13,25]. Furthermore, 
walking speed is influenced by individual physiological attributes such as age, height, and weight [13,26], and generally decreases as 
water depth increases [13,26,27]. Environmental factors, such as architectural features like staircases and longitudinal slopes, have 
also been shown to significantly affect both water flow and evacuation performance [28–30]. At the collective level, Li et al. [7,15] 
noted that the mechanisms governing collective movement under water-inundated conditions remain insufficiently explored. To 
address this gap, they conducted evacuation experiments at a water depth of 0.60 m, involving both corridor and bottleneck scenarios. 
Their results revealed that the fundamental diagrams of pedestrian flow under flooded conditions—specifically the speed–density and

influence both micro-level behavioral adjustments and macro-level flow characteristics. Comparative studies across different water 
depths are especially limited. Therefore, controlled experimental investigations are urgently required to elucidate the mechanisms by 
which floodwater affects pedestrian dynamics. Such studies can provide essential empirical foundations for the development of floodresponsive pedestrian models and offer critical data support for disaster preparedness and emergency planning.
2.2. Single-file pedestrian flow
In single-file pedestrian experiments, movement is constrained to longitudinal interactions, effectively eliminating lateral influences and reducing the impact of environmental factors on pedestrian dynamics. This makes it one of the simplest and most effective 
approaches for investigating pedestrian movement at both macroscopic and microscopic levels. Such experimental setups have been 
widely used to study pedestrian behavior across various population groups (e.g., age [34], gender [35], height [35], and cultural 
background [36]), structural configurations (including boundary conditions [37], slope [38], stairs [39] and merging scenarios [40]), 
and evacuation contexts (e.g., limited visibility [41], height restrictions [42], pandemic conditions [43], and background music [44]). 
These studies aim to explore how individuals adapt their behavior under different walking conditions. At the macroscopic level, 
research has focused on the fundamental diagram of pedestrian flow—specifically, the relationships among speed, density, and flow 
[45] —as well as self-organizing phenomena such as the emergence and propagation of stop-and-go waves [46]. At the microscopic 
level, studies have examined how walking speed is influenced by headway [41] and how gait parameters (e.g., step length and step 
frequency) dynamically adjust [47–49]. Collectively, these studies significantly enhance our understanding of pedestrian movement 
characteristics and behavioral patterns, providing essential foundations for the development of pedestrian models and the management of collective pedestrian flows. In this context, single-file experiments constitute a simplified yet powerful framework for isolating 
longitudinal walking behaviors and quantifying their dependence on individual and environmental factors. This simplification facilitates the clear identification of fundamental mechanisms—such as speed–headway coupling and gait adaptation—that serve as 
reference relationships in studies of more complex, multidirectional pedestrian movements.
2.3. Research objective
Although previous studies have provided preliminary insights into individual and collective walking behaviors under flooding 
conditions, the micro-level adjustment mechanisms and collective flow patterns across varying water depths remain inadequately 
investigated through systematic empirical analysis. To address this gap, this study first conducted a series of single-file pedestrian 
experiments under three specific water depths (0.90 m, 0.60 m, and 0.35 m) to investigate how water depth influences pedestrian 
dynamics in terms of both pedestrian flow characteristics and behavioral responses. The main objectives of this study are as follows: 
(1). To examine how water depth affects the velocity–density relationship and to quantify its influence on pedestrian flow efficiency;
(2). To characterize the stage-based response patterns of key movement parameters, and to describe the relationships between 
velocity, step length, step frequency, and headway under different water depths;
(3). To investigate following behavior by identifying the characteristic delay time in speed matching and to develop a dynamic 
mechanism of velocity regulation under water-inundated conditions.
3. Material and methods
3.1. Experimental setup
The single-file pedestrian experiments under water-inundated conditions were conducted on July 30, 2024, at the swimming pool 
of the University of Science and Technology of China, a standard 50 m × 21 m competitive pool with eight lanes. Consistent with 
previous flood evacuation research (Bernardini et al. [13], Lee et al. [50], and Dias et al. [12]), this experiment employed simplified, 
controlled conditions (static water, free of sediment/debris, constant 26 ◦C, no impurities). This widely used approach isolates the 
fundamental impact of water depth on pedestrian movement while minimizing confounding factors that could hinder result interpretation. Based on human body dimensions [51], three representative water depths were selected: 0.90 m (waist height), 0.60 m

(above the knees), and 0.35 m (below the knees). An online recruitment poster was published one week prior to the experiment, 
outlining the procedures and potential risks. A total of 188 student volunteers (101 males and 87 females) were recruited through 
online advertisements. Detailed information regarding potential risks and the experimental procedures was provided to all participants, who gave informed consent before the start of the experiments. Participants ranged in age from 16 to 33 years (mean: 22.3 ±
3.5 years), with an average height of 1.7 ± 0.1 m and an average weight of 62.6 ± 12.0 kg. The three water depth conditions were 
tested across three half-day sessions. To ensure participant safety during walking in water, four certified lifeguards were on duty 
throughout each experimental session.
As shown in Fig. 1 (a), the experimental scenario consisted of an oval track with a uniform width of 0.8 m, composed of two 5 m 
straight sections and two semicircular arcs (inner diameter: 4.2 m; outer diameter: 5.8 m), resulting in a total centerline length of 25.7 
m. The oval track was selected over a straight corridor to satisfy both methodological and practical requirements of the study. The 
closed-loop configuration enabled uninterrupted pedestrian movement, thereby eliminating boundary effects such as turning and 
stopping that would inevitably arise at the ends of a straight track. This design established a stable flow environment, which is essential 
for analyzing one-dimensional interaction patterns. Furthermore, the oval track reduced transient disturbances in trajectories and 
facilitated experimental management within the pool environment. In addition, the single-file setup has long been recognized as a 
simplified yet powerful framework for investigating fundamental pedestrian walking characteristics and their dependence on individual and environmental factors [52]. By constraining movement to one dimension and disallowing overtaking, this design eliminates 
confounding lateral interactions and crowd clustering, thereby enabling clear quantification of the baseline effects of water depth on 
walking speed and interpersonal spacing. These baseline relationships are essential for establishing reference data under controlled 
conditions prior to extending analyses to more complex, multidirectional flow scenarios. While this setup necessarily simplifies realworld evacuations, it provides essential baseline data for a progressive, stepwise investigation of increasingly realistic scenarios. The 
track was constructed using metal stanchions, flagpoles, and colorful ropes, forming a closed loop with designated entry and exit 
points. To ensure structural stability during walking, volunteers were assigned to stabilize each stanchion.
Volunteers arrived at the venue one hour prior to the experiment, read the instructions, changed into beach trousers, and entered 
the venue barefoot. Staff then explained the precautions, safety requirements, as well as the background and procedure of the flood 
evacuation experiment. Before entering the water, volunteers performed on-shore warm-up exercises and subsequently entered the 
water to acclimate to the water temperature and walking environment. Prior to the experiment, participants were evenly distributed in 
a counterclockwise direction within the channel and began moving simultaneously upon receiving the start signal. To ensure onedimensional movement, overtaking was strictly prohibited throughout the process. 4. Results
To comprehensively understand the influence of water depth on pedestrian dynamics, this section presents a multi-level analysis of 
the experimental data. We begin by examining time-space diagrams and fundamental diagrams, which reveal macroscopic characteristics of crowd movement, such as stop-and-go waves and the relationships among density, velocity, and flow. Building upon these 
observations, we then investigate microscopic interactions among individuals by analyzing how headway influences walking velocity, 
step length, and step frequency. These analyses offer insights into individual-level behavioral responses under varying density conditions. Finally, we examine how pedestrians adapt their movement over time in response to local spacing cues.
For comparative purposes, the single-file pedestrian experiment conducted by Cao et al. [58] under no-water condition—with an 
identical experimental setup—was used as a baseline. These datasets are referred to as 0-S-N in the following analysis, where 
0 denotes the water depth (i.e., no water), S indicates single-file movement, and N represents the number of participants in the 
corresponding experimental condition.
4.1. Time-space diagram
Based on high-precision pedestrian trajectories (see Fig. 2), time-space diagrams of the straight corridor were generated under 
various water depths and experimental conditions, as shown in Fig. 3. Specifically, Fig. 3 presents the time-space diagrams under 
lower-density conditions (N = 10, 20, and 30). The colour of the lines represents the instantaneous longitudinal velocity of each 
pedestrian. At low densities, clear and consistent parallel lines can be observed in all water depth conditions, indicating stable 
pedestrian flow. However, pedestrians under no-water conditions exhibited significantly higher velocities compared to those walking 
in water. As the number of participants increased to 30 (i.e., a global density of 1.17 ped/m), small-scale disturbances began to appear. 
Some trajectories deviated from ideal parallel lines, suggesting that localized fluctuations started to affect flow stability.
Fig. 4 presents the time-space diagrams under higher-density conditions (N = 40, 50, and 60). In these diagrams, pedestrian states 
are marked in red when the instantaneous velocity exceeds 0.1 m/s, indicating movement, and in blue when the velocity is below 0.1 
m/s, indicating a stop. As the number of participants increases, brief stopping behavior gradually emerges. For N = 40, pedestrians 
under water conditions exhibit clear stopping for the first time, suggesting that the flow begins to lose stability. When the number 
increases to 50 or 60, congestion intensifies, and typical stop-and-go waves become evident. These waves propagate in the direction 
opposite to pedestrian movement, as indicated by the black arrows in Fig. 4. From a dynamical systems perspective, stop-and-go waves 
represent a canonical form of self-organization in dense pedestrian flows. This phenomenon manifests as alternating stop–move

The characteristic delay time observed during following behavior suggests a more nuanced mechanism underlying pedestrian

experimental settings of this study, and the generalizability of the dynamic regulation time across different environments, pedestrian 
populations, and movement scenarios remains to be further investigated. Future research under diverse conditions is necessary to 
validate the universality and applicability of this parameter.
5. Discussion
This study investigates fundamental questions regarding the self-organized behavior and collective coordination of pedestrian 
systems under water-inundated conditions. Through controlled laboratory experiments, we systematically examined how varying 
water depths alter single-file pedestrian flow—a minimal yet informative configuration for understanding emergent crowd dynamics. 
While previous studies have primarily focused on isolated changes in individual walking speed or averaged group-level metrics under 
specific water levels, the interaction between microscopic gait adaptations and macroscopic flow patterns across multiple flooding 
scenarios has remained largely unexplored.

5.1. Phase-dependent modulation of the velocity–density relation
This study systematically reveals the regulatory effect of water depth on the velocity–density relationship in single-file pedestrian 
flow and proposes a three-phase mechanism describing its evolution: 
(1) Low-density regime — water resistance dominated:
At low densities, where interpersonal interactions are minimal, pedestrians move freely within a relatively large space. The results 
show that velocity decreases slightly with increasing density, exhibiting a clear monotonic trend across all water depths: greater depths 
correspond to lower velocities. This trend is primarily attributed to hydrodynamic resistance—specifically viscous drag and added 
mass—which increases energy expenditure and reduces free speed. Gait analysis further confirms that both step length and frequency 
decrease with increasing water depth in the free regime (see Fig. 16). In this phase, water resistance is the dominant limiting factor for 
pedestrian mobility. 
(2) Medium-density regime — coupled water-human interaction:
As density increases, spatial interference between individuals becomes significant. Pedestrians must respond not only to

At high densities, spacing becomes highly compressed, and the system transitions into a constrained flow state. Velocities across all

This three-phase mechanism provides valuable insights for flood evacuation modeling. Traditional models often assume a constant 
drag coefficient [6,62], neglecting the dynamic influence of water depth on pedestrian behavior. However, our findings show that the 
impact of water depth on pedestrian behavior is density-dependent: water resistance is predominant at low density, whereas interpersonal interactions dominate at high densities. Therefore, we recommend integrating density-dependent drag coefficients into future 
simulation models to more accurately capture the coupled relationship between environmental conditions and pedestrian behavior.
5.2. Effect of water depth on capacity and optimal density range
Water depth further influences the macroscopic characteristics of single-file pedestrian flow by modulating individual movement 
capacity and interaction patterns. Experimental results show that as water depth increases, system capacity declines, while the density 
corresponding to optimal flow efficiency shifts toward higher values. This reflects a dual regulation effect of water depth on both 
capacity and efficiency interval: First, increased environmental resistance reduces individuals’ free-flow velocity, thereby lowering the 
upper bound of system capacity. Second, pedestrians in deeper water tend to adopt more stable and conservative walking strategies, 
exhibiting reduced sensitivity to local density fluctuations. As a result, the system can sustain smoother flow at relatively higher 
densities. From a nonlinear perspective, these findings suggest that water depth does not fundamentally alter the universal 
rise–peak–collapse structure of the flow–density relationship, but rather shifts its critical thresholds. In other words, environmental 
resistance modifies the location of phase transitions without changing their intrinsic form, underscoring the robustness of nonlinear 
patterns in pedestrian flow.
This phenomenon aligns with predictions from the classic Nagel–Schreckenberg (NS) [63] model in vehicular traffic flow: when the 
maximum speed is limited, critical density increases and capacity decreases [64] —illustrating a fundamental principle in flow dynamics. The wading pedestrian flow observed in this study exhibits a similar macro-level mechanism, indicating that although the 
water environment introduces additional physical disturbances, it does not alter the core dynamic structure of the flow system.
5.3. Dynamic regulation mechanism of velocity
This study suggests that pedestrian velocity regulation may not rely solely on instantaneous spatial gaps, but also involves 
anticipatory adjustments based on the motion of leading individuals. The characteristic delay time observed during following behavior 
indicates a behavioral time window within which pedestrians predict forward space and adapt accordingly. Building on this concept, 
the notion of anticipated headway was introduced, which integrates the actual headway with the space expected to be vacated by the 
leading pedestrian. This formulation provides a cognitively plausible extension to traditional velocity regulation models, shifting the 
mechanism from purely reactive responses to predictive, time-aware adjustments. The relative stability of velocity–anticipated 
headway relationships across water depths, compared to relationships based on actual headway, highlights the potential of this 
formulation to more accurately capture the time-delay feedback mechanisms in pedestrian movement under environmental 
constraints.
From a modeling perspective, anticipated headway offers a time-integrated variable for describing individual regulation, and may 
facilitate the development of more adaptive, perception-driven behavioral rules. However, its generalizability requires further 
empirical validation across diverse scenarios and pedestrian populations.
5.4. Limitations and future work
Despite the advances in theoretical analysis, this study has several limitations that require further investigation.
First, the experiments were conducted in a controlled pool under static water conditions, which simplifies real-world flood environments. Factors such as flowing currents, uneven terrain, and reduced visibility—common in actual flood scenarios—were not 
included and may significantly affect pedestrian behavior. Future studies should incorporate dynamic water flow, non-uniform surfaces, or environmental disturbances to better replicate realistic conditions. Second, the participant sample was relatively homogeneous, consisting predominantly of healthy young adults. The behavioral responses of more diverse populations, including children,

the elderly, and individuals with mobility impairments, remain unexplored. Broader sampling is necessary to improve the generalizability of the findings. Third, the study focused on single-file movement and primarily longitudinal interactions. This experimental 
design, which is widely adopted to reveal fundamental walking characteristics, provides a scientifically valuable baseline for understanding one-dimensional pedestrian dynamics. However, the imposed constraints inevitably exclude spontaneous behaviors—such as overtaking, lateral avoidance, crowd clustering, and other social dynamics—that are integral to real-world evacuations. 
As a result, the present findings should be interpreted as representing fundamental, one-dimensional relationships. Future research 
should therefore extend to two-dimensional systems, where lateral interactions and complex group behaviors can be systematically 
examined under more realistic flood evacuation conditions. Lastly, although the study introduces the concept of a density-dependent 
drag coefficient, it remains at the conceptual stage. A full integration into established simulation models (e.g., social force or cellular 
automata frameworks) and validation through urban flood simulations are required to evaluate its practical applicability.
Future research should prioritize enhancing environmental realism, diversifying participant demographics, expanding the dimensions of behavior modeling, and further investigating the influence of geometric configurations—particularly curved sections—on 
pedestrian movement stability under inundated conditions. These insights should be systematically integrated into validated simulation platforms to improve both the understanding and practical application of pedestrian dynamics in floodwater environments.
6. Conclusion
This study presents the first systematic investigation into emergent coordination and behavioral regulation in single-file pedestrian 
movement under varying static water depths (0.35 m, 0.60 m, and 0.90 m), based on high-resolution trajectory data. Under controlled 
experimental conditions and through comparative analysis with a no-water baseline, we demonstrate how water depth modulates the 
self-organized structure of pedestrian flow across both macroscopic and microscopic levels. The key findings are summarized as 
follows: 
(1) Phase-dependent modulation of the velocity–density relation:
Although the velocity–density relationship consistently declines with increasing density, the presence of water introduces distinct 
self-organizing phases. At low densities, hydrodynamic resistance predominantly influences individual behavior. At medium densities, 
water–human interaction generates feedback mechanisms that alter local spacing and movement stability. At high densities, social 
spacing and interpersonal interaction dominate, leading to velocity convergence across water depths—indicating a density-induced 
reorganization of collective flow. 
(2) Effect on system capacity and optimal density interval:
The flow–density relationship under all water depths follows a typical trend: increasing with density, reaching a peak at critical 
density, and subsequently declining. As water depth increases, the peak shifts to higher densities, the optimal interval expands, and 
system capacity decreases—by 27.4 %, 27.4 %, and 40.3 % under 0.35 m, 0.60 m, and 0.90 m, respectively, compared to the no-water 
condition. 
(3) Two-phase pattern of pedestrian movement:
Movement parameters (velocity, step length and step frequency) – headway relations exhibit a two-phase pattern under all water 
depths: a constrained regime characterized by linear trends and a free regime with stable values. As water depth increases, all parameters in the free regime decrease. Specifically, free speed declines by 40.8 % from the no-water condition to a depth of 0.90 m. Free 
step length consistently decreases under all water conditions (~0.50 m), with values approximately 25 % lower than in the no-water 
condition (0.67 m). In contrast, free step frequency drops sharply once water depth exceeds knee level (~1.0 Hz vs. 0.8 Hz), showing a 
reduction of about 20 %. 
(4) Dynamic velocity regulation via anticipated headway:
To better capture the dynamic nature of pedestrian velocity regulation, this study introduces the concept of anticipated headway, 
which incorporates spatial anticipation based on characteristic delay time. Compared to the traditional velocity–headway relation, this 
index demonstrates more stable performance across water depths. The regulation time remains consistent (1.41 ± 0.06 s). Such 
behavioral invariance suggests an intrinsic coordination strategy embedded in pedestrian systems, robust even under environmental 
perturbations.
This study advances the theoretical understanding of self-organized pedestrian dynamics in complex, flood-affected environments. 
The identified mechanisms and empirical laws can inform the development of adaptive multi-agent models and support the design of 
evacuation strategies in high-risk enclosed spaces such as subways, tunnels, and basements. Ultimately, our findings contribute to an 
interdisciplinary framework that links physical constraints with emergent behavioral adaptation, highlighting the relevance of 
complex systems theory in real-world disaster resilience.
