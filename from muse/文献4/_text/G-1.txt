Introduction: The safety of humans in crowded environments has been recognised as an
important and rapidly growing research area with significant implications for urban planning, event management, building design, fire safety engineering and rescue service to
name a few. This stream of research is aimed at guiding safe designs and effective evacuation plans by simulating emergency scenarios and estimating measures such as total
evacuation time. A large body of research has also been dedicated to the development
of modelling tools with the capability to identify (and thus prevent) circumstances that
lead to crowd discomfort, crashes or disasters in mass gatherings and public facilities. It
has, however, been argued that the empirical knowledge in this area has lagged behind
the theoretical developments and computational capabilities. This has left the descriptive
power of the existing models for reproducing the natural behaviour of humans questionable given that in many cases there is a lack of reliable and well-conditioned data for
model validation or calibration purposes.
Methods: With the vast majority of the empirical knowledge in this fast-growing and interdisciplinary field being very recent, a survey of the existing literature is still missing.
Here, we gather together the existing empirical knowledge in this area in a comprehensive review (based on surveying more than 160 studies restricted to those published in
peer-reviewed journals since 1995) in order to help bridge this gap. We introduce for the
first time a categorisation system of the relevant data collection techniques by recognising
seven general empirical approaches. We also differentiate between various aspects of human behaviour pertinent to crowd behaviour by putting them into perspective in terms of
three general levels of “decision making”. We also discuss the advantages and disadvantages offered by each data collection technique. Major gaps and poorly-explored topics in
the current literature are discussed.
Findings and applications: Our major conclusion is that the empirical evidence in this
area is largely disperse and even in some cases mixed and contradictory, requiring a more
unified system of terminologies and problem definitions as well as unified measurement
methods in order for the findings of different studies to become replicable and comparable. We also showed that the existing body of empirical studies display a clear imbalance
in addressing various aspects of human behaviour with certain (but crucial) aspects (such
as “pre-movement time” and “choice of activity”) being poorly understood (as opposed to
our knowledge and amount of data about “walking behaviour” for example). Our review
also revealed that previous studies have predominantly displayed a stronger tendency to
study the behaviour based on aggregate measures as opposed to individual-level data col-

1. Introduction
How humans react to threats and emergencies in crowded spaces is a crucial question for disaster preparedness, emergency management and evacuation planning. Furthermore, identifying and understanding the mechanisms that may lead to
crowd disasters and incidents (in the absence of external threats) has also been majorly important for assuring the safety
of humans in crowded environments. These problems has received growing attention within the recent years in the light of
the increasing frequency and scale of crowd incidents as well as the occurrence of major emergency incidents in high-rise
buildings and pedestrian facilities around the world.
These questions have guided an area of research that aims at developing prediction models and simulation tools that can
be used for assessing the effectiveness of evacuation plans, building designs and crowd management strategies. Significant
advances have been made in terms of developing various methodologies and theories for this purpose. However, whether
the descriptive power of such methodologies is reliable enough for practical applications has remained unclear. This has
been largely attributed to the insufficiency of the evidence based on which one can validate the descriptive capability of
their models or fine-tune them by estimating their essential parameters.
The abovementioned issue highlights the need for developing robust empirical knowledge on human crowds’ behaviour.
The problem is of particular importance in relation with the emergency escape behaviour for which observations in natural
settings are extremely rare. With the current developments in the computational capacities and modelling techniques, it can
be arguably claimed that tackling this problem would not be prohibitively difficult should the analyst has access to sufficient
observations that offer the possibility of robust model calibration or validation.
The recent advancements in the image processing techniques have offered researchers in this area the possibility of
gaining knowledge about certain aspects of this problem from naturally-occurring settings. It should however, be noted that
although the past real events of emergency or past crowd incidents always offer the possibility of gaining useful insights,
this does not necessarily guarantee that these observations can perfectly serve as modelling material (like calibration data)
nor does it mean that they can cover the entire range of phenomena that the analysts need to understand in order to
develop models with extensive range of applicability. This underlies the importance of experimentation in this field which,
as will be shown by this study, has gained an increasing attention in the recent years.
Experimental attempts in this field, however, have often come under criticism on the account of their inherent limitations
such as sample size, environmental realism, contextual realism, data extraction accuracy, measurement accuracy etc. Unlike
many other areas of behavioural sciences, it is essential in many cases in this field to gather a large number of individuals in
the experiment environment at the same time which proves costly and logistically difficult. Creating the contextual elements
of an emergency scenario while observing the ethical requirements in an experimental setting also poses a major challenge.
These issues have made the extent of empirical knowledge in this field lag relatively far behind computational and modelling developments. Although the number of studies that have attempted to fill this gap has shown a sharp rise within
the last ten years, a survey of the existing empirical knowledge has still been missing. Published studies have been scattered over a wide range of scientific journals (including disciplines like psychology and behavioural sciences, engineering,
ergonomics, safety, transportation and applied physics to name a few) owing to the interdisciplinary nature of the problem.
Scholars from a wide range of research backgrounds have contributed to this topic from various and often vastly different
perspectives.
This study is set out to address this gap by making a comprehensive review of more than 150 studies that offer empirical
insight and data contributions to crowd behaviour and emergency evacuation modelling. For the first time, we introduce an
inclusive categorisation of the data collection techniques, and contrast the advantages and disadvantages of various methods. We aim to depict a clearer picture of the empirical knowledge and identify what do know and what we do not know
so far about this multi-faceted problem. We hope that researchers working on this topic find this contribution useful as
a benchmark for a making quick evaluation of the previous studies, finding the major knowledge gaps, identifying possible contradictory findings of earlier studies, forming hypotheses, designing their experiments and testing the accuracy of
commonly-used theoretical assumptions in this field. We also hope that the method categorisation and the subsequent discussions help to identify the data collection approaches that suit the researchers’ specific problems in the best way.
In the rest of this paper, we first itemise some of the major challenges in regard to modelling crowd behaviour in general
and in emergency situations in particular. We then will have a brief overview of the existing review and meta-analysis
studies that are to any degrees related to our topic in hand in order to further clarify the gap that this review study aims to
bridge. The inclusion criteria and the scope of this review will be discussed next. We then introduce a general categorisation
of the data collection methods in this field and look at the findings and the details of the relevant studies in each category

2. General challenges in crowd behaviour and emergency modelling
Understanding the mechanisms underlying the behaviour of human crowds, whether in normal or emergency conditions,
proves to be challenging. In this section we aim to identify and enumerate primary sources of this challenge to the best of
our knowledge, although one can never reasonably claim this list to be exhaustive.
(1) Complexity of human behaviour and its variation. The analyst has to deal with predicting and replicating the behaviour of humans which is intrinsically complex and multi-faceted. One particular aspect is the variety of decisions
that pedestrians have to make as well as the variation of the behaviour that different individuals display in similar
circumstances, making it difficult from a modeller’s perspective to come up with a universal rule of behaviour that
can well represent the general population.
(2) Continuity of the movement space. Unlike the movement of vehicles in urban road networks that are largely restricted
to the structure of the network as well as the driving regulations, the movements of pedestrians in buildings or urban
areas are far more flexible. The movement space can be, in comparison, considered as continuous over space and far
less restricted or regulated. The pedestrians are also at more liberty to revisit and possibly change their movement
decisions almost continuously in time and this adds further complexity from modelling perspective.
(3) The variety of confining geometries. Again, compared to the structure of urban road networks that display certain
universally consistent features, the types of geometries that have to be potentially considered when dealing with
pedestrians are far more varied in terms of their shape, structure and size. The congregation of pedestrian humans
can take place in variety of geometries from relatively open areas such as those of festivals, or from single-level
buildings to high-rise buildings, or from small venues to large venues such as sport stadiums; and from geometrically
simple spaces to complex venues such as major transport stations. This variety of environmental context makes it
more difficult for modellers to introduce universal models or behavioural rules that can serve adequately in all those
situations.
(4) Context-dependency of the pedestrians’ behaviour. It is crucial for the descriptive capability of the pedestrian models
to distinguish between major contextual factors that can potentially trigger vastly different types of behavioural responses. For example, it is important to distinguish whether a modelling rule is to be applied to dense crowds, such
as those of Hajj pilgrimage, or is intended for the behaviour displayed in lightly congested areas. For mass gatherings,
it may be necessary to take into account the type of population and the purpose of gathering (e.g. sport events, music festivals, political protests etc.). Also, distinguishing between different types of movement decisions that are made
under emergency conditions as opposed to the normal circumstances is of crucial importance. Some may even take
this further, and argue that even within the context of emergency one may need to differentiate between various
sources of emergency (e.g. fire, terror attacks etc.) that may also trigger different responses. This variety of context
and the dependence of the behaviour in each case makes it even more difficult to propose universal models with
fully generic applicability. From the data collection perspective, it also shows the vast broadness of the behavioural
phenomena that is to be understood, and thus the necessity of experimentation and data collection on a very wide
range of topics.
(5) Scarcity of empirical data and observations. Further to the issues discussed above, data and observation appears to
be an inevitable requirement of successful modelling in this field. Such data may originate from natural settings or
from experimentation. It has been argued that the natural observations may not suffice to cover the entire range
of phenomena that is needed. In some contexts such as emergency evacuations, such observation are either barely
accessible by researchers or are not in proper condition for detailed analysis. Experimentation, on the other hand, has
proven costly (particularly for large crowd experiments) or unrealistic in many contexts. For certain topics related to
emergency behaviour, the experimenter is restricted by ethical considerations and need to make a reasonable balance
between the realism and invasiveness level of their design. Our later discussion on the advantages and disadvantages
of experimental and data collection methods will shed more light on these challenges in details.
3. Existing review and meta-analyses studies
The body of research on the topic of crowd modelling and safety has grown at a fast pace within the past years necessitating review and meta-analysis studies that present syntheses of these numerous studies. Hughes (2003) provides an
overview of the examples in which the theory of “crowd motion as continuous flows” has been successfully applied to provide assistance in the annual Muslim Hajj and evaluating various design and control strategies. The study of Papadimitriou
et al. (2009) presents a critical assessment of the modelling approaches of pedestrians in urban environments with a particular focus on “route choice” and “crossing” behaviour of pedestrians. They pointed out to a lack of comprehensive pedestrian
models with ability to cover an entire trip (i.e. a model capable of replicating all types of decisions displayed by pedestrians).

The study of Shi et al. (2009) presents a meta-analysis based on their predecessor studies that provided measurements
on different stages of evacuation including “pre-evacuation time”, “movement speed” and “occupants’ characteristics” with
particular focus on fire safety in buildings. In the latest meta-analysis study at the time of drafting this paper, Bosina and
Weidmann (2017) focused on “walking speed” and estimated the impact of important factors influencing walking speed.
They also recognised the incompatibility of the measurement methods of walking speed in the past studies.
Kobes et al. (2010a) present a review of the literature on human behaviour in fire in buildings and suggest a set of
critical factors that determine occupants’ fire response performance. They also point to the inconsistency of some of the
assumptions about fire safety in buildings with the (empirical) knowledge in the literature. With a narrower focus, Shiwakoti
and Sarvi (2013c) look at the studies of collective human dynamics under emergency based on experiments with “nonhuman organisms”. They emphasise on the need for gaining a better understanding of the similarities and dissimilarities
between (collective behaviour) of humans and animals in order to develop better experiment designs.
The review study of Kok et al. (2016) presents an overview of the state-of-the-art methods in crowd behaviour analysis
from “physics and biologically inspired” perspectives, with a particular emphasis on computer vision techniques. A similar
perspective is also presented by the review studies of Zitouni et al. (2016) and Yogameena and Nagananthini (2017) with
a central focus on “visual crowd analysis”. Illiyas et al. (2013) also present a survey of the crowd stampede incidents during mass gatherings in India which revealed the significant share of religious gatherings in such incidents as well as the
inadequacy of crowd risk management strategies in India.
In another review study, Zheng et al. (2009) recognised seven “methodological approaches” for crowd evacuation and
discussed their advantages and disadvantages. They also pointed out the need for taking into consideration the “psychological elements” affecting collective behaviour in this context. Similar to the review study of Zheng et al. (2009), the study
of Duives et al. (2013) also mainly focused on the “methodological approaches” of “crowd simulation” models by evaluating
them in terms of their capability for replicating high-density crowd phenomena. By taking a relatively similar perspective,
Vermuyten et al. (2016) reviewed “optimisation models” for pedestrian evacuation and design. Their major conclusion suggested that “more attention should be given to calibration and implementation of the proposed models” (Vermuyten et al.,
2016).
Table 1 presents more details of these review and meta-analysis studies discussed above in chronological order based
on their date of publication. Based on this survey of the previous review studies, we concluded that the literature has
been missing a comprehensive review of the “experimental and data collection” body of studies on this topic which has
experienced a distinct growth in the recent years. It has been the main motive of this study to bridge this gap.
4. Method, scope and inclusion criteria
This review merely focuses on studies that present “empirical evidence” about crowd behaviour. Pure theoretical studies
and simulation applications with no relation to data collection or calibration were considered out of the scope. The publications had to be related to the “crowd behaviour” in general or to the concept of “emergency escape” and “evacuation” in
particular.
As discussed in the previous sections, our focus was in general on studies whose ultimate aim is either to reduce the possibility of “crowd disasters” by identifying various crowd phenomena and management solution or to plan for the safe and
quick “evacuations” of crowded facilities. From that perspective, some of our references may particularly be about emergency
escape whereas others present general empirical evidence about crowd behaviour (which may or may not be applicable to
emergency contexts). In more specific terms, studies with empirical insight about “emergency escape” of pedestrians such
as “fire evacuations of buildings” (high-rise or low-rise) are included along with studies with strong empirical components
related to “crowd management” and “crowd disaster” prevention.
We limited our search to the studies published since 1995 (to April 2017) in English-language peer-reviewed journals
indexed by “Web of Knowledge” or “SCOPUS”. As we show in subsequent sections, most of the empirical studies in this field
have been published after 2000 (with a vast majority having been published since 2008), therefore we limited our search
to the more recent part of the literature by excluding studies published before 1995.
Given that the scope of the review is relatively wide, aiming a critical theme for the review was rather impractical as
it would have resulted in an unreasonably long paper. Also, as mentioned before, the body of the empirical knowledge in
this field is relatively young and the literature offers many experimental studies that may come across quite primitive or
unrealistic to some researchers and that includes the authors of this paper as well. As a result, taking a critical theme would
have given rise to lengthy discussions about individual papers that was not set to be the purpose of this study. Accordingly,
we do not engage in this work in criticising findings or methods of individual papers although we point out to the possible
inconsistencies or contradictions between findings of different studies whenever we identify such mixed evidence. This was
the main reason why we strictly limited our references to the studies in peer-reviewed journals indexed by major databases.
As will be discussed in the following section, we recognised seven general methods of data collection upon which this
paper and our list of references is structured. The keywords used in our search process differed for each method. As a
result a quite wide range of keywords and their combinations were used in the aforesaid databases in addition to the
general searches in Google Scholar. The list of keywords includes but is not limited to terms such as “crowd behaviour”,
“human behaviour”, “pedestrian behaviour”, “ pedestrian crowd”, “crowd motion”, “crowd disasters”, “data”, “experimentation”, “calibration”, “validation”, “estimation”, “fire evacuation”, “emergency escape”, “emergency evacuation”, “laboratory

5. Categorising the data collection methods
We identified seven general approaches for data collection in this topic. This includes (1) animal experiments, (2) controlled laboratory experiments with human subjects, (3) virtual reality (VR) and hypothetical choice (HC) experiments, (4)
evacuation drill experiments, (5) analysis of natural disasters, (6) Video analysis of pedestrian crowds’ movement in natural
environments and (7) post-disaster interview with survivors.
In relation to the category (1), we simply refer to the studies that make use of any “non-human living organism” (such as
ants, mice or sheep) as a model of human crowd behaviour. In relation with category (2) we refer to the experimental conditions that entail real movement and activity of human subjects often in groups of people but in controlled environments.
In relation to category (3), we acknowledge that this type of experiments is also obviously based on human subjects (like
category (2)) and is of laboratory nature as well. The choice of the terminology is not perfectly accurate but compromising
its accuracy was inevitable in order to facilitate the communication in the rest of the paper. In relation to category (4), we
refer to the controlled experiments with humans that specifically take place in actual places such as real buildings rather
than artificial or virtual setups. The studies in category (5) are predominantly based on video analysis (like category (6)) but
relate to specific incidents in the past that resulted in injuries or deaths of pedestrians or the natural past disasters such as
earthquakes. Studies in category (7) also mostly refer to actual incidents and major cases of emergency (often fire and terror
attacks) in the past and are based on making individual contact with the survivors often through interviews with survivors
of such events.
The compromises that we made for the choice of terminologies for this categorisation, the similarities and dissimilarities
of these methods as well as their advantages and limitations will be discussed in a dedicated section later on. In following
sections, we survey individual studies based on each method while the main focus in the body of the text will be on
the findings of the individual studies, the phenomena that they investigated and their relation with similar studies in that
category. Further details such as the details of the experiment design, sample sizes, data extraction technique etc. can be
found in the relevant tables that synthesise these elements for the studies of each category.
5.1. Animal experiments
Experimentations of crowd movement have been employed as a non-invasive method to humans particularly when the
emergency escape context is of researcher interest. They have been regarded as an approximate model of human “collective”
behaviour. They allow a range of phenomenon to be investigated that are otherwise hard to be replicated in experiments
with humans without compromising subjects’ safety. Some studies refer to this as the state of “panic” and claim their
experiments as models of “panic behaviour”. The animal experiments have proven most useful when aggregate measures of
collective movement are of primary interest as opposed to the study of individuals’ behaviour. So far, experimentations with
ants and mice has been the most common although empirical evidence based on the movements of sheep herd has also
been reported in a number of studies. In order to make the subjects of the study (i.e. the animal or insect) perform the task
(often escape from the physical setup designed by the experimenters) and initiate the movement, repellents of some type
have been predominantly used in these experiments including invasive chemicals, water or heat. A number of studies have
used a measured level of invasiveness as a controllable proxy of the level of motivation for escape.
A pioneer experiment in this field can be found in the study of Saloma et al. (2003) where they performed mice experiments in water pool for investigating “self-organised queuing” and scale-free behaviour. They showed that the distribution
of the burst sizes for escaped mice follows exponential and (truncated) power-law distributions depending on the width of
exit and that this phenomenon is exclusively observable at a critical sampling rate. In a follow-up and more recent publication, Saloma et al. (2015) used a similar method for studying the same phenomenon, self-organised queuing. They looked at
the impact of “prior individual training”. They showed that groups of trained mice escaped the water pool at a much faster
rate than their untrained peers while showing self-organised queuing. They reported that queuing was not observable in
untrained mice groups. Their study also suggested that the benefit of prior training was more distinct when the water pool
was more crowded.
Another pioneer experiment of escape behaviour using non-human organisms was reported in the study of Altshuler
et al. (2005). Using ants as models of pedestrians, they showed that individuals confined to a room can display a nonsymmetrical use of two identical exits under “panic” conditions whereas they use the exits in approximately equal proportions under normal conditions. They refer to this effect as “symmetry breaking” caused by “panic-induced herding”.
By connecting this to the theoretical assumption of “herd behaviour” proposed by the study of Helbing et al. (2000) they
claimed their observations as an empirical verification of this assumption. They also concluded that this can be used as
evidence that collective escape behaviour of humans and ants can be quite similar. The notion of “symmetry breaking” was
also mentioned in two more recent studies. Based on experiments with ants, Li et al. (2014) examined the dependence of

M. Haghani, M. Sarvi / Transportation Research Part B 107 (2018) 253–294 259
this effect on the density of ants and showed that the imbalance in the use of exits increased and then decreased as the
density of ants increased. In the most recent study of this phenomenon, Wang et al. (2016) also observed symmetry breaking in ant experiments. They also showed that ant escape was most efficient when the exits were further apart, and that
unlike humans, ants did not display jamming around exits.
Another phenomenon that has been rather extensively studied using animal experiments has been the “faster-is-slower”
effect which refers to a counter-intuitive situation where an elevated desire (or motivation) for escape may actually hinder
the escape efficiency and make it slower. This was also first observed and suggested by the theoretical study of Helbing et
al. (2000) based on computer simulations of escape of crowds (based on social force notion) from a single door. We know
of four studies that have addressed this particular topic with the use of animal experiments. Soria et al. (2012) pioneered
by using different and measured concentrations of chemicals as repellent in their ant experiments to create different levels of urgency and motivation to escape from the chamber. They showed that a minimum evacuation time was observed
for an intermediate level of repellent chemical concentration that was found compatible with the theoretical observation
of Helbing et al. (2000). However, in a follow-up publication they revisited their earlier finding and suggested that their
previous observation had actually been “of a different nature to that predicted by the social force model” (Parisi et al., 2015)
and suggested their previous conclusion as an artefact of ants’ “uniform distribution over space”. In drawing that conclusion, they argued based on observing ants not producing high-density area and jamming effect at exits (an observation also
mentioned by Wang et al. (2016), as discussed previously). Further to this study, Boari et al. (2013) also investigated this
phenomenon using ants this time utilising controlled levels of heat as a measured proxy for “stress” during escape (instead
of the use of chemicals). Their study resulted in findings that actually contradicted the concept of “faster is slower”. They
observed that higher temperature led to faster evacuations. Similar to the findings of the studies mentioned above, they also
reported that they did not observe jamming and clogging near exits. In the light of this observation, they recommended that
ant experiments cannot be reliably used as an accurate model of competitive egress dynamics in humans. In addition to the
abovementioned studies, two more studies have been reported recently both providing strong evidence in favour of the validity of the faster-is-slower assumption. One is the study of Pastor et al. (2015) in which they observed the recordings from
the ingress behaviour of sheep in a farm under two different motivation levels. They used observations in two different seasons (i.e. associating with two different weather temperatures) that is claimed to have caused different levels of movement
vigour. In their study, the level of motivation was not continuously measurable (like the amount of heat or chemical in a
fully experimental condition) thus not allowing a continuous plot of egress time (here, ingress time) versus the motivation
level. Yet, their observations based on these two distinct levels of motivation or competitiveness level was consistent with
the “faster-is-slower” assumption. In the most recent study on this topic, Lin et al. (2016) used mice and “joss stick” as
adverse stimulus. They reported that their finding verified the “faster-is-slower” effect. However, it should be noted that the
original observation of this effect based on simulated self-driven particles as models of humans produced a non-monotonic
effect, whereas the observation of Lin et al. (2016) indicated a rather monotonic relation between the escape time and the
degree of stimulus.
A large body of research using animal models of escape has looked at the effect of architectural features of the escape
environment and their impact on the movement efficiency. In a number of interconnected studies of ant experiments, it was
suggested that the presence of a round column near the exit can enhance the overall efficiency of ants’ escape and that the
placement of the exit at the corner (as opposed to the middle of the room side) also makes a significant difference (Burd
et al., 2010; Shiwakoti and Sarvi, 2013b; Shiwakoti et al., 2009, 2011a). In two other interrelated studies, the finding about
obstacle effect in front of exit was also observed in sheep flocks (Zuriguel et al., 2016; Zuriguel et al., 2014). The study of
Zuriguel et al. (2016) particularly looked at the effect of obstacle position and showed that positioning an obstacle in front
of an exit as a design strategy relies on its position. Depending on the distance of the obstacle to the exit, the outcome
could vary from beneficial to detrimental.
Another stream of studies related to the notion of geometric design has investigated the effect of corridor configuration
in angled and merging passages using ant models of collective motion (Dias et al., 2012; Dias et al., 2013; Shahhoseini et
al., 2016). The observations of Dias et al. (2012) indicated that right-angled egress paths decrease the flow rate significantly
compared with straight pathways of the same dimension. Further to this finding, Dias et al. (2013) also observed that higher
turning angles can reduce flow rates significantly. In relation to the efficiency of flow in merging corridors, Shahhoseini et
al. (2016) observed that symmetric angle setups significantly outperformed the asymmetric setups (with equal angle). They
also observed that the flow rate displayed a dependency on the angle between the two merging corridors but the effect did
not prove to be monotonic indicating that the optimum angle may be at intermediate levels.

experiments, similar to animal studies, in that they take place in temporary setups (although often not a conventional
laboratory) and under the influence of the experimenters’ manipulation of certain design factors. However, compared to
the virtual reality experiments (that are also of laboratory nature), subjects make actual movements and feel the presence
of others as real individuals as well as the physical setup surrounding them. Moreover, with the advancements in image
processing and video tracking techniques (Boltes and Seyfried, 2013), the behaviour of subjects in such experiments can be
monitored and analysed with a great deal of precision. From this perspective, this class of experiments can be regarded as
a reasonable compromise between pure laboratory and fully natural field observation benefiting from certain advantages of
both approaches to certain degrees. The literature shows a steep rise in the use of this class of experiments since 2008 and
studies based on this method represent the biggest body of empirical research on the topic of our interest in terms of the
quantity of publications.
The full flexibility and great precision of tracking individuals in these laboratory setups makes them particularly useful
for the analysis of “walking behaviour” which offers implications for crowd management and control, as well as modelling
for both normal and emergency contexts. Moussaïd et al. (2009) performed a series of controlled experiments of “collision
avoidance” tasks and investigated the change of direction and speed of pedestrians for various interaction distances and
angles. Their observations were also used in a subsequent study as a benchmark for model verification (Moussaïd et al.,
2011). Similarly, Seitz et al. (2016) proposed simple heuristics for explaining social interactions in pedestrians’ movements
and used aggregate measures of behaviour derived from a series of laboratory experiments to test the validity of their
assumptions. Asano et al. (2010) also utilised observations of “crossing (i.e. intersecting) pedestrian flows” for validation of
their proposed walking behaviour model using macro-scale measures (e.g. speed-flow relationships). In another laboratory
study, Moussaid et al. (2012) examined self-organised “lane formation” phenomenon in bidirectional flows of pedestrians.
Their findings suggested that the formation and breakdown of self-organised lanes depends heavily on the inter-individual
variation of walking speed.
A class of studies have also used laboratory experiments to understand architecturally-imposed features of pedestrians’
movements in various designs including movements in angled, crossing (i.e. intersecting) and merging corridors. The experiments of Dias et al. (2014b) demonstrated the speed reduction tendency of pedestrians in angled corridors within a region
with fixed dimensions independent of the turning angle. The dimensions of this region however proved to vary depending
on the initial speeds (Dias et al., 2015). Shiwakoti et al. (2015) examined the influence of merging architectural features on
movements of pedestrian flows. Their findings also demonstrated that pedestrians tended to reduce their speed in merging
regions which resulted in the reduction of flow rate particularly for larger merging angles. It was suggested by the study of
Shi et al. (2016) that merging angle had a significant influence on fluctuations of pedestrian flows. Another study on merging
flows has also shown the dependency of the merging angle as well as the angle configuration (i.e. symmetric versus asymmetric setups) on the efficiency of flow (Shahhoseini et al., 2017). However, unlike the study of Shiwakoti et al. (2015) the
findings suggested by experiments reported in Shahhoseini et al. (2017) did not indicate a monotonic pattern of dependency
between the flow rate and the merging angle. Zhang et al. (2013) specifically looked at the 180-degrees merging flows, also
known as “T-junctions”, in experimental setups and based on deriving fundamental diagrams in three different regions. They
showed that the fundamental diagrams of the two merging branches matched, whereas the velocities in the third branch
(i.e. merged corridor) were significantly lower compared with those of the upstream at same densities. The movement of
pedestrian crowds in intersecting corridors and four-directional movements have also been studied in experiments of Wong
et al. (2010) and Lian et al. (2015) respectively, with particular focuses on model validation and deriving velocity–density
relations.
Another major stream of research using laboratory techniques have looked at the characteristics of pedestrian flows
through bottlenecks and narrow exits. Seyfried et al. (2009) studied unidirectional pedestrian flows through bottlenecks with
varying widths and showed a linear growth of the flow with width. The experiment and findings of Tian et al. (2012) also
provided evidence as to the linear increase of the flow rate with the bottleneck width. Tobias et al. (2006) also suggested a
linear decline of the specific flux with increasing widths for narrow bottlenecks (i.e. bottlenecks that allow one person at a
time only) and a constant value for larger bottleneck widths. Daamen and Hoogendoorn (2012b) used experimental data for
estimating the influence of door width, population composition and stress level on the capacity of exit doors. Their observations were also employed for calibration of a simulation model in another study (Daamen and Hoogendoorn, 2012a). By
instructing subjects to exit a narrow doorway in competitive and non-competitive ways, Garcimartín et al. (2014) substantiated the validity of the “faster-is-slower” assumption based on empirical evidence. In the most recent study on this topic,
Nicolas et al. (2017) examined the effect of individual behaviour on the flow by instructing “selfish” behaviour to a fraction
of subjects while others were prescribed to behave “politely”. They identified the emergence of different flow characteristics
as the fraction of selfish behaviour increased.
A substantial number of the laboratory studies have also been dedicated to the concept of fundamental diagram. Armin
et al. (2005) examined this for single-file movement showing a linear relation between velocity and the inverse density.
Seyfried et al. (2010) revealed a large variation across flow-density relations derived from different measurement methods
in different studies. On this account, Zhang et al. (2011) compared four different measurement method and their effects on
the shape of fundamental diagram for “straight” and “T-junction” corridors. The study of Jelic´ et al. (2012) suggested that
the linear relation between instantaneous velocity and spatial headway assumed by their predecessor studies is not fully
accurate by stating that the relation is rather “piecewise linear” and that this effect would be appreciated should one covers

M. Haghani, M. Sarvi / Transportation Research Part B 107 (2018) 253–294 263
a large range of density in their observations. A study of fundamental diagram for bidirectional flows can also be found in
Zhang et al. (2012a).
A different theme of research, mostly recent, has been conducted using laboratory experiments to understand “wayfinding behaviour” and “navigational decision making”. In two interrelated study, the ability of an informed minority to lead
a naïve group without obvious signalling (when the group is instructed to maintain their coherence while navigating)
was discussed as the core finding (Dyer et al., 2008; Dyer et al., 2009). In a subsequent and similar study, Faria et al.
(2009) demonstrated how “the many-wrongs principle” (predicting that group cohesion can mitigate individual direction
error and improve group navigational accuracy compared to singletons) was moderated by the group size and the level of
directional uncertainty. The study of Faria et al. (2010) examined “leadership” and the use of “social information” during
navigation of human crowds and demonstrated group ability to identify the directionally informed leader.
The particular problem of “exit choice” behaviour in human crowds has also recently attracted an increasing attention as
a sub-topic of navigation and wayfinding. Haghani and Sarvi (2017a) revisited the assumption of “herd behaviour” discussed
in the previous section and provided evidence showing that the “imitative behaviour” assumption is not applicable to all
contexts of emergency (in contradiction to the findings emerged from ant experiments which we discussed previously). In
a subsequent study based on their experiments of emergency evacuations, the validity of the “social attraction–repulsion
force” categorisation based on inter-individual distance was scrutinised (Haghani & Sarvi 2017c). They showed that spatial
distance was not a perfectly accurate criteria for defining the direction or the magnitude of the social forces in that crowds
can be in far distance and yet act strongly as repulsion forces whereas they may be near the individual and act as attraction
force. Also, Haghani and Sarvi (2017b) looked at the nature of the individual variations in perception of peer behaviour
during evacuations and showed that this variation was more of discrete nature than continuum. In another experiment,
Wagoum et al. (2017) studied the “queuing behaviour” at exits and showed that pedestrians use time-dependant information
to optimise their exit choice. In a follow-up study based on this experiment, Liao et al. (2017) showed that pedestrians
performed trade-offs between exit widths and anticipated crowdedness level in making their decisions. The significance and
the presence of multi-attribute trade-offs in escape decision making has also been emphasised in previous studies (Haghani
and Sarvi, 2017c).
Two studies have reported on addressing the influence of “social groups” in the crowd evacuation process using laboratory experiments. In the study of Bode et al. (2015c), the egress time and pre-movement time was of main interest. The
authors showed that the presence of social groups in the crowd increased egress time. They also showed that compared to
individuals, groups were slower in making a movement response as well as reaching the exits. The study of von Krüchten
and Schadschneider (2017) also showed that increasing the group size can give rise to longer evacuation times.

5.3. Virtual reality and hypothetical-choice experiments
The use of virtual reality techniques offers another alternative for performing perfectly safe and non-invasive experiments
in the context of human crowds and evacuations. It presents stronger components of laboratory experiments compared with
the experiments that involve actual groups of subjects and real movements. While using human subjects, the experimenter
can cover a relatively wide range of topics and can also overcome the necessity of dealing with tens of subjects at the
same time and in a same place. The method offers great flexibility in terms of the type of geometrical setups that the
experimenter can introduce to the subject. The experimenter is in perfect control of the variation of the design factors and
the type of response (e.g. what exactly the subject has to do, when the subject has to make a decision, what type of decision
subject can make etc.) can all be predetermined in the design. This feature (i.e. the full control of variation and response),
to large degrees, has to be compromised in experiments with groups of people in actual physical setups.
Recent advances in realistic “three-dimensional” and “immersive” visualisations of environments together with the possibility of designing interactive tasks has further enhanced the potential applicability and popularity of this approach. A major
concern about the use of this method has been the limitations that it imposes in regard to the perception of other individuals (often simulated and animated agents) in such experiments that may be majorly different from that of real-world
crowds. However, promising progress has recently started to emerge as to the possibility of using “shared”, “multi-user” experiments (Moussaïd et al., 2016) making the potential relevance of this method more prominent than ever before. Also, the
level of subjects’ engagement, whether or not they can realistically relate to the hypothesised context and whether or not
they are motivated enough for performing the tasks (in the same way as they would do in an actual scenario in real world)
has been another major concern. However, recent studies have provided promising evidence suggesting that this issue may
not be as prohibitive as we used to assume before (Haghani and Sarvi, 2016b, 2017d; Moussaïd et al., 2016) and that this
method can produce behavioural patterns that are observable in real world.

facilitate evacuees’ wayfinding. They showed that the absence of evacuation signs resulted in insignificantly longer evacuation times in a virtual building. Occhialini et al. (2016) made use of the neurological activity analysis for evaluating the

The perception of “social influence” has also been rather extensively studied using this method. Based on experiments
of virtual tunnel fire, Kinateder et al. (2014b) observed that the presence and movement of another virtual agent affected
subject’s route choice but not their destination choice. Their subjects displayed a tendency for choosing the route taken
by the virtual agent. In a follow-up study and again in the similar context of virtual tunnel evacuation, Kinateder et al.
(2014a) differentiated between the solo condition versus the social influence condition. Within the social influence context,
they also differentiated between the passive and active behaviour of the virtual agent as well as the conflict between social
clue and the direction to emergency exit. The notion of social influence has also been examined using hypothetical discrete
choice experiments (Haghani and Sarvi, 2016c; Lovreglio et al., 2016). The study of Lovreglio et al. (2016) suggested that the
occurrence of herd behaviour depends both on environmental and personal factors, and Haghani and Sarvi (2016c) showed
that formation of herd tendency also depends on the level of uncertainty in the escape environment. Their findings suggested that “unless evacuees face certain levels of uncertainty, heavy flows of evacuees are unlikely to be followed, rather
the central tendency would be to avoid them.”
The topic of “exit choice” has also been closely regarded with the notion of social influence as it is believed that social
interaction is a major factor in exit decision making. In fact this has arguably been the most popular research question with
the use of virtual reality in this context. In a number of pioneer and inter-related studies, this topic was addressed using
interactive computer-based experiments visualised in two dimensions while subjects had to interact with virtual agents
while controlling their own (Bode et al., 2014, 2015b; Bode and Codling, 2013). In these studies the authors did not find
an inherent preference for familiar routes. It was also shown that under stressful treatments, subject more likely made
decisions detrimental to their evacuation time and was also less willing to adapt and change their decision in response to
the congestion. It was shown how individuals attended and reacted to multiple sources of directional information at the
same time by making trade-off between them. Also, Bode et al. (2014) suggested in particular that the observed differences
in behaviour could not be explained based on observable demographics like age and gender.
In another study, Haghani and Sarvi (2016b) confirmed the aforementioned observation at least in relation to gender.
Their findings suggested that the individual differences might be more closely related to latent characteristics of different people. In their study they identified only two major classes (i.e. types) of behaviour relevant to exit choice that can
sufficiently accommodate the inter-individual variation. Using hypothetical-choice methods, Haghani and Sarvi (2016a) also
investigated the major differences in exit choice behaviour under normal and emergency conditions. It was suggested by this
study that the non-emergency exit choice was predominantly determined by minimising distance, and that social interaction
effect was more meaningfully relevant to the emergency context (in conjunction with time-independent factors (like spatial
distance)). The use of hypothetical choices has also been reported in the studies of Lovreglio et al. (2014) and Duives and
Mahmassani (2012), where the latter has focused primarily on implementing discrete choice models in a crowd simulation
platform and contrasting the resultant behaviour with pre-fixed strategies (e.g. least distance path). Also in a study linked to
evacuation of high-rise buildings, the authors showed how a simple system of flashing light can influence people’s choices
(Andrée et al., 2016). And finally, relevant to this method, a number of studies have taken a relatively different perspective
by looking at the topics such as “social identification” as well as “altruism” and “cooperation” during emergencies (Bode et
al., 2015a; Drury et al., 2009c; Moussaïd and Trauernicht, 2016), as well as the escape “judgment” and “decision making”
(Li et al., 2016; Li et al., 2015) as connections between our context and topics in social sciences and psychology. Although
the wealth of knowledge in this area is limited, these studies have provided evidence in support of the emergence of collective identity and solidarity with strangers and cooperative tendencies during disasters. The study of Li et al. (2016) also
presented evidence showing that people less prefer to follow the crowd in escape scenarios and that “intuitive thinking”
presents a more significant influence on judgment and decision making than “deliberative thinking”. Table 4 synthesises the
details of the experiments discussed above.
5.4. Evacuation drill experiments
Evacuation drills take place in real venues and actual buildings as opposed to the controlled lab-type experiments in
which the physical structure is often a temporary setup designed by experimenters. Drill experiments however represent
some elements of laboratory too in that subjects often are aware that they are about to be tested (at least in announced
drills). As a result, the observations cannot be regarded as fully natural. The method also offers relatively less amount of
flexibility in terms of the manipulation of the control and design factors particularly when it comes to the structure of the
physical environment. Repetition is also another issue as in many cases the experimenters have had to settle with one-off
observations given that running repeated scenarios in real buildings often poses logistic challenges. In terms of the monitoring and surveillance, this method may not offer as much precision and details as the laboratory setups since there may be

“exit choice behaviour”, “pre-evacuation time estimate” and “movement speed on stairs”.
For the study of exit choice, Heliövaara et al. (2012) performed experiments in a corridor with two exits located asymmetrically and used macroscopic measures of behaviour for their analysis. The experiments revealed the possible suboptimality of evacuees’ exit decisions in non-trivial scenarios. Their observations also suggested that the evacuation time
of the group was shorter when instructed to behave “egoistically” as opposed to behaving “cooperatively”. This topic was
also investigated in the evacuation experiments of Fang et al. (2010) staged in a teaching building. Their observations were
indicative of a strong preference for choosing the nearest exit even when an alternative exit was only slightly further away.
They used their observations in this experiment as a benchmark for calibrating a certain parameter of their proposed simulation model. Liu et al. (2009a) also performed experiments in a classroom with obstacles primarily for validating a simulation
model that takes the effect of occupants’ density around exits into account (in addition to spatial distances to exits). Their
validation was based on the distribution of evacuation times. Guo et al. (2012) also performed evacuation drills in a classroom under two general conditions of “intact and zero visibility” whose observations were also used for model validation.
They looked at factors such as effect of capacity, inter-individual interactions, following behaviour and evacuation efficiency.
They also based their validation analysis on the distribution of evacuation times. Fridolf et al. (2013b) studied exit choice behaviour phenomenon with 100 subjects in a tunnel demonstrating the importance of emergency exit designs. They showed
how verbal guidance alarm can prove effective in attracting people to exits in tunnel evacuations. In another tunnel evacuation study, Fridolf et al. (2016) again observed evidence that substantiated their previous finding about the effectiveness of
loud speakers that broadcast voice messages. These two studies also provided useful information and estimates on people’s
movement speed in tunnel evacuations. In another tunnel evacuation study, Nilsson et al. (2009) found that information
signs were also important for the choice to initiate the evacuation. They also found the social influence to be important
for both pre-evacuation and exit choice decisions. Kobes et al. (2010b) staged unannounced drill evacuations in a hotel and
investigated the possible influence of smoke and low placed exit signs on occupants’ response. They showed that in the
absence of smoke, participants mostly escaped via main exits whereas when smoke was introduced to the environment, the
majority chose fire exits. Their observations also demonstrated the positive influence of low placed exit signs for guiding
occupants to the nearest fire exit.
The notion of pre-movement and pre-evacuation time has been among the relatively less explored topics in crowd
evacuation in general. The evacuation drill experiments prove to be the technique through which most of the knowledge
about this aspect has been generated (Fridolf et al., 2013a; Proulx, 1995; Shields and Boyce, 2000). Nilsson and Johansson
(2009) reported on analysing the data from an evacuation drill (performed by a predecessor study) in a cinema theatre.
The main question of their analysis was to see how people are influenced by others during initial phases of an evacuation.
They differentiated between “recognition” and “pre-movement” time in their measurements. They realised that the social
influence played a prominent role particularly when the fire alarm was unclear or uninformative. They also suggested the
presence of a dependency between the social influence and the inter-individual distance with the influence becoming less
strong from people in further distances than people in closer proximities. In a follow-up study, Lovreglio et al. (2015) used
the same dataset to calibrate a discrete choice model for decision-making during pre-evacuation. The model produced probabilities of choosing to start “investigating” and “evacuating” and takes into account both physical and environmental factors
along with the personal characteristics. Xudong et al. (2009) also performed an announced evacuation in a retail store with
two floors and five emergency exits. Their observations emphasised on the importance of the pre-movement time as a
determining factor of the total evacuation time.
A class of evacuation drill experiments have taken place in high-rise buildings with a main focus placed on investigating
behaviour and speed of occupants in stairwells (Ronchi et al., 2014). Peacock et al. (2012) collected an extensive stairwell
movement data during fire drill evacuations of office buildings and estimated empirical ranges for the local and overall
speed. They observed a wide variation of the local movement speed within given stairwells. In another study in high-rise
buildings, Fang et al. (2012) identified three determining factors for downward speed on stairs. These factors are “merging
behaviour in the entrance buffer”, “participants’ strength” and “visibility in the stairwell”. Ma et al. (2012) looked at this
problem in ultra-high-rise buildings. They differentiated between single pedestrian movement and mass evacuations in their
experiments and measured mean speed and evacuation times. Related to the topic of movement speed estimate, Fridolf et
al. (2014a) investigated the impact of smoke on walking speed and showed how the level of smoke concentration led to a
reduction on walking speed. They reported that they found no significant effect for factors such as type of floor material,
age, gender and height.

5.5. Analysis of natural disasters
In addition to the use of experimental methods discussed earlier, it is also possible to get insight from the natural behaviour of people in emergencies or learn lessons from analysing the behaviour in the lead-up to past crowd incidents in
order to identify underlying causes. This fully overcomes the concern about the lack of reliability of observations that can
be levelled at almost all experimental methods to varying degrees. Yet, it offers by comparison less flexibility in terms of
the replicability, availability and abundance of observations, and the accuracy of measurements as well as the range of phenomena that can be investigated using such data. First of all, the data often has to be made available by authorities to the
researcher which may not be an easy process. Second, the observations are one-off and there is no way for the analyst to
replicate them and see whether the same behaviour will emerge. This makes it rather difficult to infer robust statistics and
disentangle random aspects from the inherent features of behaviour. Thirdly, unlike the controlled experiments, the analyst
cannot vary any of the factors and explore their influence nor can they be guaranteed a full overview of the area of the
interest by the surveillance cameras. And lastly, such observations are subject to higher degrees of measurement error in
terms of dimensions, movement trajectories etc.
Helbing et al. (2007) pointed out that “for ethical reasons there is a serious lack of experimental data regarding crowd
panic”. They analysed video footage of the 2006 crowd disaster during Hajj pilgrimage and examined critical crowd conditions. They identified sudden transitions from laminar to turbulent flows (that involve stop-and-go waves). They also argued
that this transition is not readily replicable by current crowd simulation models (at least at the time of their publication).
They suggested this phenomenon to be a cause of sudden displacements and trampling of people and that better understanding of this phenomenon may allow one to identify circumstances when crowd disasters tend or are about to occur.
The 2010 Love Parade crowd incident in Germany has been referenced as a benchmark for a number of studies that have
taken this approach. Helbing and Mukerji (2012) revisited the causality of crowd disasters via qualitative analysis of the
video recording of the 2010 Love Parade. Their main interest was understanding whether crowd incidents are necessarily
caused by “relentless behaviour of people” and “mass panic” or they can happen due to the “breakdown of coordination”
without any “bad intention” of people or external threats. Their case of analysis revealed “crowd turbulence” (or “crowd
quake”) as the reason that led to people pile up over each other in this case. This suggest that it is crucial to distinguish
this from crowd incidents that may occur because of the “psychological state of panic”. In another post-disaster study of
Love Parade 2010, Krausz and Bauckhage (2012) presented an automatic, video-based analysis of the event and introduce a
system capable of detecting motion patterns that characterise crowd stampedes. They propose that such techniques can be
utilised for detection and early warning of critical conditions at mass gatherings.
Another stream of studies based on this method has commonly looked at the available video recordings (mostly surveillance camera and CCTV footage of buildings) during earthquakes. By investigating the footage recorded during a magnitude
8.0 earthquake in China, Yang et al. (2011) investigated the difference between “real-life escape panic” and simulated experiments. Their main conclusion was that the relation between arrival times and order of people arriving shows a nonlinear
pattern which they claimed to be different from simulated exercises where this relation appears linear. D’ Orazio et al.
(2014) proposed an agent-based modelling approach to earthquake evacuation which describes different phases and rules
of pedestrians’ motion. They derived their approach based on analyses of earthquake footage. They also provided empirical
estimates of distance between people and average evacuation speed in outdoor motion. In another similar study, Bernardini
et al. (2016) used an extensive amount of earthquake footage in order to identify step-by-step behavioural stages that are
activated during earthquake evacuations. They also provided empirical estimates for motion quantities such as speed and
acceleration and fundamental diagram. Gu et al. (2016) analysed evacuation behaviour of school students during earthquakes
and compared that with the behaviour under normal condition. Their main conclusion was that the behaviour under normal
condition was “stable” and that cumulative number of departures show a linear trend whereas they found the evacuation
behaviour “chaotic”. Also they reported that their analysis did not support the notion of “faster-is-slower” in emergency
evacuations. As the most recent study of this kind, Shiwakoti (2016) performed a quantitative comparison of emergency
escape with laboratory and experimental data for crowd egress at a bottleneck. They found underlying differences between
the headway distribution and flow under real and experimental conditions and suggested that pedestrian evacuation models
should be evaluated based on their ability to produce “fundamental headway distribution” that they observed in their study.

analysed along with the dates and locations (wherever reported), as well as the phenomena they investigated, their measured variables and main conclusions.

In an influential study, Antonini et al. (2006) proposed a discrete choice modelling framework of pedestrian “walking behaviour”. The model presents a radial discretisation of the movement space and takes into account a range of factors such as
“occupation”, “direction”, “destination” and “angle” for predicting the choice of next step at each given point in time. They
used individual-level video recording data of walking for estimating parameters of this model. In a subsequent publication,
Robin et al. (2009) further refined this model by accommodating behavioural factors such as “leader–follower” and “correlation between alternatives”. The model went through a further validation process based on more datasets of natural (and
experimental) walking. A similar approach of discrete choice modelling and estimation based on video-generated individuallevel choice data can be found in the study of Xie et al. (2016) with minor differences in terms of model specification and
formulation.
In another influential study, Moussaïd et al. (2010) made use of datasets of this type to investigate the impact of the
presence of “social groups” on walking behaviour. They identified different spatial patterns of groups that depend on crowd
density level. In another study on the topic of social groups, Do et al. (2016) provided evidence showing that individual
were more likely to change or adjust their trajectories than groups. Their evidence also suggested that in higher densities,
the likelihood of groups splitting increased. Zeng et al. (2014) employed pedestrian trajectory manually extracted from video
recordings at an intersection in Japan and used them for calibration of a set of parameters for a modified social force model.
They disentangled their parameters to those directly estimable (or more accurately, measurable) and those that have to be
estimated via a maximum likelihood procedure. The calibration of social force model based on this type of data has also
been reported in Rudloff et al. (2011). They compared three calibration methods for a modified social force model and
investigated the sensitivity of the model parameters to these methods as well as their transferability to different scenarios.
By taking a different approach compared to the studies discussed above, Zhang et al. (2012b) analysed video recordings
of a mass crowd at macro-scale level. Similar to the study of Helbing et al. (2007), they also identified two critical flow
conditions, “laminar” flow and “stop-and-go” waves and made analogies between crowd flows and those of fluids in pipes.

5.7. Post-disaster interview with survivors
In addition to the use of experimentation or observation, it is also possible to obtain information from those who have
been involved emergency cases (like terror attacks, fires etc.) and have personal experiences in that regard. This particular
method is also a certain combination of field and laboratory in that people have been in the real-world scenario but the
information is structured by how the analyst designs the interview as well as the type of questions that are asked from
interviewees. Although rare insight can be obtained by means of this method that are otherwise impossible to receive from
an experimental setups, one can express scepticism about the reliability of the information provided by the subjects. The
extent to which they can remember the event that they have experienced, the extent to which they try or are willing to
provide accurate information and the extent to which their responses are influenced by the wording and the structure of
questions are all largely unknown. Whether they try to figure out and give the interviewer the type of answers that they
seek (i.e. the answers that they perceive to be “correct answers”) is also unclear. Some of these shortcomings are inherent
to this method and not readily fixable but some can be alleviated through a fine interview design. In terms of the costs
and logistics, this method is not so cheap for clear challenges involved in finding and accessing survivors’ details, contacting
them and having them accept the interview that makes reference to an unpleasant experience in their past. Moreover, the
range of phenomena that can be investigated is quite limited. For example, there cannot be any information relating to
movement speed and factors as such.
Using this method, a number of studies have looked at the concepts of “mass panic”, “social identification”, “collective
resilience” and “altruism” during emergencies (Cocking et al., 2009; Drury et al., 2009a, 2009b; von Sivers et al., 2016).
Drury et al. (2009b) interviewed survivors of the 2005 London bombing to investigate the prevalence of “mass panic” and
“selfish behaviour” as opposed to “collective resilience” and “helping” tendencies. According to their study, “selfish” behaviour was found to be rare and altruism tendencies was found more prevalent. The authors attributed this to a sense of
unity and shared perception of danger among many individuals. They also offered an explanation of this evidence based on
the theory of “self-categorisation” (from “me” to “us”) as an outcome of the elevated level of concerns for others. The data
from this study was later on used as a benchmark for structuring an algorithmic “social identity” model by von Sivers et al.
(2016) which can be regarded as one of the few attempts of incorporating psychological models into computer models of
pedestrian motion. They replicated the 2005 London bombing in a simulated environment using this method. The findings
of a number of subsequent similar studies in which the survivors of the 2005 London bombing and other emergencies were
approached further corroborated the idea that “mass panic” should be considered as a myth for which there is no solid
evidence and that crowds can be expected to behave in more social ways in emergencies than previously thought (Cocking
et al., 2009; Drury et al., 2009a).

perfectly fall in our categories in that it did not target any survivors of a past event. Alnabulsi and Drury (2014) performed a
large-scale survey of pilgrims on the Hajj to Mecca and tested the hypothesis that the effect of density on (perceived) safety
can vary depending on whether the person has a shared sense of identification with the crowd. Their results manifested the
moderating effect of the “social identification with the crowd” on how density affects reported safety, by showing that high

6. Advantages and disadvantages of the data collection methods
In the previous sections, we provided short discussions on the main features of various data collection methods relevant
to each category based on certain criteria such as the degree to which they belong to the “laboratory” or “field” setting
(we think of this as a fuzzy belonging), the level of control that they offer over the variation (of design factors), the level
of control over the responses of people (when to make decisions, what decisions to make etc.), the level of contextual and
environmental realism, whether the observations are readily replicable, and the range of possible topics that they offer for
investigation. Another concern is always the logistics and financial costs of performing an experiment as researchers are
often restricted by pre-defined budget amounts. Also we believe that the process of data extraction using certain methods
(like virtual reality technique in which you may get your data almost immediately after surveying your sample) are far easier
than others (like the laboratory experiments that often necessitate a perfect location for camera installation, having subjects
wear coloured caps and a relatively longer procedure of image processing and trajectory extraction afterwards whose time
can vary considerably depending on the technique and type of data extraction).
Here we put different methods of data collection into contrast based on these criteria consistent with what we elaborated
on earlier about each individual method. We believe that there may be no quantitative measure and clear definitions for
assessing different methods based on these features, yet it is possible to put them into perspective based on relative and
qualitative scales. Table 9 is intended to provide such information about methods in an abstract format.
Based on the information presented in this table, it can be observed that the empirical methods in this field vary on
this qualitative scale in terms of the level of “laboratory” or “field” features that they represent. Some methods, like animal
experiments for example, present stronger components of a laboratory setting and share less similarity with field-type data
(relative to other methods, like video analysis of real emergencies for example). Certain methods reside at the two ends
of this spectrum. However, methods like experiments with actual crowds in artificial environments or evacuation drill experiments and even the post-disaster interviews present a mixture of laboratory and field observation features. Evacuation
drills for example, take place in real locations but often the subjects are aware of the fact that they are participating in an
experiment and do not face a real danger. Survivor interviewees have experienced real-life threats but recall the context in
a laboratory setting and within the framework of the questions designed by the researcher. Controlled laboratory experiments with groups of humans are for example of less laboratory nature and are closer to a field context than virtual-reality
experiments in that people actually perceive the presence of other individuals as well as the physical environment, yet the
presence of laboratory features is undeniable.

the field of crowd behaviour in that they convey an appreciation of the fundamental differences that these methods present
(as oppose to referring to all first four methods in Table 9 as “laboratory” methods and the three others as “field” methods
which simply neglects their underlying differences). As we move towards the “field” end of the spectrum among these seven
methods (i.e. as we move downwards in Table 9), the methods generally display more contextual and environmental realism
but less control over the variation (and often less control over the responses of the subjects) (Falk and Heckman, 2009).
As an example, in a virtual-reality experiment of wayfinding, the researcher is in perfect control of the variation (of
the design factors) in that he/she can determine what scenario with what characteristics the subjects should face (like,
how they get influenced by the animated humans in the environment), what kind of actions the subject should take (their
task, like making an exit choice) and (possibly) at which moment they should take an action (the decision moment could
be specified in advance). In terms of the variation, the experimenter determines perfectly through the design that which
design factor(s) should change from scenario A to scenario B, in what direction and to what degrees. Whereas, in an equivalent experiment with real human groups in an actual environments, these actions might take place more naturally but
(depending on the design, the intended analysis (whether it is based on individual-level or macro-scale measure) and the
investigated phenomenon) the experimenter may have to compromise the perfect and direct control over the type of responses and the variation of the analysis variables to certain (and varying) degrees (depending on the specific experiment).
He/she may not for example be able to exactly determine how many people should go where so that individual X faces
the exact same scenario that the experimenter requires. This issue would of course be more closely felt if an individuallevel (i.e. micro-scale) analysis is intended and could be largely mitigated if the experimenter simply focuses on analysing
aggregate measures (such as total evacuation time, or exit utilisation). In the former case, having great variability in the
dataset may become of the experimenter(s) primary interest whereas in the latter case, maximum repetition could be the
most desired feature (for an example, one may refer to and compare the route choice experiments reported in Bode and
Codling (2013) and Haghani and Sarvi (2017a). In the second reference the experimenters had to let go of any direct control
over the analysis variables (performed at the level of individuals) and resorted to a number of indirect “control” factors just
to ensure maximum variation in the dataset). The level of control over the variability and the responses may even further
diminish if the experimenter decides to perform his/her experiment as an evacuation drill in a real building in which case

M. Haghani, M. Sarvi / Transportation Research Part B 107 (2018) 253–294 283
major changes to the environmental structure (from scenario A to scenario B) could become impossible and the number of
possible repetitions may need to be restricted to a small number (maybe even to a one-off observation).
It is also evident from Table 9 that there is no single method that outperforms all other methods. Each method presents
certain advantages and certain disadvantages, in addition to the fact that some research questions can only be practically
addressed using certain empirical methods. For instance, the aspects of behaviour related to walking behaviour are best
represented by field video observations and controlled laboratory experiments and do not show a reasonable fit to the
virtual-reality or interview techniques, for example. As another example, methods such as animal experiments or video
analysis of walking do not display any real potential for exploring psychological aspects such as altruism or social identity.
Therefore, we cannot think of single methods as superior to others nor do we tend to recommend any of them as substitutes
for other methods. Rather, it appears that they should be utilised in a complementary fashion for the accumulation of
knowledge in this field.
7. Categorising human decision-making levels
From an individual behaviour analysis perspective and with an acceptable level of accuracy, all aspects of humans’ behaviour in crowds can be interpreted as a certain class or level of decision making. From this point of view, three general
levels of decisions have been recognised in order to differentiate between various behavioural phenomena. This include (1)
strategic-level decisions describing “what-to-do” actions and “the choice of activity” (which in case of an emergency could
be associated with the decision of “when to initiate the evacuation” or “whether to help others or not”), (2) tactical-level
decisions describing “where-to-go” actions and the choice of route or exit (assuming that a strategic decision has been
made already), and (3) operational-level decisions describing “how-to-get-to-target” actions and the momentary choice of
step made by each pedestrian in order to reach the goals defined by the decisions at two higher levels while avoiding
collision with others and obstacles. This taxonomy of behaviour has recently gained widespread acceptance and increasing
popularity in the literature of pedestrian crowd modelling and many authors have recognised the usefulness of such categorisation in their publications (Asano et al., 2010; Bode and Codling, 2013; Duives and Mahmassani, 2012; Haghani et al.,
2014; Hoogendoorn and Bovy, 2004; Liao et al., 2017).
It is clear that the behaviour that emerges at the aggregate or collective level can be regarded as the cumulative outcome
of these three levels of decisions made by individuals as constituent elements of the crowd. In that sense, it is crucial for any
self-sufficient modelling or simulation tool to firstly accommodate all these types of decisions (otherwise, the information
may need to be imported from another source or need to be assumed by the analyst, given that the decision from higher
levels often are influential inputs for the decision(s) made at lower levels.). Secondly, such model would need to present
a consistent level of accuracy at all three levels. Clearly, a model cannot replicate the steps of pedestrians in a perfectly
accurate way while incapable of reproducing exit choices and yet be regarded as an ideal model. The overall accuracy of the
model is obviously determined by the realism at all three levels.
Studies in which the behaviour at the level of individuals has been analysed in some ways have almost in all cases been
quite clear about the level(s) of decision they have addressed (this does not indicate that all those studies have clearly
used the aforesaid terminologies or adhered to this system of categorisation, but the belonging to these categories is almost
invariably inferable from their content). However, a range of studies do not present individual-level measures and restrict
their analyses merely to “macro-scale” or “aggregate” measures (such as “flow-density relation”, or “total evacuation time”,
or “exit utilisations”) which we refer to as “collective behaviour” analyses.

they have addressed empirically. This information will be used in the subsequent section for extracting summary statistics
that demonstrate whether or not the literature has shown a balance in exploring various aspects of human behaviour in
crowds, and if not, which aspects demand further attention. Table A1 summarises this information.1

9. Conclusions and directions for future research
Our review of the existing empirical knowledge on crowd behaviour and emergency modelling revealed certain facts and
patterns that we hope guide the future research on this topic in a more efficient way than before. It was shown by our
analysis that the current knowledge about various aspects of human behaviour in this context is imbalanced and majorly
fragmented in certain areas. We clearly know far more about how people choose steps for example than how they make
navigational decisions or how long they take to initiate an evacuation. Clearly, a successful model necessitates consistent
accuracy at all these levels. We currently do not have access to any analytical evidence to suggest with certainty which
aspect may be more crucial in modelling and predicting aggregate estimates such as total evacuation times (although we
have some intuitions about it), nor do we know of any former study that has methodically addressed this question. We
suggest that an objective sensitivity analysis on most conventional prediction measures (e.g. total evacuation time) using
existing simulation techniques may prove useful for distinguishing the aspects of behaviour upon which our descriptive
measures are most dependant (i.e. the decision level(s) that matters most for the accuracy of our predictions), thereby
revealing the levels in which more research should be invested. Without trying to convey any subjective suggestion here
in the absence of evidence, it may just be possible that the prediction of an evacuation scenario is more sensitive to how
people make “navigational choices” or “how long they take to show a reaction to a threat” or “what actions they choose
to take” than how they “move”. The answer to this question could guide us to identify which parts we should get right in
order to achieve an acceptable prediction accuracy.
Clearly, the ultimate goal of this research stream is making reasonable predictions and deriving reliable estimates. However, it should be noted that many of the prevalent theoretical assumptions underlying our models that have exerted significant influence in the literature have not yet come under scrutiny based on empirical evidence. Recent empirical data
found in a number of new studies for example have presented findings that contradicted deeply-entrenched assumptions
like “herd behaviour” (as a widely used behavioural rule in many simulation models), “mass panic” or “social attraction and
repulsion variation based on inter-individual distance” (Haghani and Sarvi, 2017c). The existing contrast between the evidence supporting the “emergence of a social identity” in emergencies and the theory of “mass panic” is for example quite
stark (Cocking and Drury, 2014; von Sivers et al., 2014). When relaying on mathematical models structured based upon
intuitive modeller-defined assumptions or modeller-suggested heuristics, it goes without saying that rigorous verification
analyses would come crucial to make certain that those underlying assumptions or intuitions hold true to certain degrees
and at least are not grossly off.
Part of the literature on crowd behaviour also presents mixed and often contradictory evidence making it further research
necessary before one can draw definitive conclusions. The evidence on the concept of “faster-is-slower” for instance is very
mixed and confusing (Boari et al., 2013; Pastor et al., 2015). While some authors have claimed that they have “proved” this
assumption, there is a noticeable body of studies that did not support this assumption. Our personal belief is that, in many of
these cases, the assumption per se may not be entirely false but it could be that the assumption carries oversimplifications
in a way that it may falsely extend to a wider context than it should. In other words, the assumption may hold true under

286 M. Haghani, M. Sarvi / Transportation Research Part B 107 (2018) 253–294
certain circumstances and within certain contexts but the problem is that those circumstances and thus the domain of their
applicability may not be perfectly clear to us yet. In more specific terms, regarding the example we made, it is not clear how
narrow an exit door should be, how hard people should push or how dense the crowd has to be in an egress situation so
that we observe a “faster is slower” effect and when should we expect to see the intuitive “faster is faster”? Similar to the
previous example, there is a similar mixed evidence about the “herding” concept. The emerging evidence has been leaning
towards the fact that a simple universal assumption stating that people imitate others in an escape scenario could be just an
oversimplification and can mislead the predictions in many contexts (Haghani et al., 2017). Our review of the recent studies
showed that the new body of the literature has started to recognise the contexts within which this assumption holds true
(or false) as well as identifying the factors that may moderate an imitation tendency. Our conclusion is that while simple
universal assumptions about crowd behaviour may appear appealing from a model formulation perspective, they may not
necessarily be adequate. The best interest may be in treating these assumptions as context specific and in conjunction with
the role of their moderating factors.
The problem of transferability of the results derived from one context or physical setup to other contexts and setups has
also provoked scepticism about the applicability of the findings presented by individual studies. The answer to this question
is crucial for disentangling the aspects of the findings that are inherent to human behaviour from those that are specific to
the particular setup of an experiment, and the only reasonable way for providing answers to this issue may be replication
of same phenomena in multiple contexts and physical geometries, using independent samples and observe the commonality
among the resultant findings to identify universal aspects of the behaviour.
The problem of replicability, however, has to large degrees been overlooked so far in this field. This may have been in
part due to the lack of a unified system of definitions, classifications, measurement methods and terminologies that different
researchers generally agree upon. We believe that this may be a major reason why there has been so few meta-analysis
attempts in this context thus far. The literature basically has not offered this possibility due to the lack of consistency of
measurements and definitions as well as the scarcity of replications.2 This survey, for example, revealed the concept of
pedestrians’ fundamental diagram as a particular case in which measurement methods matter much (Seyfried et al., 2010).
The problem of context dependency has also recently been recognised as an overlooked issue. More research has yet
to be done to identify the underlying behavioural differences in different contexts such as in different levels of crowding
and in different types of emergency of levels of urgency. Also, how sensitive the behavioural lessons are to the size of the
facility that hosts pedestrians or to the occupants’ familiarity with the environment is largely unknown. One for example
may expect to see different escape responses and choice of survival strategy from people in small venues than in large
facilities. It may be argued that the search for a global model that can handle all these contextual combinations may be
out of reach, but it is possible to develop dedicated models for different contexts should there is enough knowledge about
them.
This study covered a range of techniques and approaches for data collection. We do not think of any of these methods
as definite superior (although they are different in terms of the benefits they offer) nor do we regard them as substitutes.
Rather, we believe that they should be thought of as complements since different data collection techniques often allow
different topics to be covered.
Despite what mentioned above, there are also certain topics that can be explored using a number of different data collection techniques. We believe that the literature currently lacks robust links between various methods. Such attempts may
make it possible to have a clearer idea of the borders of applicability of different data collection techniques. For example,
what are the limitations of the applicability of the virtual reality and hypothetical choices, or to what degrees we can learn
from animal experiments? The answer to these questions would probably necessitate replication of the experiments of one
type using other experimental methods and observe their commonalities and observe whether they produce similar behavioural patterns. The recent studies on the connection between the virtual-reality and laboratory experiments with actual
crowds have heightened our awareness towards this problem (Haghani et al., 2016; Moussaïd et al., 2016). Similar connections could be potentially made between the animal experiments and controlled laboratory experiments with humans. The
issue on the animal models of behaviour is of particular importance in this field given that many authors have expressed
scepticism about the reliability of such findings (Parisi et al., 2015) while there has also been promising evidence about
the relevance of this method that we cannot afford to easily ignore (Shahhoseini et al., 2016; Shahhoseini et al., 2017). It is
therefore, crucial to identify the type of questions that we can explore using the animal models and the type of phenomena
that are beyond the accuracy scope of this method. The implications of such research goes beyond behaviour exploration
per se in that it can offer essential guidance for continuation of the research in this field and the choice of best suitable and
valid data collection techniques for specific research questions.
And finally, as many scholars working in this area have argued, it appears inevitable for accurate and reliable models
to accommodate psychological aspects of behaviour and treat the simulated agents as “thinking” entities rather than “selfdriven particles” (Drury et al., 2013; Drury and Stott, 2011). We believe that topics such as the particular role of “stress
and time pressure” on escape decision making behaviour, the role and the nature of “individual differences” in perception,

that in many cases different researchers have used different sets and values for the model parameters or mathematical structures which in many instances

“individual and group” decision making during emergencies (or in normal crowds), the prevalence and the moderating factors of “altruism” tendencies, and also the underlying factors that make people “revisit” or “change” their various decisions
(both in normal and emergency contexts) have yet to be further investigated.
We hope that these discussions can facilitate the avenue for future research on this topic, help researchers form betterinformed hypotheses and choose most suitable data collection techniques for their questions. We also hope that our contribution lead to a better appreciation of the significance of empirical knowledge in this research area and promote higher
quality experiment designs in future studies based on the demands of the literature which may eventually lead to filling the
gap between theoretical developments and empirical knowledge in this area.
