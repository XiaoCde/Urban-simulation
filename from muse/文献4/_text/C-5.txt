ABSTRACT
Floods are the most frequent and destructive natural hazards globally, yet the human impacts 
remain unevenly documented across regions. Evidence on mortality, displacement, health outcomes, and livelihood disruption remains incomplete, particularly in low- and middle-income 
countries. This review investigates global evidence on the human impacts of floods published 
from 1980 to 2025, focussing on vulnerability patterns, reporting gaps, and socio-spatial inequities. The systematic Scopus search yielded 19,105 records, where 82 met the inclusion criteria. The reviewed studies examined outcomes related to mortality, displacement, injuries, 
health effects, and livelihood consequences. A formal meta-analysis was not undertaken because 
of methodological heterogeneity. Instead, we applied narrative synthesis, complemented by 
methodological quality assessment using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). Among the 
outcomes examined, mortality was reported most consistently, appearing in 86.6% of studies, 
whereas displacement, health effects, and livelihood impacts were documented less consistently. 
The literature was geographically skewed toward Asia and North America, while Africa, Latin 
America, and the Middle East remained underrepresented despite substantial exposure and 
vulnerability. Risk was socially uneven, with older adults, children, low-income households, rural 
populations, and marginalized groups experiencing disproportionate vulnerability. Cross-study 
comparability and risk estimation are constrained by inconsistent reporting, limited outcome 
standardization, and the underrepresentation of non-fatal impacts. Flood impacts are strongly 
mediated by the interaction of hazard, exposure, governance, infrastructure, and social vulnerability. Global understanding remains constrained by systematic gaps in the literature, especially 
in relation to displacement, long-term health effects, and livelihood consequences. Expanding 
research coverage, standardizing reporting, and integrating social vulnerability metrics are critical to support evidence-based, equitable flood risk reduction strategies.

1. Introduction
Floods are the most frequent and geographically widespread natural disasters worldwide and affect more people each year than 
any other natural hazard (Doocy et al., 2013; Jonkman & Vrijling, 2008). Flood risk arises from the interaction of multiple drivers, 
including hydro-metrological hazards (e.g., rainfall intensity), land-use and land-cover change, urban expansion, infrastructure 
conditions, and patterns of human exposure and vulnerability. Their impacts extend far beyond immediate physical destruction, 
resulting in substantial mortality, injury, displacement, and long-term socioeconomic disruption. Between 1980 and 2009 alone, 
floods were responsible for an estimated 539,811 deaths, 361,974 injuries, and affected approximately 2.8 billion people globally 
(Doocy et al., 2013). In recent decades, increasing flood risk has been linked not only to climate variability and change, but also to 
rapid urbanisation, deforestation, and unsustainable land-use practices that modify catchment hydrology and increase exposure 
(Hirabayashi et al., 2013; Winsemius et al., 2016). The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) projects that flood risks 
will continue to intensify throughout the twenty-first century, particularly in developing regions where adaptive capacity and disaster 
preparedness remain limited (IPCC, 2021). Thus, to monitor and minimize disaster and adapt to climate, it is relevant to understand 
the drivers and mechanism of flooding.
Many interacting processes are known to cause and amplify floods when it occurs. First, heavy precipitation episodes due to 
hydrometeorological factors, while deforestation (changes in land use land-cover change (LULC)) tend to increase in surface runoff 
and acceleration of catchment response. Also, deforested regions increase erosion and sediment delivery in water bodies, thereby 
causing siltation and reduction in drainage or river carrying capacity. Urban sprawl which increases impervious surfaces limit natural 
drainage to amplify flood-risk while, exacerbating flood conditions when inadequate infrastructure and poor waste management can 
obstruct drainage systems. Thus, making these interactive drivers into a multi-causal process to understand the mechanism of 
flooding.
Floods generate diverse human impacts across health, social, and economic domains, including mortality, injury, displacement, 
disease risk, mental health effects, and livelihood disruption (Alderman et al., 2012; Fernandez et al., 2015). However, these impacts 
are unevenly distributed across populations and places. Vulnerability is shaped by interacting demographic, socioeconomic, environmental, and infrastructural factors, making flood outcomes highly context-specific (FitzGerald et al., 2010; Jonkman et al., 
2008; Tapsell et al., 2002). While these impacts are substantial, they are best understood as downstream outcomes of interacting 
drivers of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability, rather than as standalone phenomena.
The human impacts of floods are multidimensional and extend across health, social, and economic domains. Direct impacts 
include mortality and physical injuries, while indirect consequences include infectious disease outbreaks, mental health disorders, 
livelihood disruption, and prolonged displacement (Alderman et al., 2012; Fernandez et al., 2015). Drowning is widely recognised as 
the leading cause of flood-related deaths and accounts for a large proportion of reported fatalities in flood events (Ashley & Ashley, 
2008; Jonkman, 2005). However, developing nations, where health infrastructure is generally limited, contend with indirect mortality related disease outbreaks that significantly adds the total problem of flood-related disasters (Agampodi et al., 2014; Ahern 
et al., 2005; Brown et al., 1998). Also, during disaster episodes and clean-up campaigns, people are exposed to either contaminated 
water and hazardous materials, or long-term mental health conditions, which contributes to flood-related morbidity(Duclos et al., 
1991; Lowe et al., 2013). Another crucial human impact is displacement, where millions of homes are destroyed and forced into 
overcrowded housings with poor sanitation facilities (Aditi & Ganapathy, 2022).
Notwithstanding the increasing scale and complexity of global flood disasters, evidence on human impacts still remained incomplete and unevenly distributed across regions. Though earlier reviews have made valuable insights, most studies concentrated on 
specific flood types, or on selected outcome categories, rather than providing a more comprehensive account of human flood impacts 
(Ahern et al., 2005; Alderman et al., 2012). Although these studies offer valuable understandings, they capture only part of the 
broader range of human consequences associated with flooding. Additionally, the existing literature is heavily concentrated in highincome countries, mainly in North America, Europe, and Australia, leaving significant evidence gaps in low to middle-income areas 
with highest regular flood vulnerability (Ashley & Ashley, 2008; FitzGerald et al., 2010). Earlier analyses have also tended to cover 
shorter time periods and have not fully captured more recent patterns associated with climate change, urban expansion, land-cover 
change, and increasing disaster exposure (Ababakr et al., 2023; Ahmed et al., 2026; Alcântara et al., 2023; Amini et al., 2025; 
Hamaamin et al., 2025; Hamza et al., 2022; Jonkman, 2005; Tapsell et al., 2010).
Moreover, prior reviews have rarely linked global evidence analysis with national-scale spatial analysis to show how broader 
patterns of flood risk and vulnerability are expressed in specific local contexts. These gaps limit the capacity of policymakers and 
disaster management agencies to design targeted interventions and allocate resources effectively for disaster risk reduction. To 
address these limitations, this study presents a systematic review of global evidence on the human impacts of floods between 1908 
and 2025. The review analyses findings across multiple impact domains, including mortality, displacement, injuries, health outcomes, and livelihood disreuption, and examine how these impacts vary across geographic regions, population groups, and exposure 
contexts.
The study carried out the following objectives: 
1. To systematically review published global evidence on the human impacts of floods between 1980 and 2025.
2. To identify temporal, geographic, and thematic patterns in flood impact research, with particular attention to evidence gaps 
across regions and among vulnerable populations.
3. To examine variations in flood-related impacts by flood type, demographic context, and degree of exposure and vulnerability.

This study contributes to the literature by presenting an updated and complete synthesis of global evidence on the human impacts 
of floods over more than four decades. It draws attention to pronounced geographic inequalities in the distribution of research and to 
critical evidence gaps in highly vulnerable regions. By bringing uneven evidence together within a coherent framework, the study 
provides informed disaster risk reduction, enhanced early warning systems, and targeted climate adaptation policies.
The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2 describes the systematic review methodology. Section 3 presents the 
results of the global evidence analysis. Section 4 discusses the implications for disaster risk reduction and climate adaptation. The 
final section outlines study limitations and proposes directions for future research.
2. Methods
2.1. Review design and protocol
This study used systematic review based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 
2020) guidelines (Page et al., 2021). It ensures the study is transparent, reproducible, and consistently reported. An a priori review 
protocol was developed to define the research questions, eligibility criteria, search strategy, screening procedures, data extraction 
framework, and quality assessment methods.
The main review question was: What is the global evidence on the human impacts of floods between 1980 and 2025 in relation to 
mortality, displacement, injuries, health outcomes, and livelihood impacts? Secondary questions examined geographic patterns in the 
literature, vulnerable populations, temporal trends in reported flood impacts, and variation across flood types. The review was not 
registered in PROSPERO.
2.2. Search strategy and information sources
A comprehensive literature search was conducted in Scopus, a large multidisciplinary citation database covering peer-reviewed 
research in the environmental, health, and social sciences (Baas et al., 2020). Scopus was selected because of its broad interdisciplinary coverage relevant to flood impacts. The search was conducted on 15 December 2025 and targeted studies published 
between 1980 and 2025. The search strategy combined flood-related terms and human-impact terms using Boolean operators (AND/ 
OR). Flood-related terms included: flood, flooding, flash flood, riverine flood, coastal flood, urban flood, pluvial flood, and inundation. 
These were combined with impact-related terms such as human impact, mortality, fatality, casualty, displacement, evacuation, injury, 
morbidity, health impact, public health, livelihood, socioeconomic impacts, and affected population. The search was limited to Englishlanguage peer-reviewed journal articles. No geographic restrictions were applied. The search strategy was refined iteratively through 
pilot searches to improve sensitivity and relevance, resulting in 19,105 records for screening.
2.3. Eligibility criteria
Studies were eligible if they examined human populations affected by flood events in any geographic region or demographic 
context. Eligible flood exposures included riverine, coastal, flash, urban, pluvial, and hybrid flood events, provided that flooding was 
a major hazard component. Eligible outcomes included: mortality and fatality counts, injuries and morbidity, displacement and 
evacuation, physical and mental health outcomes, livelihood and socioeconomic impacts and affected population statistics. All 
empirical study designs were considered eligible, including quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods studies, as well as epidemiological, cohort, cross-sectional, ecological, and case-based studies with extractable human-impact data. Studies were excluded if 
they: 
1. examined hazards unrelated to floods
2. focused only on property, infrastructure, or economic losses without human-impact outcomes
3. modelling or simulation studies without empirical disaster data
4. not peer-reviewed journal articles
Studies focused solely on environmental or ecological impacts without human dimensions were also excluded. Duplicate publications and secondary analyses of already included datasets were removed during screening.
2.4. Study selection
Study selection followed a multi-stage screening process. All 19,105 records retrieved from Scopus underwent title and abstract 
screening by two reviewers using the predefined eligibility criteria. A pilot screening exercise involving 50 randomly selected records 
was conducted before formal screening to improve consistency in the application of inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements 
were resolved through discussion. Any record considered potentially relevant by either reviewer proceeded to full-text review. At the 
title and abstract stage, 18,685 records were excluded, mainly because they were unrelated to floods, lacked human-impact outcomes, were modelling studies without empirical data, or were not peer-reviewed journal articles.
The remaining 420 full-text articles were assessed independently by two reviewers. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and, where necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Most were excluded primarily due to the absence of extractable

2.5. Data extraction
The study designed a standardized data extraction form. Based on a pilot-test, about five studies was tested to improve reliability 
and extensiveness in data collection. Two independent reviewers performed the data extraction, whereas a third reviewer randomly 
sampled about 10% to check and verify reliability and quality. Extracted variables included: 
• bibliographic information: author, year, title, journal
• study characteristics: study design, country, region, urban/rural context
• flood characteristics: flood type, temporal scale, spatial scale, duration, recurrence interval (where available)
• human-impact outcomes: mortality, injuries, displacement, health effects, livelihood impacts, and affected population statistics
• methodological quality indicators
For clarity in this review, affected population refers broadly to individuals experiencing adverse consequences of a flood event, 
including injury, evacuation, displacement, livelihood disruption, or other direct impacts, whereas displaced population refers 
specifically to people forced to leave their usual place of residence, either temporarily or permanently, because of flooding. Quality 
indicators were also extracted for subsequent methodological assessment. As part of quality control, all extracted numeric values 
were cross-checked against the original source material, and any affected summary totals were recalculated following correction of 
identified extraction or classification errors.

2.6. Quality assessment
Methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed using criteria adapted from the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for observational 
studies and the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) (Hong et al., 2018; Wells et al., 2014). Studies were evaluated across several 
domains, including: 
• representativeness of the study population
• sampling strategy
• adequacy of outcome measurement and case definitions
• control of confounding
• robustness of the analysis
• transparency in reporting limitations
Quality assessment was performed independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved through discussion. Studies were 
not excluded on the basis of quality; instead, quality ratings informed interpretation of the evidence.
2.7. Data synthesis and analysis
A formal meta-analysis was not feasible because of substantial heterogeneity in study design, flood type, outcome definition, 
exposure classification, and reporting format. Accordingly, a narrative analysis approach was used (Popay et al., 2006). The analysis 
included: 
1. Descriptive analysis of study characteristics, including publication year, region, country income level, flood type, study design, 
and outcome category.
2. Thematic synthesis of major human-impact outcomes, grouped into mortality, displacement, injuries and morbidity, health effects, and livelihood impacts.
3. Subgroup analysis was undertaken to examine differences across geographic region, flood type, and vulnerable population groups.
4. A comparative analysis was undertaken to examine areas of convergence and divergence across the reviewed studies.
5. Quantitative summary statistics, including counts, proportions, ranges, and medians, were generated where data permitted.
This approach enabled identification of broad global patterns in the human impacts of floods, as well as major evidence gaps 
across regions, outcomes, and vulnerable populations.
3. Results
3.1. Study selection and characteristics
A total of 82 studies were included in the review. A total of 82 studies were included in the review. Considerable heterogeneity 
was observed across the included studies with respect to research design, geographic setting, flood type, and outcome measures. A 
broad range of study designs was represented, including time-series epidemiological studies, retrospective observational analyses, 
cross-sectional surveys, case-control studies, prospective cohort studies, interrupted time-series analyses, outbreak investigations, 
ecological studies, randomized controlled trials of interventions, qualitative case studies, mixed-methods studies, GIS-based analyses, 
and event-based disaster assessments. This diversity of evidence base highlights the multidisciplinary scope of flood-impact research, 
drawing on epidemiology, public health, disaster management, geography, hydrology, engineering, and the social sciences. The 
reviewed studies covered several geographic scales, ranging from ranging from community-level investigations to regional multicountry analyses and global assessments. The flood events addressed in previous studies extended from single discrete events, such as 
hurricanes or flash floods, to multiple events within defined periods, seasonal flooding patterns, and long-term historical analyses. 
Human impacts were reported across a wide range of outcomes, with some studies focusing solely on mortality, while others examined injuries, displacement, infectious disease outbreaks, mental health effects, and livelihood disruption.
Although the search covered studies published between 1980 and 2025, the included evidence was concentrated in more recent 
years, with most studies published from 1997 onward. To facilitate interpretation, graphical summaries were prepared to illustrate 
temporal trends, geographic distribution, and the frequency of reported impact categories.
3.2. Geographic distribution of studies
The geographic distribution of the included studies was highly uneven across world regions and income groups] (Figs. 1 and 2). 
The evidence base was heavily concentrated in Asia and North America, with Africa and other underrepresented regions contributing 
markedly fewer studies.
At the national level, a limited number of countries were disproportionately represented, especially those with greater research 
capacity, more developed disaster reporting systems, and higher visibility in the academic literature. Contrarily, many highly floodprone regions, especially those in low-income settings, were either sparsely represented or entirely absent from the literature. This

3.3. Temporal Trends in Research Publication
The temporal distribution of the included studies revealed a strong concentration of publications in recent years Fig. 3). While the 
review spans several decades, the majority of included studies were published from 2010 onward, with the most recent years 
contributing the largest share.
This increases likely reflects growing global concern about climate-related disasters, improvement disaster data systems, advances 
in geospatial and interdisciplinary research, and methods, and expanding policy interest in disaster risk reduction and climate 
adaptation. Recent studies appear to engage more explicitly than earlier work with questions of vulnerability, displacement, health 
impacts, and governance.
Yet, the historical evidence base gives rise to a measure of recency bias. Although the literature captures contemporary flood-risk 
conditions, it is less informative about longer-term historical changes in flood impacts and adaptation. Generally, Fig. 3 suggest that 
the evidence base remains temporally uneven and concentrated heavily in the last decades, despite the evidence base rapidly grew.
3.4. Flood types examined
The included studies represented a broad spectrum of flood types, underscoring the diversity of hydrological and meteorological 
processes that produce inundation events (Fig. 4). The most commonly reported category was unspecified flood type, followed by 
riverine floods, flash floods, and flooding associated with hurricanes or cyclones]. Studies of riverine flooding primarily examined 
events resulting from river overflow following prolonged rainfall or snowmelt, typically affecting extensive geographic areas and 
producing prolonged inundation. Flash flood studies focussed on rapid-onset events triggered by intense rainfall over short durations, 
particularly in urban or mountainous environments. Hurricane- and cyclone-related studies addressed compound hazards, including 
storm surge, heavy rainfall, and associated flooding.
Other flood types were less frequently represented, including monsoon floods, pluvial (rainfall-induced) flooding, urban flooding, 
and compound flood-landside events. Monsoon floods were primarily reported in South and Southeast Asia, where seasonal flooding 
is recurrent. Pluvial flooding referred to surface water accumulation exceeding drainage capacity, while urban flooding highlighted 
vulnerabilities in densely built environment with limited infiltration and inadequate drainage systems. Only a small number of 
studies addressed coastal flooding outside storm surge contexts or landslide-related flooding.
A notable limitation in the literature is the high proportion of studies that did not specify, flood type, which restricts the ability to 
compare impacts across hazard categories. The absence of classification is significant because flood type can strongly influence 
patterns of mortality, displacement, infrastructure damage, and post-disaster recovery processes. The distribution of studies across 
flood types also reveals important evidence gaps, with very limited attention to hazards such as ice-jam floods, dam-failure floods, 
and glacial lake outburst floods, despite their potentially severe impact in specific regions Flood type distribution studies further 
underscores important evidence gaps, especially the limited attention given to hazards such as ice-jam floods, dam-failure floods, and 
glacial lake outburst floods, notwithstanding their potentially severe consequences in specific regions.

3.5. Human impact outcomes
3.5.1. Overview of outcome reporting
The 82 included studies documented a broad range of human impact outcomes, highlighting the multidimensional consequences 
of flooding (Fig. 5). Mortality was the most commonly reported outcome, whereas injuries, displacement, health effects, and livelihood impacts were documented less consistently across the literature less consistently. This pattern suggests that the studies remain 
disproportionately focused on acute, measurable outcomes, with comparatively less attention to longer-term and more complex 
impacts. A vital aspect of evidence base is that various forms of harm are unevenly represented. Mortality was systematically 
documented, probably because deaths are more consistently recorded in official records and disaster reporting systems. On the 
contrary, outcomes such as prolonged displacement, mental health effects, disruption of chronic disease care, and livelihood erosion 
were less consistently quantified, even when identified as major consequences. This finding implies that measurable and institutionally recorded outcomes are more likely to be reported, while longer-term and socially mediated impacts remain comparatively neglected.
This pattern is further influenced by methodological differences. Quantitative epidemiological and disaster-event studies are 
generally more likely to report mortality and injury counts, whereas qualitative and mixed-methods studies more capture displacement experiences, psychosocial impacts, and livelihood disruption. For this reason, the frequency with with certain outcomes 
are reported in the literature should be interpreted with causion. Livelihood impacts were frequently identified, but they were 
generally reported in aggregated terms rather than documented through detailed household-level evidence. In general, Fig. 5 produces both the human burden of floods and the methodological biases influencing the evidence base, signaling that immediate 
impacts are documented more effectively than longer-term consequences..
3.5.2. Mortality impacts
As shown in Table 1, flood-related mortality varied substantially across studies, ranging from small-scale events to major catastrophic disasters. These differences may be explained by variation in hazard intensity, exposure levels, and reporting practices. 
Drowning was the main direct cause of death, especially in flash floods and rapidly inundated settings. At the same time, indirect 
factors, including infrastructure failure, delayed evacuation, and transport-related risks, also contributed to mortality, pointing to the 
importance of exposure, infrastructure, and vulnerability. The evidence on mortality is disproportionately shaped by large, highvisibility disasters, whereas smaller and more frequent events remain less consistently documented. This introduces a visibility bias, 
with extreme events disproportionately shaping the evidence base. General, patterns of flood mortality are highly variable, yet they 
are consistently shaped by the interaction between hazard characteristics and underlying vulnerability.

3.5.3. Displacement and evacuation
Displacement occurrences were frequently significant but not adequately quantified (Table 2). Both immediate evacuation and 
longer-term displacement lasting weeks, months or years werer reported in the 21 studies. The main drivers that emerged were 
housing damage, continued flood risk, contamination, infrastructure disruption, and economic constraints on recovery. Quantitative 
evidence on displacement was scare, with only one study providing an extractable estimate. This highlights a significant gap in the 
evidence base when considered against the global scale displacement caused by flooding. People displaced by flooding experienced a 
range of overlapping hardships, including inadequate shelter, loss of privacy, disruption of social networks, interruption of education 
and employment, psychological distress, and heightened disease risk in shelters. Displacement was also socially differentiated, with 
low-income households, renters, older adults, and persons with disabilities experiencing disproportionate difficulties during evacuation and recovery. Overall, the findings suggest that displacement remains a central but under quantified aspect of flood impacts, 
with important consequences for equity and long-term vulnerability.
3.5.4. Injuries and morbidity
Flood-related morbidity includes both direct physical injuries and substantial indirect health effects (Table 3). Reported injuries 
included fractures, lacerations, puncture wounds, near-drowning cases, burns, and injuries associated with debris, structural collapse, 
and vehicle incidents. Most injuries were minor to moderate, but severe trauma was more common in flash floods and structurally 
destructive events. Evidence from trauma centre studies confirmed the presence of multi-trauma cases requiring hospitalisation and 
surgery. Beyond acute injuries, floods were associated with worsening of chronic diseases, including cardiovascular, respiratory, and 
metabolic conditions. These effects arose from medication interruption, reduced access to healthcare, evacuation-related stress, and 
deteriorating living conditions. Healthcare systems were also disrupted by flooding, leading to increased demand for services and 
changes in care-seeking behavior. For example, significant increases in emergency department utilization were observed after major 
flood events, reflecting both the direct and indirect health burdens of flooding. The available literature shows that non-fatal morbidity is possibly underestimated, specifically in low-resource settings where early warning systems are weak or non-existent and 
informal treatment is widely used. Overall, morbidity extends well beyond immediate injury and represents a substantial but underrecognised component of flood impacts.
3.5.5. Health effects
Flooding was associated with a wide range of infectious, mental, and chronic health outcomes (Table 4), many of which persisted 
beyond the acute disaster phase. Infectious diseases were among the most frequently reported outcomes, including cholera, typhoid, 
hepatitis A and E, diarrhoeal diseases, malaria, and dengue. These were linked to water contamination, sanitation breakdown, and 
vector proliferation. Mental health impacts—including post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, anxiety, and grief—were consistently reported and often persisted for months or years. Vulnerability was higher among children, older adults, and socially 
isolated individuals. Floods also exacerbated chronic diseases through medication disruption, reduced healthcare access, and stressrelated deterioration. These findings indicate that flood-related health impacts are multi-system, prolonged, and socially patterned.
3.5.6. Livelihood and economic impacts
Floods produced multidimensional livelihood impacts (Table 5), affecting income, employment, food security, assets, and longterm economic stability. Income loss resulted from business interruption, crop failure, and asset destruction, disproportionately 
affecting informal workers, farmers, and small enterprises. Infrastructure damage and displacement contributed to employment 
disruption through job loss, reduced working hours, and unstable earnings. Reduced availability, rising prices, and disrupted supply 
chains contributed to food insecurity, while asset loss further limited recovery capacity. Floods also strained household finances by 
reducing savings and increasing debt burdens, often resulting in prolonged financial hardship. Households’ need to redirect resources 
toward recovery disrupted educational continuity and may have had lasting consequences for human capital development. Repeated 
exposure to flooding contributed to the persistence of poverty and constrained social mobility, especially among already vulnerable 
populations. General, livelihood impacts were rarely discrete and were typically characterized by overlapping and mutually reinforcing forms of economic hardship.
3.6. Quantitative analysis of numerical impact data
Extractable numerical data were available in only a subset of studies (Table 6), which constrained the potential for quantitative 
synthesis. Mortality data were the most consistently reported, whereas data on displacement and affected populations remained 
sparse and highly heterogeneous. Displacement data were especially limited, as only one study reported extractable numerical 
estimates. This substantially limits comparative analysis and underscores a major gap in the literature. Heterogeneity in definitions, 
temporal scope, and reporting formats further limited comparability.

3.8. Study quality assessment
The methodological quality and risk of bias of the 82 included studies were assessed using criteria adapted from the 
Newcastle–Ottawa Scale and the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (Table 8). The MMAT-based quality assessment showed that 15 
studies were rated as low risk of bias, 62 as moderate risk, and 5 as high risk. Common methodological limitations included incomplete ascertainment of non-fatal outcomes, reliance on secondary data sources, recall bias in self-reported measures, exposure 
misclassification, and limited adjustment for confounding. These findings were taken into account when interpreting the strength and 
consistency of the evidence, although no studies were excluded on the basis of quality assessment alone.
Studies rated as low risk of bias generally relied on stronger data sources, clearer outcome measurement, and more appropriate 
analytical methods. By contrast, the much larger group of moderate-risk studies often depended on secondary data, ecological 
designs, self-reported outcomes, or incomplete adjustment for confounding. A much smaller number of studies were classified as high 
risk of bias because of substantial methodological weaknesses, including incomplete datasets, poorly described methods, and heavy 
dependence on historical or highly aggregated sources. Across the evidence base, recurring strengths included clear description of 
flood events, transparent acknowledgement of study limitations, and appropriate use of analytical methods for the available data. 
Frequently identified methodological weaknesses included incomplete ascertainment of injuries, displacement, and longer-term 
health effects; short follow-up periods; ecological fallacy associated with area-level exposure assignment; recall bias; exposure 
misclassification; and inadequate control for confounding. These limitations are consequential because they may contribute to the 
underestimation or distortion of flood impacts, particularly in relation to non-fatal, long-term, or socially mediated outcomes.
General, in Table 8, evidence base show offers useful, though methodologically uneven, support for understanding the human 
impacts of flood. The high proportion of moderate-risk studies suggests that literature is capable of identifying broad patterns, 
despite, it is not consistently robust enough to support precise comparisons across regions, outcomes, or contexts. This is particularly 
significant in the interpretation of less visible or less consistently measured outcomes, including displacement, injury burden, and 
long-term health effects. The limited proportion of low-risk studies further emphasizes the need for more rigorous disaster epidemiology, including prospective designs, validated measurement instruments, representative sampling, and more comprehensive 
outcome assessment.

4. Discussion
This study synthesizes the global evidence on the human impacts of floods spanning over four decades of published research. The 
results suggest that flood impacts are multidimensional, socially and spatially uneven, and unevenly represented in literature. 
Mortality was the most commonly reported outcome, followed by displacement and injuries, whereas health effects, livelihood 
disruption, and longer-term social consequences received less consistent attention in the literature. These patterns suggest that the 
prevailing evidence base remains disproportionately focused on acute and immediately measurable outcomes, with comparatively 
less attention given to the broader and more enduring human consequences of flooding (Doocy et al., 2013; Jonkman, 2005).
A key finding of the review is the predominance of mortality in the literature on flood impacts. This is largely consistent with previous 
global assessments (Doocy et al., 2013; Jonkman, 2005), however, the current review expands the evidence base by covering a longer time 
period and a wider diversity of study designs. The importance of mortality possibly reflects both the immediate visibility of deaths in postdisaster reporting and the relative ease of measuring mortality compared with less visible outcomes, including displacement, mental health 
effects, and livelihood disruption (Ahern et al., 2005; Alderman et al., 2012). Contrarily, extractable numerical data on displacement were 
reported in relatively few studies, despite displacement being a major humanitarian consequence of flooding. This imbalance suggests that 
flood research remains weighted toward acute and institutionally recorded outcomes, while medium- and long-term social consequences 
remain under-documented (UNHCR 2023; UNHCR, 2024; WMO, 2023). Although displacement emerged as an important theme across the 
reviewed studies, the very limited availability of extractable numerical data means that conclusions about its aggregate magnitude remain 
tentative and should be interpreted primarily as evidence of underreporting and inconsistent measurement.

The temporal distribution of the included studies further indicates that the evidence base is strongly concentrated in recent years. 
This likely reflects growing scholarly and policy attention to climate-related disasters, improved disaster reporting systems, and 
expanding interest in disaster risk reduction and climate adaptation rather than serving as a direct indicator of increasing flood 
frequency alone (IPCC, 2021; WMO, 2023). More broadly, this pattern suggests that the literature on flood impacts is expanding, but 
remains temporally uneven, with contemporary research priorities influencing which outcomes and populations become visible in the 
published evidence base. While climate change is expected to influence the frequency and intensity of extreme precipitation events, 
the evidence reviewed does not support direct attribution of observed mortality patterns to climate change alone, as outcomes are 
shaped by interacting factors including exposure, infrastructure, and social vulnerability.
One of the most important contributions of this review is the identification of persistent geographic imbalances in flood-impact 
research. Asia and North America were relatively well represented, while Africa, Latin America, and the Middle East remained markedly 
underrepresented despite experiencing substantial flood burdens. This uneven distribution reflects wider structural inequalities in research 
funding, institutional capacity, surveillance systems, publication access, and disaster documentation (Di Baldassarre et al., 2010; Douglas 
et al., 2008). Consequently, some of the populations most vulnerable to flooding remain among the least visible in the international 
evidence base. Thus, the geography of flood research does not fully reflect the geography of flood risk.
The limited representation of African studies is especially concerning. Although the continent faces high exposure to climate 
variability, limited early warning systems, weak drainage infrastructure, rapid urbanisation, and substantial socioeconomic vulnerability, only a small proportion of the studies in this review were performed in Africa. This finding is consistent with earlier studies 
indicating that flood disasters in Africa often have severe human consequences but remain underrepresented in the global evidence 
base (Di Baldassarre et al., 2010; Douglas et al., 2008). This persistent gap constrains understanding of how flood impacts unfold in 
setting characterized by infrastructure deficits, informal settlement growth, limited institutional capacity, and intersecting social 
vulnerabilities.
The review also shows that flood type plays an important role in influencing both flood impacts visibility and its interpretation. 
The literature focused disproportionately on rapid-onset and highly visible events, particularly flash floods and hurricane- or cyclonerelated floods, whereas slower-onset, recurrent, or less clearly classified flood processes remained comparatively understudied. This 
imbalance results in an interpretive bias, as highly visible disasters are more likely to appear in the peer-reviewed literature than 
smaller but recurrent events, even though such events may also produce substantial displacement, livelihood loss, and chronic 
hardship. Moreover, the high proportion of studies that did not specify flood type reduces analytical precision and constrains direct 
comparison across hazard categories.This review also makes an important contribution by highlighting the socially unequal distribution of flood impacts. The evidence indicates that flood impacts fall most heavily on populations already facing poverty, insecure 
housing, limited early warning access, fragile livelihoods, inadequate healthcare access, and constrained capacity to recover. Older 
adults, children, low-income populations, rural populations, and socially marginalised groups were repeatedly identified as vulnerable, although important population groups—such as migrants, linguistic minorities, homeless populations, and incarcerated 
persons—were rarely addressed directly. Taken together, these findings support a wider body of disaster research showing that 
vulnerability does not arise from hazard exposure alone. It is also influenced by socioeconomic conditions, demographic characteristics, environmental settings, and institutional capacity (FitzGerald et al., 2010; Tapsell et al., 2002). Accordingly, flood risk is 
best understood as multidimensional and context dependent, rather than as a function of rainfall intensity or national wealth alone.
The review also demonstrates that flood impacts reach far beyond mortality. The broader burden of flooding includes non-fatal 
injury, infectious disease, mental health effects, disruption of chronic disease care, displacement, and livelihood erosion. These 
outcomes receive far less consistent measurement and reporting. Such uneven visibility reflects both methodological and institutional 
biases, with quantitative epidemiological and disaster-event studies tending to document mortality and injury, while qualitative and 
mixed-methods studies more often capture displacement experiences, psychosocial stress, livelihood disruption, and recovery 
challenges. Consequently, the frequency with which a given outcome appears in the literature should not be interpreted as a direct 
proxy for its severity in real-world settings.
At the same time, these findings need to be read against important limitations in the available evidence. Cross-study comparison 
remains difficult because the reviewed literature varies considerably in study design, the classification of flood types, temporal coverage, 
spatial scale, and the definition of outcomes (Popay et al., 2006). Mortality may be reported as direct flood deaths, all-cause deaths during 
the flood period, or modelled attributable mortality. Displacement may be defined inconsistently, reported qualitatively, or omitted 
entirely. In addition, the literature is likely affected by reporting bias. The evidence base is more reliable for outcomes that are acute, 
visible, and routinely captured in institutional records, particularly mortality, than for consequences that are less visible or unfold over 
time, such as prolonged displacement, disruption of chronic disease care, mental health effects, and livelihood erosion (Alderman et al., 
2012; Fernandez et al., 2015). A similar bias is evident in the types of events that enter the literature: large catastrophic floods are more 
likely to be published than smaller recurrent ones. As a result, this review supports stronger conclusions about overall patterns and gaps in 
the evidence than about precise estimates of flood impact magnitude across different contexts.
Several policy and practice lessons emerge from the review. One is that mortality should not remain the dominant lens through 
which flood impacts are understood, as displacement, chronic health effects, and livelihood disruption can also produce severe and 
lasting harm. Another is that the uneven geography of the evidence base calls for stronger research support in regions that remain 
poorly represented, especially Africa and other low-visibility settings. The findings further suggest that effective flood risk reduction 
cannot rely on hazard control alone, but must also address the social and structural conditions that shape vulnerability, including 
poverty, insecure housing, weak infrastructure, and limited access to health and social protection. In addition, the lack of consistency 
in outcome reporting across studies points to the need for better disaster data systems and more standardized approaches to documenting non-fatal and longer-term impacts.

Another significant understanding is that forecasting alone does not guarantee lower flood losses. In many settings, technical capacity 
for hazard monitoring has improved, but persistent mortality and other human impacts point to continuing weaknesses in disaster governance and warning delivery. Earlier studies indicate that stronger early warning systems are generally associated with lower disaster 
mortality, although their effectiveness remains highly uneven across contexts (UNDRR & WMO, 2023; WMO, 2023). In practice, many 
systems still face last-mile problems, including weak communication, low trust, limited accessibility, poor institutional coordination, and 
constraints on households’ ability to respond. As a result, effective warning systems depend not only on accurate forecasts, but also on risk 
awareness, preparedness, timely communication, and the broader social and institutional capacity to turn warnings into action.
In general, the review highlights several key priorities for future research and practice. These priorities involve expanding the 
literature in poorly represented regions, ensuring ore consistent documentation of non-fatal and longer-term impacts improving the 
ways vulnerability is conceptualised and assessed aross different social groups and flood setting. Broadly, flood disasters are not soley 
as a result of hydrological processes. The human impacts of flooding are shaped by social inequality, infrastructure conditions, 
governance capacity, and geographic context. Addressing these gaps in literature is important for supporting flood risk reduction 
strategies that are more equitable, evidence-informed, and sensitive to local realities.
5. Policy implications and recommendations
The findings indicate that flood risk is influenced not only by hazard processes, but also by governance, infrastructure, and social 
vulnerability. Furthermore, the current evidence base remains geographically uneven and disproportionately focused on mortality, which 
limits the visibility of non-fatal and longer-term impacts such as displacement, health effects, and livelihood disruption. Accordingly, flood 
policy must not focus on hazard control alone, but also address the spatial and social inequalities that shape vulnerability.
The findings also indicate that future research should give greater priority to underrepresented regions, more consistent disaster 
reporting, standardized outcome definitions, and better documentation of non-fatal and longer-term impacts. Flood risk reduction 
should also incorporate social vulnerability metrics, strengthen early warning systems, support local research capacity, and advance 
more integrated, context-sensitive approaches to governance.
6. Limitations
The review observed some limitations. 
1. The review was limited to peer-reviewed studies indexed in Scopus in English language. This may contribute to some regions and 
context under-represented.
2. The review was based on narrative analyses due to many factors such as research designs, heterogeneity in flood types, outcome 
definitions, and reporting formats prevented formal meta-analysis
3. Reporting was particularly inconsistent for displacement, non-fatal injury, long-term health effects, and livelihood disruption, 
thereby constraining cross-context comparison.
4. The evidence base also showed a clear imbalance, favoring acute and measurable outcomes such as mortality over more diffuse 
and longer-term consequences.
5. These limitations also point to the need for future research to expand database coverage, include grey literature where possible, 
and improve the consistency and standardization of flood-impact reporting.

8. Conclusions
This review examines evidence published between 1980 and 2025 on the human impacts of floods, with attention to mortality, 
displacement, injury, health outcomes, and livelihood disruption. The findings show that both the distribution of flood impacts across 
populations and regions and their representation in the literature are uneven. Of the outcomes reviewed, mortality remains the most 
frequently reported and most readily quantifiable, whereas displacement, health effects, and livelihood disruption receive less 
consistent documentation and are rarely presented in standardized forms for comparison. The evidence base also reveals a marked 
geographic imbalance, as Africa, Latin America, and parts of Asia remain underrepresented despite their high flood vulnerability. 
These patterns point to central limitations in the existing literature’s ability to capture the full social, temporal, and regional dimensions of flood impacts.
The review suggests that flood impacts arise from the interaction of hazard, exposure, infrastructure, and social vulnerability, 
rather than from hydrometeorological factors alone. Research attention is concentrated on acute and highly visible outcomes, 
especially mortality associated with rapid-onset floods, whereas medium- and long-term effects such as displacement, mental health 
impacts, and livelihood disruption are still reported less consistently. The social distribution of flood risk is also uneven, with older 
adults, children, low-income households, rural populations, and marginalized groups experiencing heightened vulnerability]. The 
heterogeneity of study design, outcome definitions, and reporting practices further limits cross-context comparison and requires 
caution in interpreting the findings. These review findings have important implications for research, policy, and practice. Improving 
comparability and evidence synthesis will require more standardized reporting, especially for displacement and other non-fatal 
outcomes. More effective, equitable, and context-sensitive flood risk reduction depends on better documentation of the scale, 
duration, and long-term consequences of displacement, stronger integration of social vulnerability metrics, and expanded research in 
underrepresented regions.
