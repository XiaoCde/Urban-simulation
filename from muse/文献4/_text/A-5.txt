ABSTRACT
Understanding post-hazard evacuation decisions poses challenges due to the complex interplay
between individual behaviors and community influences. This study investigates the evacuation
decisions made by residents from three counties of Ohio and Pennsylvania, correlating their
choices with various sociodemographic and behavioral factors. By analyzing diverse evacuation
durations—spanning a week, two months, and a permanent period—the research identifies patterns in distance preference, influenced by attributes such as gender, race, social networks, and
travel habits. Evacuation duration emerged as a significant predictor of the distance traveled,
with prolonged durations leading to farther evacuations. The research highlights the importance
of leveraging local social networks for effective communication during hazards and informs policy recommendations for tailored evacuation advisories and inter-state evacuation collaborations.
1. Introduction
Climate-induced hazards are on an unprecedented rise, posing escalating threats to urban landscapes across the globe [1,2,3].
Cities, often densely populated and complex in their infrastructure, are particularly vulnerable, necessitating strategic evacuation
plans for the safety of their inhabitants [4–7]. Evacuations, frequently triggered by emerging climate threats or unforeseen emergencies, are fundamentally linked to the multifaceted human decision-making processes [8,9,10]. The decision of “how far to evacuate”
emerges from various factors, including social factors [11–14], sociodemographic attributes [15,16], personal beliefs [17], past experiences [18], and the projected duration of evacuation [19,20]. As climate change impacts continue to exacerbate, elucidating these
evacuation decision determinants becomes crucial, holding implications for the safety and well-being of vast populations at risk
[21,22].
Despite the extensive exploration of evacuation behaviors, significant gaps remain in understanding how diverse evacuation durations impact decision-making processes, particularly in non-specific hazard contexts. Ersing et al. [23] underscores the complexity of
evacuation decision-making by examining both geophysical and social influences during Hurricane Irma, suggesting that a multitude
of factors contribute to the evacuation process. Furthermore, [24] delve into the nuances of evacuation behavior during multi-hazard
scenarios, emphasizing the balancing act between following peers and assessing risk, thereby illuminating the intricate dynamics at
play in evacuation decisions. This study addresses these gaps by focusing on the influence of social connections and the interplay of

2. Literature review
2.1. Demographics and evacuation information
Demographic attributes play a pivotal role in evacuation decisions, with recent studies elucidating the nuanced impacts of various
factors. For instance, gender significantly influences evacuation likelihood [26–31], with women often more likely to evacuate than
men, reflecting heightened risk perception or concern for dependents [32,33]. Age is another critical factor [34]; older adults exhibit
higher evacuation rates, possibly due to increased vulnerability or previous experiences [13,30]. Ethnicity and race not only affect
evacuation decisions but also the resources available for evacuation [17,35,36], with minority groups sometimes facing barriers due
to socioeconomic factors or mistrust in official communications [13,37]. The presence of children or elderly [38] in the household
significantly elevates the likelihood of evacuation, underscoring the protective instincts of caregivers or concerns over the dependents' safety and well-being during hazards [29,30]. Moreover, individuals with disabilities or specific medical needs require tailored
evacuation plans and additional support, highlighting the importance of inclusive hazard preparedness efforts [39,40].
Income levels also play a crucial role, with lower-income households facing more significant challenges in evacuating due to financial constraints, pointing to the need for equitable access to evacuation resources and support services [31,41]. For example, a
study on coastal residents under typhoon threat [37] demonstrated that higher family income significantly increases the likelihood of
evacuation, underscoring socioeconomic factors' role in hazard response. Additionally, a study has further detailed how evacuation
decisions are influenced by a complex interplay of these demographic factors alongside individual and household characteristics, underscoring the multifaceted nature of evacuation behavior [42].
A multitude of studies have investigated the effects of evacuation orders during hazard evacuation process. Some studies noted
that mandatory evacuation orders are more effective than mere evacuation recommendations ([43]; Wong et al., 2023). Individuals
who rely on radio, television, and social media for weather-related updates are more likely to evacuate than those predominantly dependent on internet sources. Lindell et al. [29] corroborated this by stating that, besides geographical attributes, evacuation warnings
and mandates from authorities strongly impact the evacuation decision-making process. Sadri et al. [43] illustrated the influence of
warning information sources on evacuation choices, drawing from egocentric network data gathered during Hurricane Sandy. Risk
perception is pivotal in grasping the evacuation decision-making framework [44]. Discerning how individuals acquire and assimilate
information enables researchers to gauge human responses to hazard alerts. Individual process data influenced by specific attributes
like cultural backdrop, education, past experiences, etc. [45]. As a result, different people might interpret identical pieces of information in varying ways.
The research conducted by Dash & Gladwin [30] highlighted that individual factors like geographical location and prior evacuation experiences significantly influence evacuation decisions. Similarly, recent studies such as one by Kuligowski [31] emphasize that
timely and clear evacuation orders are crucial as they substantially impact the decision to evacuate, particularly during wildfires, due
to a combination of perceived threat and communication clarity. Wong et al. [46] further reinforced that proximity to the hazard, previous evacuation experiences, and household composition significantly shape evacuation behaviors, underscoring the importance of
tailored communication strategies to different demographic groups to enhance evacuation compliance. Additionally, the study by
Collins [47] illustrates how public health crises can complicate evacuation behaviors, with concerns like fear of contagion at shelters
dissuading some from evacuating, highlighting the necessity for integrated emergency management planning that accommodates
multiple concurrent hazards.
2.2. Risk assessment and physiological concerns
Risk perception also remains central to understanding the evacuation decision-making framework. Several studies have posited
that individuals frequently grapple with risk assessment. This challenge encompasses both gauging the likelihood of an event and
weighing the options available [48,49]. Some studies have emphasized that evacuation decisions are often more influenced by one's
own first-hand assessment of the danger, which is additionally shaped by private communications and established social relationships
[50]. For example, the quality of relationships with friends, family members, neighbors and co-workers can significantly affect one's
decision-making [38]. The nature of an individual's social network and their relationships therein can shape their perception of risk

2.3. Social ties, networks, and connections
Other research has delved deeply into the influence of social factors, such as churches, community organizations, or voluntary associations on the evacuation decision-making process. A review by Sadri et al. [52] focuses on the role of social influence in evacuation decision-making, concluding that evacuation behavior is heavily influenced by peer actions and community norms. Observing
others preparing to evacuate significantly increases the probability that an individual will decide to evacuate, indicating the potential
effectiveness of community-based preparedness programs. Additionally, community behavior and perceived norms were highlighted
in a study by Siam et al. [53], which found that households are more likely to evacuate if they perceive their community is also evacuating. This emphasizes the importance of social influences on evacuation decisions. Sadri et al. [43] explores the impact of social networks and information sources on evacuation decisions during hurricanes, concluding that individuals embedded in richer social networks who receive consistent and credible information from trusted sources are more likely to evacuate. The study also highlights the
significant role of social media in spreading information and influencing evacuation decisions, illustrating that modern communication tools are crucial in shaping public responses to hurricane warnings.
Sadri et al. [54] further emphasizes that the density of an individual's social networks is particularly crucial. A dense interpersonal
network, signifying close connections between individuals, often facilitates the efficient sharing of information and resources. Murphy [55] also emphasizes the importance of social capital resources, such as networks of both strong and weak ties, positing that such
networks bolster community resilience in the face of hazards. Some studies have demonstrated that families with relatives outside the
hazard-affected area are more inclined to evacuate [56,57]. These “invited evacuations” are typically influenced by weaker ties in
one's network. Furthermore, compared to weak ties, strong/close ties are more effective buffers against the effects of stress and other
emergencies rather than the hazard itself [51]. During the time when hazards such as hurricanes and earthquakes affect the whole
community, small, dense, and close tie may not be able to function effectively. Lin et al. [58] emphasized that post-major life events,
such as a divorce, assistance is predominantly derived from close ties rather than weak ones. Studying 40 families affected by Hurricane Katrina in New Orleans, Louisiana, [59] highlighted the significance of bonding social relationships (close ties) for immediate
support.
Yamamura [60] observed that social capital can mitigate the damage caused by hazards through cooperative efforts among residents. Lyu [61] delved into how social capital influences individuals to evacuate without official orders during rainstorm events in
China. Their research highlights that all forms of social capital—bonding, bridging, and linking—significantly increase the likelihood
of shadow evacuation, emphasizing the vital role of strong social connections in hazard response.
Additionally, social capital emerges as a critical asset for receiving assistance both before and after hazards. However, some studies suggest that social capital may be a weaker predictor of evacuation behavior before hazards strike. In certain communities with
high levels of social capital, the peer pressure from individuals opting not to evacuate may lead others to ignore mandatory evacuation orders. Social influences also play a significant role in the psychological process of deciding whether to evacuate. Hasan &
Ukkusuri [62] further corroborate this by noting that the perception of danger can spread through social networks. However, various
studies indicate that certain strong social factors might deter individuals from evacuating preemptively. For example, elements such
as social cohesion, territorial markers, civic engagement and volunteerism have been associated with decreased hurricane evacuation
rates. This potential counterproductive effect was echoed in Cordasco's [63] research, which revealed that some respondents, despite
having the means and financial ability to evacuate, based their decisions on local culture, social obligations, and a greater trust in
their networks over official authorities.
Moreover, Adger et al. [64] suggest that individuals and communities consider mobilizing assets, networks, and social capital to
cope with potential hazards through adaptive strategies. Sadri et al. [65] highlighted that the frequency of contact, discussion topics,
and geographical proximity significantly influence evacuation decision-making among social relations. Consequently, they advocate
for community leaders and policymakers to consider social, neighborhood, and community factors when preparing for hazards, recommending community activities to strengthen social ties and plan for specific hazard scenarios. Later, Sadri et al. [52] examined the
impact of household characteristics such as transportation access on evacuation decisions. They found that households with readily
available private vehicles and clear evacuation routes are more likely to evacuate compared to those reliant on public transport or residing in areas with congested routes.
2.4. Research gaps
Existing research has elucidated numerous aspects of evacuation behaviors, yet several research gaps persist. Firstly, a particularly
under-researched facet is the impact of evacuation distance on behavior and decision-making. Preliminary studies have indicated that
the anticipated distance of evacuation could significantly influence preparedness, route choice, and mode of transportation [20].
However, there is a lack of extensive studies connecting evacuation distance with evacuation duration. Secondly, the influence of regular social interactions on evacuation decisions, especially during short-term evacuations, is another under-researched area. Studies
by Smith & McCarty [12] hinted at the influence of social networks but did not delve deeply into their relationship with evacuation
distance. Lastly, while personal experiences, information access, and the broader social environment are acknowledged as factors influencing evacuation behaviors, their specific roles in shaping evacuation distances need clearer elucidation. The work of Dash &

3. Data and Methods
3.1. Survey data and analysis
To gather insights into people's reactions to evacuation scenarios, we utilized data from the Neighborhood Connectivity Survey
(Appendix 1). This mail-based survey from 2019 targeted residents in three locations: Akron, State College, and Philadelphia County
in Ohio and Pennsylvania (Fig. 1). The data is not collected for a specific hazard event. The survey received funding from the John S.
and James L. Knight Foundation. All participants volunteered to take part.
Out of the 1030 respondents, 562 did not provide evacuation locations for the various scenarios or omitted essential background
information—such as social support, connectivity patterns, and demographic characteristics—needed for further analysis. As a result,
they were excluded, leaving an analytical sample size of 468. Given the multiple evacuation scenarios, there were instances of missing
values. Specifically, the sample size for the multiple linear regression model for a one-week evacuation stands at 419. The two-month
evacuation model incorporates 397 respondents, and the permanent evacuation model comprises 357.
The evacuation distance was conceptualized as the distance between a respondent's home and their chosen evacuation destination. Respondents indicated different locations (neighborhood, city, state) when asked about potential evacuation durations: a week,
two months, or a permanent period, particularly in response to events that only affected their city. The demographic factors we examined included age, gender, race, relationship status, political orientation, household size, highest level of education, employment status, and the presence of children under the age of 18 in the household.
Neighborhood connectivity was gauged through five survey questions that inquired about participants' social habits. Specifically,
participants were asked about their frequency of.
1. Eating lunch with coworkers.
2. Socializing in person with friends.
3. Having lunch with friends.
4. Inviting people over to their homes for social gatherings.
5. Visiting others' homes for social purposes.
The survey also collected data on the number of friends respondents felt at ease with when asking to go out for dinner and the
number of friends they would feel comfortable visiting without prior notice. Detailed findings from these queries are presented in
Tables 1 and 2. Table 1 offers a demographic snapshot, showing a broad age range of respondents with the majority being White, reflecting the predominant ethnicity of the sampled regions. Notably, a significant portion of the survey population possesses at least a
bachelor's degree, suggesting a relatively high education level among participants, which could influence their access to and interpretation of evacuation information. Furthermore, the survey reveals a considerable percentage of the population strongly values neighborhood ties, with a high frequency of socializing at home and with friends, potentially affecting their willingness to evacuate and
preferred distance. However, it is essential to acknowledge that our final sample has a higher representation of white and female respondents compared to the general population of Akron, State College, and Philadelphia County, as per the demographic data available from the U.S. Census Bureau.

dummy coding, a standard method that transforms categorical variables into a series of binary variables. This process allows us to

4. Results
4.1. Response to evaluation scenarios
In this part we looked into the decisions people in OH and PA make when they need to evacuate. This evacuation duration varied
in a week, two months, or a permanent period. One clear trend is that the longer the evacuation, the more people considered moving
further away (Fig. 3). The data showed a progressive increase based on the duration: for a one-week evacuation, the mean distance
was 315.12 km; this jumped to 504.13 km for a two-month period and surged to 761.26 km when the evacuation duration is permanent. This further underscores the trend of people opting to move further away as the uncertainty or duration of their evacuation extends. Moreover, for a week-long evacuation, around 37 % of respondents are moving to another state. When that timeline stretched
to two months, this number jumped to about 47 %. And if people didn't know when they'd be coming back, a significant 54 % were
considering a move across state lines. While both OH and PA shared this general trend, PA consistently had a higher percentage of its
residents evacuate out-of-state compared to OH across all three timeframes. Interestingly, the thought of leaving the U.S. mainland
wasn't widespread, but it did become a slightly more popular option for those facing a permanent evacuation.

4.2. One-week evacuation
In our study, we determined that factors such as race, gender, travel experiences, local social networks, and the regularity of social
interactions with friends significantly influence evacuation distance during short-term evacuations, spanning periods like one week
and two months.
When people need to evacuate for a week in the face of a hazard (Table 3), gender and race proved to be a significant predictor of
evacuation distance. Specifically, males tended to evacuate further compared to females and Black or African individuals evacuated
further than White individuals. Socializing habits yielded mixed results. Participants who strongly agree they have a good social network but never had people to home to socialize with had a notable decrease in their evacuation distance. Conversely, individuals who
socialized daily with friends but rarely went to others’ homes to socialize had a significant increase in their evacuation distance.
4.3. Two-month evacuation
When the duration of the evacuation was extended to two months (Table 3), gender and race remained statistically significant predictor of evacuation distance. The Black or African category showed an increase in evacuation distance, though this was borderline
significant (p = 0.08). Individuals of Hispanic or Latino descent exhibited a significant increase in evacuation distance compared to
the reference White racial group. Those who never had people over to socialize evacuated 0.769 units less than those who socialized
at home monthly. Neither following news nor focusing only on local news showed a significant relationship with evacuation distance,
suggesting that information consumption patterns may not play a critical role in this extended evacuation scenario. Interestingly,
transportation habits indicated that people who drive and have long-distance travel experience with public transportation tend to
evacuate further during this period.
4.4. Permanent evacuation
In the face of a permanent evacuation (Table 3), factors such as gender, political orientation, specific social habits, and travel experiences play a crucial role in shaping evacuation distances. Males exhibited a trend towards evacuating further than females, although this result was borderline significant (p = 0.05). Notably, those with undisclosed gender were found to evacuate significantly
further by 1.073 units compared to females. Participants identifying as conservative politically showed a decrease in evacuation distance compared to the reference liberal group. However, other political affiliations, such as independent and undisclosed, did not reveal any significant association with evacuation distance.
The number of friends, participants felt comfortable inviting for dinner on a typical night (Friends_dinner), showed a borderline
significant positive correlation with evacuation distance. Participants who strongly agree they had a good social network had a notable decrease in their evacuation distance under both permanent and one-week evacuation scenarios. Contrastingly, individuals who
reported never having lunch with friends evacuated less than those who had lunch with friends monthly. Those who socialized
weekly at others’ homes were also found having longer evacuation distance.
Travel experience revealed a significant positive relationship with the evacuation distance. Specifically, people with more experience using public transportation to travel long distance evacuated further during the permanent evacuation scenario.

However, our study also has its limitations. As mentioned in the Data and Methods section, our sample was biased toward white,

The underrepresentation of other racial, gender, and demographic groups might have resulted in missed nuances or distinctive behavioral patterns inherent to those communities. Moreover, as our study primarily focused on residents of OH and PA, the findings might not be generalizable to other regions or
countries with different sociopolitical and environmental contexts. Many other factors, such as income level, physical disabilities, and
resource accessibility, might also shape evacuation decisions but weren't evaluated in this study. Additionally, while our multivariate
analysis is robust, it is also based on the voluntary responses of individuals, which may introduce response biases.
5.2. Policy implications
Our findings have several policy implications for urban planning and disaster management. Authorities should recognize the distinct evacuation patterns across different demographic groups and tailor their evacuation advisories and support services accordingly.
Given the evidence that people with strong local social networks tend to stay closer, authorities can harness these networks for communication and support during hazards. Since a significant proportion considers moving across state lines during longer evacuations,
states should collaborate to streamline inter-state evacuation processes, share resources, and provide support as needed. Moreover,
given the indication that past experiences with long-distance travel influence evacuation distances, promoting familiarization trips,
especially using public transport, can be beneficial. Such initiatives can equip residents with the confidence and know-how to evacuate farther when needed. Finally, our findings on the influence of political orientation on permanent evacuation decisions suggest that
risk communication strategies need to be politically sensitive and diversified. Authorities should develop communication tools that
resonate with different political groups to ensure the message's effectiveness.
6. Conclusions
In this study, sociodemographic factors such as gender and race are consistent predictors of evacuation distance. Notably, specific
demographic groups, like males and Black or African individuals, typically travel farther during evacuations than their counterparts.
There is also a clear and direct relationship between the duration of evacuation and the distance people are willing to travel. Longer
evacuations invariably lead to people considering more distant destinations, with the distances progressively increasing from one
week to permanent evacuations. This indicates the importance of temporal factors in evacuation planning and decisions.
Consistent with pervious study [51,63,65,67], we find that people with strong local social networks tend to remain closer, while
those regularly socialize with friends might travel farther. The interplay between local social connections and the urge to evacuate
provides an interesting dichotomy for further exploration. The study also suggested that information consumption patterns may not
be a primary influencer for evacuation decisions. Interestingly, political orientation influenced evacuation distances during permanent scenarios. This raises pertinent about whether personal beliefs and ideologies can influence decisions even during emergencies.
Our findings also indicate that familiarity and comfort with long-distance travel, especially using public transportation, appear to affect their evacuation decisions, suggesting that past experiences shape responses during emergencies.
Future research should delve deeper into the roles of cultural factors and personal ideologies in shaping evacuation decisions. Additionally, while our study focused on three specific regions, expanding the geographical scope can provide a more comprehensive
understanding and guide national evacuation policies. Exploring the role of technology and real-time information dissemination in
evacuation decisions also presents a promising avenue for future research.
