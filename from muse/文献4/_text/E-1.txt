ABSTRACT
Climate change is driving extreme weather, heat and flooding, which increasingly require evacuations. Recent 
studies have found inconclusive results on the determinants of evacuation-related decisions and have reported 
widely varying evacuation rates, especially in high-density areas. We use a large dataset of geotagged evacuation 
choices in Paris, France (n = 2976) during a flood, to show that while they are rarely addressed, location, social 
vulnerability, length of residence, and hazard exposure are critical predictors. They can be used to infer the 
impact of previous experience on evacuation intentions, which is usually difficult to collect at scales relevant to 
decision-making. We address multiple evacuation choices over time that have previously been overlooked, 
including gradually self-fueling spontaneous evacuations after observations of peers evacuating or flooding 
proximity and post-impact evacuation due to infrastructure disruption. Our findings reveal that many people 
wait until their home is flooded to evacuate. The gap between the initial share of people leaving immediately and 
the minority refusing to leave in any case even after their housing is flooded gradually fills when considering 
different evacuation behaviors over time. Such chronology might explain the wide range of evacuation rates 
reported in the literature. However, people in vulnerable situations and with increased hazard exposure are more 
likely to declare that they would disregard evacuation instructions. One key implication is that policies aimed at 
reducing social vulnerability might be more effective than communication campaigns to increase preparedness 
and support evacuation.
1. Introduction
Impacts from flooding are expected to increase due to climate change 
and continued development in flood-prone areas, despite the trillions of 
dollars of public funds allocated to flood risk reduction (Bloschl ¨ et al., 
2019; Coronese et al., 2019; Ward et al., 2020). In Europe, flood damage 
is expected to rise to €44 billion per year by 2100, with half a million 
Europeans exposed to river flooding (Dottori et al., 2023). The United 
Nations regularly acknowledges the need for early warnings and evacuation plans to reduce exposure (World Meteorological Organization 
WMO, 2022). While inequality affects flood mortality, some behaviors, 
such as evacuating, requesting help, and escaping, decrease human 
losses and urban dysfunction due to flooding (Yari et al., 2021). However, the evacuation of high-density districts before or during a disaster 
is proving increasingly challenging, as illustrated by the people refusing 
to evacuate during the 2016 flood in Paris (OECD, 2018). Recent studies 
have reported widely varying evacuation rates across contexts and 
continents, from 29 % (Parker et al., 2009) to over 90 % (Thieken et al., 
2022). Meta-analyses have found that many individual determinants – 
notably socio-demographic characteristics – have either minor or 
inconsistent effects on household evacuation (Huang et al., 2016).

no association (Thieken et al., 2022), with other reviews pointing toward highly hazard-specific and context-specific factors (Wang et al., 
2021). Although many studies disagree on the factors, understanding 
the motivation for various evacuation choices remains critical

Risk awareness campaigns are sometimes assumed to be sufficient 
for fostering the desired judgment, motivation, and behavior (Rufat 
et al., 2020). Previous studies on flood evacuation focused on compliance and protective actions through surveys (Botzen et al., 2022), 
computer simulations (Kubo et al., 2021), controlled experiments (Dias 
et al., 2022), or field observations (Armijos et al., 2017). These studies 
often focus on the individual level and while a few exceptions consider 
the interaction of official warning systems with informal warning networks (see Lindell et al., 2019b) or social cues, how people interact with 
others in search for additional information (Wood et al., 2018), most 
overlook interactions, particularly among social groups (Rufat et al., 
2020). Robust quantitative data are still lacking, and evacuation 
modeling is often based on oversimplifications of behavioral dynamics 
(Wang et al., 2021), social factors, and/or group dynamics (Aerts et al., 
2018). To fill these gaps, we surveyed evacuation strategies and 
expressed preferences in the Paris metropolitan area after a series of 
floods.
While it remains difficult to predict compliance with evacuation 
instructions, spontaneous evacuation, and post-impact evacuation due 
to infrastructure disruption from individual characteristics, ranging 
from demographic to social, physical, or previous experience characteristics, we argue that context, location, and social vulnerability matter. 
The contribution of this study is to address multiple evacuation behaviors over time, one previously neglected (post-impact evacuation due to 
degraded living conditions exhaustion, with the exception of Huang 
et al., 2017) and two previously studied as predictors but not as a 
dependent variable (observations of peers evacuating and flooding 
proximity), while identifying the interactions among predictors, 
including social vulnerability, exposure and location which are rarely 
addressed. We suggest that the location of residence indirectly captures 
a wide range of predictors, and the ways they overlap, including previous hazard experience, which are difficult to capture with the usual 
social and demographic proxies.
2. Risk perception, social vulnerability, and evacuations
Evacuation to safer areas has been made even more complex by the 
COVID-19 pandemic (Botzen et al., 2021; Page-Tan, Fraser, 2022). The 
literature focuses on compliance with protective action instructions and 
mandatory evacuations, as well as re-entry management, as they play 
key roles in the success of emergency operations (Lindell et al., 2019a). 
The evacuation rate reported in previous studies varies widely, with 
29 % in the United Kingdom (Parker et al., 2009), 33 % in Iran (Yari 
et al., 2021), 40 % in Tokyo (Otsuka et al., 2022), 46 % in Accra (Yin 
et al., 2022), 67 % in South Carolina (Martin et al., 2020), and over 90 % 
in Germany (Thieken et al., 2016). Different compliance rates have also 
been reported from one event to another at a given location, for example 
in South Carolina in different storms (Huang et al., 2016). Conversely, 
spontaneous evacuation refers to people leaving their location because 
of perceived risk without being directed to do so (Lindell et al., 2011). 
Empirical studies have found mixed and inconclusive results on the effects of social and demographic variables – including education, gender, 
age, income, health, physical mobility, and costs incurred from previous 
floods (Baker, 1991; Wang et al., 2021). While many quantitative studies 
inquire about evacuation intentions or expectations rather than actual 
behavior, they have been shown to be related (Kang et al., 2007), and 
both have the same predictors (Huang et al., 2016). Another challenge is 
that social vulnerability, risk perception, capacity, and behavior are 
rarely linked (exceptions include Armas et al., 2015; Cruz-Bello, 
Alfie-Cohen, 2022). Some meta-analyses suggest that 
socio-demographic factors are highly context-specific (Wang et al., 
2021). As a result, disentangling the various drivers is challenging 
(Rufat, 2015), and the uncertainty about citizens’ responses weighs on 
forecasts, shelter distribution, and infrastructure decisions (Kolen and 
Helsloot, 2014).
While the Protective Action Decision Model (PADM) asserts that 
evacuation decisions are also influenced by environmental and social 
cues, such as warnings relayed by peers (Lindell and Perry, 2012), many 
studies assume a top-down flow of information to a complying public or 
develop modeling tools disconnected from empirical data (Aerts et al., 
2018). Apart from hurricanes in the United States (Kim, Oh, 2015) and 
tsunamis in Japan (Mas et al., 2012), quantitative studies on evacuations 
are still in the minority (Huang et al., 2016; Kreibich et al., 2017), 
especially in large metropolises. The lack of quantitative knowledge on 
social and demographic drivers makes it challenging to calibrate simulations (Haer et al., 2017). Social and technological changes have 
widened the knowledge gaps about behavior (Santoni, Rufat, 2021). 
While tackling the interplay between risk perception, social vulnerability and evacuation is challenging, especially across space (O’Neill 
et al., 2015), spatial approaches are currently limited to the spatial 
distribution of shelters and resources (Karaye et al., 2020) or the impact 
of disaster distance on perception (Osberghaus, Fugger, 2022). Recent 
studies have shown that socially vulnerable communities have lower 
levels of self-protection and risk knowledge (Rufat and Botzen, 2022). It 
was not the isolated effect of age, class, immigration status, language 
barriers, lack of mobility or dependence of care that explained in situ 
sheltering, impediments to evacuation and the disproportionate burden 
during and after disasters, but rather multiple dimension of vulnerability 
combining and compounding inequities (Rufat et al., 2015). This 
renewed focus deepens the understanding of how people facing multiple 
social inequities affect and are affected by the impacts of various crises 
(Kuran et al., 2020).
Following these less explored approaches, we hypothesize that the 
neighborhood of residence indirectly captures a wide range of predictors 
(including previous hazard experience, residential segregation, and 
environmental inequalities), which are difficult to capture with the 
typical socio-demographic proxies. The Hazards-of-Place (HoP) model 
emphasizes that many people do not have access to the resources 
necessary to respond safely to hazards (Cutter, 1996), in agreement with 
other social vulnerability theories (Rufat et al., 2020). While social 
vulnerability can limit protective decision-making, including evacuation 
behavior (First et al., 2022), there is no definitive list of variables to 
implement the HoP model, and it requires to focus on the combined 
impact from multiple variables (Drakes and Tate, 2022). Indeed, while 
sociodemographic factors are often studied in isolation, intersectional 
approaches highlight the impact of compounded vulnerability (Versey, 
2021): social vulnerability requires a multivariate analysis implementing its various dimensions (Rufat et al., 2015). Moreover, the combination of the same factors affects residential locations in high-hazard 
areas (Koks et al., 2015) and socially vulnerable groups disproportionately inhabit flood-prone areas (Tate et al., 2021). This points out to the 
neighborhood of residence being an additional proxy of social vulnerability and compounding inequities, and when controlling for the underlying individual characteristics, to being a possible predictor of limits 
to protective decision-making and evacuation intentions. While PADM 
includes a wider series of informational steps in the decision-making 
process (Lindell and Perry, 2012), it could be argued that risk information campaigns and evacuation exercises are place-based, resulting in 
memory of exercises and official information varying according to the 
neighborhood of residence. Moreover, PADM focuses on the specific 
socio-environmental context a person finds themselves within as 
framing factors, making the official hazard exposure level and the 
neighborhood of residence all the more relevant to decision-making and 
evacuation intentions. We use a multiple correspondence analysis 
(MCA) and a random forest analysis (RFA) to evaluate the most

3. Methods
3.1. Context and survey
The Paris metropolitan area (13 million people; 17,000 km2
) is the 
second largest in Europe. Located on the flood plain of the Seine River 
and with an oceanic climate, the capital of France is exposed to river 
lowland floods, urban floods with drainage issues paralyzing the historical core, and other weather-related hazards (Baubion, 2016). In 
2014, the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development 
(OECD) estimated that a flood similar to the 1910 disaster could affect 
up to five million people and cause damages up to €30 billion 
(Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development OECD, 
2014). In March 2016, the EU funded the Sequana flood simulation, a 
two-week international field exercise replicating the 1910 flood in the 
Paris metropolitan area (November and Cr´eton-Cazanave, 2017). This 
simulation took place because massive evacuation was not a behavior 
anchored in the French mindset, due to a lack of training and preparedness (Vinet et al., 2012). Not long after, the June 2016 flood 
affected 486 municipalities over several weeks, causing €1 billion in 
damages when the Seine River reached a level of 6.3 m in Paris: 18,000 
people were evacuated, 750 refused to evacuate, and four people died 
(Richert et al., 2017). In January 2018, the Seine River reached a level of 
5.9 m in Paris, causing €200 million in damages and affecting 242 
municipalities over several weeks: 2500 people were evacuated, and 
over 300 refused to evacuate (OECD, 2018).
We surveyed households about flood risk perception and evacuation 
behavior six months after the 2018 flood in Paris, France (Fig. 1). We 
used a stratified random representative large sample (n = 2976) of the 
population (Table 1).
We ensured representativeness at different scales with spatial and 
social stratification. Half of the sample comprised residents living in the 
official 100-year floodplain. One-third were living in indirect impact 
zones, indicating that they could face power, water, heating, or phone 
outages, and sewer backflows, even if their home was not directly 
flooded. The rest of the respondents lived outside of these exposure 
zones. For each location, the participants were randomly recruited until 
the location-specific social quotas were fulfilled. The use of geographically based strata allowed for a representative sample at several scales 
(municipality, county, metropolitan region) and across all exposure 
levels (none, indirect, direct). We conducted a face-to-face survey 
because Martín et al., (2020) confirmed that an online study might 
ineffectively capture evacuation behavior, despite its low cost to 
administer. The questionnaire was administered from September to 
December 2018 with geotagging of the place of residence, with a 
100-meter buffer to ensure privacy.
We selected four evacuation intentions of interest, including some 
rarely studied: expected compliance with evacuation instructions, 
spontaneous evacuation (readiness to spontaneously evacuate according 
to the proportion of neighbors who have already left), expected 
degraded living conditions exhaustion (post-impact spontaneous evacuation only after 24 hours of power, water, heating, or phone outages 
and sewer backflows), and flooding proximity (readiness to spontaneously evacuate only after housing is flooded).

evacuation. This allowed us to assess any association between these 
three classes of indicators, beyond the patterns and associations between 
individual variables. We precede the analysis with a Kendall’s Tau-b

the proportion of the other answer categories for that specific question 
in the overall sample.
We used the MCA to model the answers to the survey as clouds of 
points to analyze the associations between variables and the links between categories. MCA makes it possible to describe the trends of the 
different answers and explore the links between all variables from an 
explanatory perspective (D’Enza and Greenacre, 2012). The results were 
interpreted based on the relative positions of the points representing the 
answer categories and their distribution along the two top MCA dimensions, which summarize the largest part of the overall variance. Less 
distance between points shows a stronger positive association, and 
categories shared among respondents’ groups are located close together, 
forming point clouds. Conversely, diametrically opposed categories are 
negatively associated, meaning that respondents giving one of the answers were less likely to subsequently assert the opposite category. The 
weight of each category was derived from the sum of their squared 
correlations with the MCA dimensions. For clarity, only the 68 categories with a weight equal to or greater than 0.5 are represented, with a 
size proportional to their weight, categories toward the center of the 
figure capture less variance.
The MCA allows a synthetic mapping of the results by emphasizing 
both the differences or similarities between answers and between respondents. This makes it possible to confront all evacuation choices and 
perceived characteristics with hazard exposure, geographical location, 
and individual differences.
3.3. Random forest analysis (RFA)
Random forest analysis (RFA) is a non-parametric recursive partitioning algorithm in machine learning used for classification or regression. A random forest is a series of classifications, where each regression 
tree is created by recursive partitioning. As a data mining technique, it is 
used for variable screening and dimension reduction when analyzing 
large data sets containing many indicators and categories of positional 
responses. Random forests have been repeatedly shown to achieve 
higher predictive accuracy compared to more conventional regression 
methods (Lee et al., 2015). They provide an unbiased and robust ranking 
of variable importance even in the presence of complex interactions, 
measuring the effect of each predictor individually and also in multiple 
interactions with other variables, independent of overfitting and pruning, even among highly correlated predictors (Scornet et al., 2015).
We used RFA on the same dataset to build multiple conditional trees, 
portray observations with replacements, and find the best nodes split 
within a random subset. Conditional inference trees rely on a treestructured regression model to perform recursive partitioning and use 
a permutation test, a recursive significance test of the null hypothesis to 
select at each split the covariate with the highest p-value with response 
variable. This technique is suitable for non-parametric data, including 
nominal variables with many levels, and minimizes bias deriving from 
missing data, deletion, or imputation by using surrogates.
We generated 1000 trees for each outcome using the R package party. 
We used random training and testing samples to compute the prediction 
accuracy for each conditional inference tree. We used the unbiased 
permutational variable importance measure to select the most influential variables, as it is suited for unbalanced response categories observed 
in our data. The selected variables were then used to build a unique tree 
for visualization and analysis. This approach is designed to identify the 
most important predictors for each outcome of interest, accounting for 
possible interactions and handling non-linear or non-monotonic relationships. It is robust (without data assumptions) and unbiased 
(without missing data imputation). This approach is allowing to 
consider all variables (including those with numerous categorical levels) 
and highlighting the most important predictors (including their highlevel interactions).
Hence, we carried out both MCA and RFA to evaluate the most 
influential predictors associated with the interaction of risk perception 
and evacuation behavior and examine links and patterns among all 
variables for the 2976 respondents to our survey. The combination of 
MCA and RCA allows for an in-depth study of patterns and identification 
of the factors contributing to a wide range of evacuation behaviors, 
while minimizing information loss.
4. Results
4.1. Risk perceptions, previous experience, and expected evacuation 
compliance
We surveyed 2976 households six months after the 2018 flood in 
Paris (Fig. 1). We found that 60 % of respondents expected to comply 
with evacuation instructions, 33 % would refuse, and 7 % were uncertain (Fig. 2).
We found some effect of flooding proximity, with 33 % of respondents declaring that they would only evacuate after their home was 
flooded (Fig. 2). If they were not directly threatened, only 8 % would 
leave right after an alert; a cumulative 20 % would leave if parts of the 
city were already flooded; a cumulative 31 % if their workplace or their 
children’s school were flooded; 45 % if their street was flooded; and 
54 % if their building’s basement was flooded. Twelve percent said they 
would refuse to evacuate and 1 % did not know.
Only a quarter of respondents would not engage in a spontaneous 
evacuation (Fig. 2). Twelve percent would spontaneously evacuate if 
10 % of their neighbors had already left, 15 % more if one-third were 
already gone, a cumulative 46 % if half, a cumulative 60 % if two-third, 
and 69 % if 90 % of their neighbors had left, while 7 % did not know. 
Respondents expected to have some resistance to degraded living conditions exhaustion with only 26 % declaring that they would spontaneously evacuate after 24 hours of power, water, heating, and phone 
outages or sewer backflows. This proportion rises to 53 % after two days 
and 72 % after one week, leaving only 8 % that would refuse to evacuate 
and 6 % undecided.
Respondents had a wide range of risk perception and previous 
experience: 26 % declared that they never worry about floods, whereas 
8 % said that they often worry. Additional variables represent the specific context of the rapid succession of two flood events and one major 
international exercise right before the study. Only 17 % of respondents 
remembered the 2016 flood simulation exercise. Twenty-two percent 
were affected by the 2016 or 2018 floods, whereas only 14 % had 
knowledge of official information on floods.

4.3. Results of the RFA
We further discriminated among the predictors with conditional 
trees (Fig. 5). We inquired about the respondents’ expected compliance 
with mandatory evacuation instructions in cases of major flooding. We 
then measured spontaneous evacuation according to the readiness to 
spontaneously evacuate depending on the proportion of neighbors who 
have already left. We subsequently measured degraded living conditions 
exhaustion according to the readiness to spontaneously evacuate only 
after 24 hours of power, water, heating, and phone outages, and sewer 
backflows. Finally, we measured flooding proximity reactions by the 
readiness to spontaneously evacuate only after the housing is flooded.
The main predictors of compliance are income, place of residence, 
age, and education (Fig. 5A). People aged 45–59 in the low-income 
bracket (15–25k) have the lowest compliance levels (33 %, n = 119), 
followed by people with lower levels of education (primary 45 % 
n = 324, secondary 59 % n = 230). Conversely, people living in downtown Paris with medium to high education levels and from all other 
income brackets have the highest levels of compliance (75 %, n = 385). 
People living in the inner and outer suburbs (61 %, n = 1489), as well as 
those with medium education levels (63 %, n = 429), fall in the middle.
The predictors of spontaneous evacuation are income, place of residence, gender, age, and disability (Fig. 5B). People with a disability and 
medium to high income (>25k) who live in the outer suburbs have the 
lowest thresholds. They are followed by the middle-class (25–50k) living 
in the inner suburbs, or young (18–29 y) and older (>75 y) people living 
in downtown Paris. Conversely, males with low income (15–25k) have 
higher thresholds, i.e. the highest proportion of people refusing to 
evacuate. They are followed by middle-aged people (30–59 y) living in 
downtown Paris.
Spatial and educational predictors are critical indicators of degraded 
living conditions exhaustion (Fig. 5C). People in downtown Paris have 
the highest exhaustion levels, whereas people with higher education 
levels living in the suburbs have the lowest. However, the difference 
between these groups remains small, the share of affirmative responses 
varying only from 20 % to 35 %.
The main predictors of flooding proximity are income, place of 
residence, flood exposure, and age (Fig. 5D). Those living downtown 
Paris, either with low income (15–30k), or under 60 years old, have the 
least delayed reactions. They are followed by people living in a floodprone area, except in the richest bracket (>75k). Conversely, older 
people (>60 y) living in downtown Paris and people in the richest 
bracket (>75k) have the highest delayed reaction. These groups are 
most likely to wait until their housing is flooded before considering 
evacuation. Overall, we found that the main predictors of evacuation 
decisions are income and place of residence, and, to a lesser extent, 
education level, age, length of residence, social vulnerability, and flood 
exposure.
5. Discussion
In accordance with PADM suggestion that evacuations are impacted 
by warnings, environmental cues, and social cues (Lindell and Perry, 
2012), we found four behaviors: i) 8–10 % of people evacuate immediately, ii) 60 % comply with evacuation instructions at some point, 
including weeks after the warning, iii) 12–27 % increasingly follow or 
imitate others as in a snowballing effect, but in the end, iv) 12–15 % do 
not evacuate. We offer a substantial empirical basis by revealing the 
social chronology in different scenarios. Even with no warning or instruction, we found accelerating thresholds, with 12 % of respondents 
saying they would spontaneously evacuate if 10 % of their neighbors 
had already left, cumulatively 27 % of respondents if as many as 30 % of 
their neighbors were gone, 46 % if as many as 50 %, and so on. This 
gradually fills the gap between the people leaving immediately and 
those refusing to leave in any case. This unique chronology is a novelty 
that might explain the wide range of evacuation rates reported in the 
literature. One-third of residents would spontaneously evacuate only if 
their home was already flooded. These intentions are in line with the

many observations that evacuations came too late when there were no 
instructions (Vinet et al., 2012), the warning was delayed (Thieken 
et al., 2022), or some people did not receive the warnings or instructions 
(Yari et al., 2021). We show that the timing of the first 10 % of departures is critical in all scenarios to accelerate the pace of departure in 
each neighborhood and the overall final rate of evacuation. The effect of 
neighbors or peer evacuation rates might also arise from the Theory of 
Planned Behavior’s subjective norms increasing the motivation to avoid 
being left behind (Ajzen, 1991). A combination of improved or earlier 
warnings and a specific plan to ensure the fastest possible evacuation of 
the first critical segment of each neighborhood might speed up the 
triggering of the accelerating thresholds of follow-up spontaneous 
evacuations, resulting in higher evacuation rates.
Location might explain why previous studies found that individual 
characteristics were insufficient for explaining evacuation behavior 
(Bubeck et al., 2018; Mahdavian et al., 2020) and the inconclusive results on characteristics such as gender or age (Huang et al., 2016; Lindell 
et al., 2019a). Age has been shown to be associated with the length of 
residence (Seebauer, Winkler, 2020; Yin et al., 2022). Following Drabek 
(1986), we argue that length of residence can be a proxy for previous 
experience for the most exposed households. This is supported by our 
finding that the impact of age on respondents’ evacuation intentions 
varies according to neighborhood and hazard exposure (Fig. 5). It should 
be emphasized that in our data, testing for simple correlations with age 
in isolation would lead to the misleading conclusion that age (or length 
of residence) had no impact (Fig. 3). The nonsignificant or inconclusive 
results on characteristics such as gender or age reported in meta analyses 
might stem from the diversity of statistical methods and methodological 
choices, as simple correlation analyses are failing to disentangle the 
independent effects of the various socio-demographic variables, 
whereas the effects reported from regression models have been found to 
be dependent on variable choice (Rufat and Botzen, 2022). In hurricane 
studies, controlling for exposure and multidimensional socioeconomic 
status has been shown to allow for deeper exploration of the effects of 
age and gender on evacuation intentions (Bateman, Edwards, 2002).
Additionally, our study reveals the impact of social vulnerability and 
shows that there is no single predictor of any given behavior in any 
scenario, including older age. Ethnicity (Hamilton et al., 2020), income 
(Martin et al., 2020), and education (Yin et al., 2022) have been found to 
be unreliable predictors of evacuation on their own. We show that 
locally intersecting social vulnerability situations increase the likelihood 
of defying the rules while lowering trust, compliance, and the rate of 
spontaneous evacuation. Previous studies have found that low trust in 
authorities is a barrier to evacuation (Mahdavian et al., 2020); we argue 
that this is mediated by social vulnerability. Our results also associate 
this with high anxiety levels and not feeling responsible for one’s own 
safety. We offer a novel and broader perspective on the interplay of age, 
income, education, and vulnerability with evacuation choices, with the 
support of a large sample size. They often converge to make the place of 
residence and exposure to floods among the main predictors. As they are 
all linked by underlying residential segregation (Rufat et al., 2020), we 
suggest focusing on social vulnerability, location, and hazard exposure.
People with higher flood risk perception, worry, or experience are 
more likely to evacuate, comply with instructions, and abide by the 
rules. Previous studies have shown that they are more likely to collect 
information on floods (Otsuka et al., 2022) or acknowledge the importance of preparedness (Osberghaus, 2017). We find that they are also 
more likely to have adapted their homes to floods. While such sought 
after behaviors have no social or demographic predictor, our study reveals their association with previous flood experience. Although desired 
behaviors cannot be predicted by individual characteristics, they can be 
inferred from location, length of residence (rather than age), and flood 
exposure, which indirectly capture a wide range of predictors, even 
though some are very difficult to capture with the usual proxies, 
including perception and experience (Rufat and Howe, 2022). We argue 
that this is the reason why location and exposure are among the main 
predictors of evacuation in all our scenarios.
After the 2018 floods in Paris, a study found that 75 % of the respondents (n = 325) in two municipalities in the suburbs had to evacuate (Pottier et al., 2020). One year after the flood, only 10 % mentioned 
the official instructions as their evacuation driver – at least 66 % 
remembered receiving the instructions, whereas 34 % cited power 
shortages, and 21 % declared that their house was already flooded when 
they left. This confirms that many people engage in post-impact evacuation due to infrastructure disruption. Another empirical study focused 
on high-rise buildings in downtown Paris (Edjossan-Sossou et al., 2020). 
In contrast to data on past behaviors collected in the suburbs, statements 
of intentions collected downtown (n = 521) showed that official instructions were the main evacuation driver (75 %), followed by 
degraded conditions exhaustion (52 %). It could be inferred that expected evacuation compliance is overestimated by respondents. We 
argue however that the difference stems from the very different social 
composition of downtown Paris, where residential segregation pushes 
social vulnerability toward the suburbs (Le Roux et al., 2017), and 
where flood exposure is predominantly indirect. This is convergent with 
our finding that place of residence and exposure are critical to anticipating evacuation behavior.
In Paris, official flood information was found to have little effect on 
awareness (Rufat and Botzen, 2022), while large-scale flood risk 
awareness campaigns were found to be ineffective to raise preparedness 
levels in neighboring countries (Osberghaus and Hinrichs, 2021). 
Focusing on the exposed neighborhoods that have not experienced any 
disaster in recent years, those with indirect exposure, and targeting 
locally intersecting social vulnerability situations might be more effective (Rufat et al., 2023). We find that “captive populations,” those 
trapped both in vulnerable situations and with a higher hazard exposure 
(Rufat et al., 2020), are more prone to declare that they do not trust 
forecasts or authorities and would not follow evacuation instructions. 
We argue that previous studies that have found that misperception and 
mistrust are barriers to flood preparedness (Otsuka et al., 2022) and 
evacuation intentions (Mahdavian et al., 2020) overlook social vulnerability, which mediates such a link. We suggest that reducing social 
vulnerability in the most exposed neighborhoods might be the single 
best approach to increasing preparedness and evacuation compliance.
6. Conclusion
Climate change is driving extreme weather, heat, and flooding, 
which increasingly require the evacuation of dense areas. While it remains difficult to predict evacuation behavior from individual characteristics, location and social vulnerability are often overlooked. Our 
findings reveal that location, length of residence (rather than age), and 
hazard exposure are critical predictors in slow-onset flooding. They can 
be used to infer the impact of previous experience, which is usually 
difficult to collect at scales relevant to decision-making. In our study 
based on a large sample, the main predictors of evacuation intentions 
were place of residence, experience and income, followed by length of 
residence, age, education, social vulnerability, and flood exposure. We 
suggest focusing on the intersecting social vulnerability dimensions, 
location and exposure – all linked by underlying residential segregation 
– because individual characteristics have previously yielded inconclusive results. In our slow-onset riverine flooding case study, many people 
wait until their housing is flooded to evacuate, particularly among 
groups captive to vulnerable situations and neighborhoods. People in 
vulnerable situations and with more hazard exposure are more likely to 
mistrust forecasts, authorities, or disregard evacuation instructions. One 
key implication is that policies aimed at reducing social vulnerability 
might be more effective than communication campaigns to increase 
preparedness and support evacuation.
