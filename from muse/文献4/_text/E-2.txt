ABSTRACT
In recent years, rapid and unregulated urbanization has significantly intensified the challenges of 
urban flooding, particularly through the increasing prevalence of Nuisance Flooding (NF), a form 
of low-depth, low-velocity inundation that disproportionately affects densely populated megacities. While NF typically lacks the large-scale destructiveness, its frequent recurrence leads to 
persistent disruptions in mobility, public services, and the operation of critical infrastructure. 
Despite its growing significance, NF remains largely underrepresented in existing literature, 
which tends to overlook its widespread but subtle impacts, even though the associated social and 
economic losses are substantial. For the first time in flood management literature, this study 
proposes a flood risk assessment framework explicitly tailored to NF conditions over Mumbai, the 
financial capital of India. A sophisticated 1D–2D coupled hydrodynamic model was employed to 
simulate high-resolution flood hazards, while vulnerability to critical and infrastructure facilities 
was assessed using a Shannon Entropy-cum-TOPSIS framework. We employ a novel concept of 
Bivariate Flood Risk Classifier to integrate hazard and vulnerability information, producing 
spatially explicit NF risk maps. Our observations indicate that vulnerability drives flood risks over 
nearly half of the region, while more than 40 % of grids exhibit an equal contribution from both 
hazard and vulnerabilities. Sensitivity analysis revealed that slums are among the most critical 
contributors to vulnerability; excluding slum indicators resulted in a reduction in vulnerability 
across two-thirds of the wards. The framework offers a first-of-its-kind, robust methodology for 
NF risk assessment and provides crucial inputs for integrated, socially responsive flood resilience 
planning in rapidly urbanizing regions.

severe economic consequences [1]. Torrential downpours and coastal surges have rendered large-scale impacts over the Asian 
countries like India, China, Japan, and South Korea [2–4]. Experts postulate that climate warming is already showing its impacts 
vehemently, the most serious and profound on the hydrological cycle [5–7]. Apart from alterations in water availability [8,9] and 
water security [10,11], the prominent impacts are manifested in the form of extreme hydrological incidents, such as floods and

floods are the most disastrous among all known hydro-climatological disasters. They further add that their global occurrences in 2022

Growing urbanization has resulted in reduced infiltration capacities of the landscape [15,16]. This leads to increased runoff during 
heavy rainfalls, ultimately resulting in urban flooding [17]. The existing urban drainage systems are testified as inefficient to 
accommodate such large water volumes, as they were originally designed to handle lower rainfall intensities [18–20]. Exorbitant and 
haphazard population density in the urban centers adds to the adverse impacts [18,21]. Past literature reflects that urban flooding is a 
growing concern in several pockets worldwide and will do so in the future [22,23]. In the context of urban flooding over coastal 
regions, a special characteristic of the inundation pattern has been noticed in recent years, famously referred to as Nuisance Flooding 
(NF). NF is subtlety to urban flooding, per se, it is inconsequential, and non-destructive like the latter; but associated with discomfort to 
the communities and causes minor impacts and damages to the infrastructure facilities and public utilities such as shopping malls, 
subways, airport runways, railways tracks, and traffic systems [24]. The severity and occurrence of nuisance flooding (NF) are strongly 
influenced by the capacity and efficiency of urban infrastructure, particularly stormwater drainage networks and pumping systems. 
Even moderate rainfall events can trigger localized flooding when these systems are undersized, poorly maintained, or obstructed by 
debris. Moreover, NF typically manifests near critical hydrological or tidal thresholds, where even a slight increase in rainfall intensity 
or tidal elevation can shift conditions from normal to inundated. This threshold-sensitive behavior makes NF highly unpredictable and 
emphasizes the importance of maintaining resilient and well-functioning urban drainage infrastructure. In the beginning, Ray and 
Foster [25–27], and Karegar et al. (2017) conceived the generation of NF from sea-level rise only. A few years later, the necessity of 
expanding the concept to pluvial and fluvial flooding was ascertained [28]. Based on the impacts of flooding on transportation networks, public health facilities, and properties, Moftakari et al. [29] define NF as “flooding characterized by depths between 3 and 10 cm 
with velocities not exceeding 3 m/s”, regardless of the source.
By definition, NF represents a dynamic process; hence, it requires sophisticated approaches for its quantification. Coastal catchments are characterized as multi-hazard environments as they witness flooding by an intricate interaction of multiple flood mechanisms, e.g., pluvial flooding in the floodplains due to riverine overflow, pluvial flooding in the inland regions due to rainfall excess, and 
tidal flooding in the coastal belt due to storm surges [30–33]. In such cases, numerical flood models that utilize physics-based laws to 
represent the flow of water offer an ideal solution [34,35]. Teng et al. [36] and Mignot & Dewals [37] provide a comprehensive review 
of various types of flood models, viz., simple, 1D, 2D, 1D-2D coupled, and 3D models. Past literature indicates that the strength and 
capability of the 1D-2D coupled approach is by far superior to others while capturing the multi-hazard driven phenomenon over 
coastal regions [38,39]. A 1D-2D coupled approach essentially combines the interactions in the channel and overland flow patterns to 
provide a realistic representation of the flow of water. By doing so, it provides critical insights into the dynamics of flow, including 
depth, celerity, flux, and direction of propagation. The information on depth and velocity apprehends flood hazards; a dimension of 
flood risk that provides crucial information on the degree of impacts to any entity [40–42].
Recent studies and policy briefs highlight the vitality of accounting for flood risk as a holistic approach to guarantee the protection 
of communities and building resilience, if at all. The AR6 (Sixth Assessment Report) of IPCC categorically mentions the need to 
consider the vulnerability dimension together with hazards to portray flood risk. In the context of urban flooding, numerous studies 
have accounted vulnerability of physical and economic assets. A set of indicators within the urban environment that are susceptible to 
flood damages is usually considered, and their responses are determined through indicator-based approaches, e.g., Multi-Criteria 
Decision Approaches, such as Analytical Hierarchical Process [43–45], TOPSIS [46–48], etc. The contribution of each indicator to 
the overall vulnerability scores is crucial to identify, which will form a platform to provide adequate attention to it in the process of 
diminishing vulnerability.
NF is an emerging disaster in coastal urban centers and hence requires critical attention. As cities and populations grow together 
within aging infrastructure, it is important to tilt our attention towards risk from NF risk, which will form a solid foundation for 
building resilience. Although past efforts have identified the “cause”, little attention has been rendered to understanding their “impacts” 
through data-driven approaches. In other words, the degree of risk emerging from NF is yet to be comprehensively understood. Since 
coastal urban centers contain a wide variety of economic resources that are susceptible to even minor levels of flooding, it is vital to 
acquire adequate information on both flood hazards and vulnerability. The present study, for the first time, addresses a major research 
gap in flood management literature by proposing a novel framework in mapping and quantifying NF risks over a coastal flood-prone 
catchment in India. Design rainfalls, design storm-tides, and design discharges are derived for various return periods through extreme 
value statistics. The design hydraulic variables are fed to a state-of-the-art 1D-2D coupled numerical flood model to derive flood 
inundation and hazards at high resolution. The vulnerability of NF on a comprehensive set of critical and infrastructure facilities is 
determined through the Shannon Entropy-cum-TOPSIS approach. The hazard and vulnerability estimates are threaded together to 
determine flood risks; however, in a unique, unconventional approach through the concept of a Bivariate Flood Risk Classifier (BFRC). 
The identification of NF risks for the first time in flood management research provides valuable insights into its damage-inducing 
attributes, thus adding to the recommendations for effective flood management.

3. Proposed methodology
The present study aims to quantify nuisance flood risk over a sensitive urbanized coastal catchment while accounting for hazard 
and vulnerability dimensions. In order to do so, the present study adopts the following steps: (1) Estimation of design hydrometeorological variables, (2) Mapping high-resolution flood hazard information, (3) Vulnerability assessment using Shannon Entropy and TOPSIS framework, (4) Quantifying bivariate nuisance flood risk. Fig. 2 provides an overview of the proposed methodology.
3.1. Estimation of design hydro-meteorological variables
3.1.1. Design rainfall
In the present study, design hydro-meteorological variables, which include design rainfall, design discharge, and design storm tide, 
were considered as forcing for the hydrodynamic model setup. Design rainfall for the entire study area was derived considering the 
hourly rainfall data for the Santacruz monitoring station. The process involved deriving the annual maxima series and fitting a set of 
probability distributions, details of which can be found in Supplementary Table 1. In the next stage of the analysis, the best-fit marginal 
distribution for the rainfall data was determined using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K-S) test and the Anderson–Darling (A-D) test. The 
distributions were ranked based on the calculated K-S and A-D statistics, with lower statistic values indicating a closer fit to the 
empirical data. The distribution yielding the lowest K-S and A-D values was selected as the best-fit distribution. Using this best-fit 
distribution, design rainfall estimates were computed for return periods of 2,10, and 20 years.
3.1.2. Design storm-tide
In this study, the design storm tide comprising both astronomical tide and surge was estimated following the methodology outlined 
by Mohanty et al. [62]. Minute-wise astronomical tide elevations from 1900 to 2100, simulated by INCOIS, were used as the basis for 
this analysis. Harmonic analysis of the tidal time series was performed using the MATLAB T_Tide package [63,64] to extract the key 
diurnal and semi-diurnal tidal constituents. In the subsequent step, observed minute-wise storm-tide elevation data from 2011 to 2015 
were adjusted to the Mean Sea Level (MSL). The surge component was then derived by subtracting the astronomical tide series from the 
MSL-corrected storm tide during the same period. The design astronomical tide height (dAT) was estimated by fitting the Generalized

3.1.3. Design discharge
Daily discharge data spanning from 1970 to 2011, obtained from the Central Water Commission (India), were collected for this 
analysis. From this dataset, the annual maxima series representing the highest discharge recorded each year was extracted for use in 
flood frequency analysis. To estimate design discharge values for different flood scenarios, the Generalized Extreme Value (GEV)

3.2. Mapping and quantifying high-resolution nuisance flood hazard
In this study, we employed the MIKE+ 1D-2D flood model setup to generate high-resolution nuisance flood hazard data. The MIKE 
+ flood model is renowned for its ability to simulate complex hydrodynamic processes with fine spatial resolution, making it an ideal 
tool for detailed flood hazard assessments [65]. Its effectiveness in producing accurate, high-resolution flood hazard information has 
been widely validated and acknowledged in numerous previous studies, further supporting its use in this research [66–68]. The MIKE+
1D–2D hydrodynamic flood model was adopted because nuisance flooding over the Mithi catchment is driven by the combined and 
time-dependent effects of rainfall, tidal fluctuations, and river water levels. These factors often overlap within short time intervals, 
making their temporal evolution essential to capture accurately. Unlike static bathtub models, which impose a uniform water surface 
and cannot reflect the timing or interaction of multiple drivers, MIKE + simulates the dynamic progression of flood depths and extent 
under compound forcing. This enables a more realistic representation of localized nuisance flooding patterns as they develop, peak, 
and recede over time. In this study, the MIKE + River module was used for the 1D simulations, while the MIKE + Overland Flow 
module was utilized for the 2D simulations. Lateral linkages were established to couple the outputs from both the MIKE + River and 
Overland Flow modules. For accurate bathymetry generation, we employed a high-resolution Digital Elevation Model (DEM) which 
has a precise a vertical accuracy of 15 cm–25 cm. Additionally, high-resolution land use and land cover (LULC) data were utilized to 
assign Manning’s roughness coefficients to different LULC classes. The design hydro-meteorological variables, as previously discussed, 
were employed as input boundary conditions for the MIKE+ 1D-2D model setup. The governing equations and a detailed description of 
the MIKE+ 1D–2D flood model are provided in Supplementary Text 1. The detailed information on the various model editors of MIKE 
+ Hydro River and MIKE + Overland Flow Module is provided in Supplementary Table 2.
3.2.1. Calibration and validation of the flood model
To assess the performance of the developed MIKE+ 1D–2D hydrodynamic model, calibration and validation analyses were carried 
out using historical flood events. The July 2013 flood event was selected for calibration, during which Manning’s roughness coefficients for the Mithi River were systematically adjusted. The simulated discharge time series generated by the MIKE+ 1D Hydro 
River module was then compared against observed discharge data to determine the optimal roughness parameters. Model performance 
was evaluated using three statistical indicators: Percentage Deviation in Peak (%Dp), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and the Index of 
Agreement (D), as summarized in Supplementary Table 3.
The MIKE+ 1D–2D flood model was validated for the July 2023 flood event using Sentinel-1 Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) 
observations. In this study, Sentinel-1 SAR data were obtained in Ground Range Detected (GRD-HD) format and subjected to a series of 
pre-processing steps, including orbit correction, radiometric calibration, speckle filtering, and terrain correction. The processed imagery was subsequently converted to decibel (dB) values, and thresholding was applied to delineate flooded and non-flooded regions. 
Daily records of hydrometeorological variables for the July 2023 event were used as boundary inputs in the MIKE+ 1D–2D model to 
simulate flood inundation. The simulated flood extent was then compared with the inundation area derived from Sentinel-1 SAR data 
using four performance indicators: Hit Rate (HR), Critical Success Index (CSI), False Alarm Ratio (FAR), and Error Bias (EB) 
(Supplementary Table 4). Supplementary Fig. 1 provides an illustrative representation of the developed MIKE+ 1D-2D flood model.
3.2.2. Discretization of the flood hazard
The flood hazard information generated by the MIKE+ 1D-2D model was quantified in terms of floodwater depth, velocity, and 
momentum. In line with the study’s objective of assessing nuisance flood risks, we not only analyzed the depth of floodwater but also 
incorporated the velocity and momentum of the floodwaters. This comprehensive approach ensures a more accurate representation of 
flood hazards, taking into account both the water’s impact force and potential threat to infrastructure and human safety. Flood hazard 
information (depth, velocity, and momentum of the flooding water) was categorized into four discrete classes: low, medium, high, and 
very high. The classification of hazard levels and their corresponding threshold ranges was developed following the methodologies 
proposed by Mani et al. (2014), Moftakhari et al. [29], Mohanty et al. [62], and Thakur et al. [30]. These standards are derived based 
on the impacts of the flood to human-entities and economic assets. While these earlier studies primarily focused on urban flooding 
scenarios, the present work adapts and refines these classifications to address the specific context of nuisance flooding. While Moftakhari et al. [29] identified nuisance flooding as occurring when the inundation depth is below 0.10 m and velocity is less than 3 m/s, 
building on this foundation, the current study introduces, a more detailed discretization of nuisance flood hazards by delineating low, 
medium, high, and very high hazard levels within this shallow-depth regime. This refinement provides a more nuanced understanding 
of risk under frequently occurring, low-intensity flood conditions. The detailed classification criteria for each of these hazard levels are 
outlined in Supplementary Table 5.

3.3. Integrating Shannon Entropy and TOPSIS for flood vulnerability assessment
Flood vulnerability assessment is vital for identifying risks to critical infrastructure [69]. Infrastructure and critical facilities are 
vital for the functioning of society, directly impacting daily life, businesses, and government operations. Critical facilities such as fire 
stations, hospitals, police stations, railway stations, and petrol pumps are essential for effective emergency response during floods. 
Their functionality is severely impacted by floodwaters, which can hinder rescue operations, disrupt medical services, and limit access 
to fuel for emergency vehicles. Similarly, infrastructure facilities, including higher education buildings, schools, flyovers, slums, and 
roads, also contribute to flood vulnerability. Higher education institutions and schools can serve as evacuation centers, while flyovers 
and roads are vital for transportation; however, floods can damage these structures, complicating emergency access and community 
stability. Collectively, the vulnerability of these facilities highlights the urgent need for improved flood preparedness and risk mitigation strategies to enhance resilience in affected communities.
To assess the nuisance flood vulnerability of critical and infrastructure facilities, this study employs a comprehensive set of 18 
indicators. Supplementary Table 6 provides the list of critical and infrastructure indicators adopted in the study. The first step in the 
vulnerability assessment involves assigning weights to these indicators. Traditionally, index weights are determined using subjective 
methods, such as the Delphi method, expert surveys, or the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), which can introduce variability due to 
subjective influences [70]. In contrast, objective fixed-weight methods rely on the intrinsic data of the indicators to generate weights, 
minimizing human interference and yielding more accurate results. In this study, we utilize the Shannon entropy technique, which 
evaluates the degree of disorder related to system information [67,71]. Furthermore, to derive vulnerability scores, this study applies 
the weights obtained from the Shannon Entropy method within the TOPSIS framework, facilitating the generation of detailed 
vulnerability assessments. The comprehensive procedure for vulnerability assessment utilizing the Shannon Entropy and TOPSIS 
framework is outlined in Supplementary Text 2.
3.4. Understanding nuisance flood risk through flood hazard and vulnerability dimensions
This study represents the first attempt to quantify nuisance flood risk by integrating both hazard and vulnerability dimensions. To 
achieve this, the study employs the novel concept of a bivariate choropleth, which incorporates both hazard and vulnerability information. Specifically, this study builds on the bivariate flood risk framework introduced by Mohanty et al. [62] to assess the marginal 
and compound contributions of hazard and vulnerability. While bivariate choropleth mapping has indeed been applied in various 
hazard-related studies, such as those addressing flood or drought risk, our study represents the first application of this approach to 
nuisance flooding. The novelty lies in integrating both hazard and vulnerability dimensions within a single spatial framework specific 
to nuisance flood contexts. Unlike traditional flood risk assessments that primarily focus on major flood events, our approach enables 
simultaneous visualization and interpretation of the intensity of nuisance flood hazards alongside the degree of community vulnerability. This dual-layered representation allows for a more comprehensive understanding of localized risk dynamics, highlighting 
zones where frequent, low-intensity flooding intersects with high vulnerability. Such insights are crucial for formulating targeted 
mitigation and adaptation strategies for recurrent, small-scale flooding events that have often been overlooked in prior studies. Since 
hazard and vulnerability are each categorized into four classes, we develop a 4 × 4 bivariate choropleths using a spectrum of hues to 
illustrate varying levels of flood risk. The cells in the upper triangular section of the choropleth represent flood risk predominantly 
influenced by hazard, whereas the lower triangular cells reflect risk primarily driven by vulnerability. The diagonal cells, meanwhile, 
signify areas where both hazard and vulnerability equally contribute to the flood risk. Detailed visualization of this bivariate choropleth is presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. The integration of flood risk assessment using a bivariate choropleth mapping approach 
offers a comprehensive spatial representation of both hazard intensity and vulnerability levels simultaneously at any given location. 
This dual-layered visualization not only enhances the clarity of risk distribution but also serves as a strategic tool for decision-making 
by facilitating the prioritization of targeted mitigation measures. By distinctly identifying areas where hazard levels are predominant, 
appropriate structural interventions such as improved flood defenses, drainage enhancements, or river embankments can be implemented to minimize potential damage. Conversely, in regions where vulnerability surpasses hazard intensity, emphasis can be placed 
on non-structural measures, such as capacity building, community preparedness, early warning systems, and policy interventions.
4. Results and discussions
4.1. High-resolution flood hazard at the finest grid level
During the calibration process, it was observed that a Manning’s roughness coefficient (n) of 0.030 produced the closest agreement 
between the observed and simulated discharge time series, indicating that this value represents the optimal parameter for the river 
channel roughness in the model. The model achieved a Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 0.24 m, an Index of Agreement (D) of 0.99, a 
Deviation in Peak (Dp) of 0.07, and a Percentage Deviation in Peak (%Dp) of 0.25, demonstrating excellent model performance and 
calibration accuracy. Similarly, during validation, a high degree of consistency was observed between the flooded areas simulated by 
the hydrodynamic model and those delineated from the Sentinel-1 SAR imagery. The spatial comparison revealed that the model 
effectively reproduced the observed flood patterns, accurately capturing the inundation extents across the low-lying and river-adjacent 
regions of the catchment. The flood extent maps derived from both the MIKE+ 1D–2D simulations and the SAR data are presented in 
Supplementary Fig. 3. The quantitative validation results further confirm the model’s robustness, with a Hit Rate (HR) of 0.82, a False 
Alarm Ratio (FAR) of 0.17, a Critical Success Index (CSI) of 0.72, and an Error Bias (EB) of 1.39. These metrics collectively demonstrate

accurately characterizing flood hazard in different scenarios. Furthermore, we notice that the influence of higher design discharge, 
precipitation, and tidal values is most pronounced near the river, around the lake, and in coastal regions. These areas exhibit a significant escalation in hazard levels, underscoring the critical impact of extreme hydrological and tidal events on flood hazards in these 
locations. Across all three parameters and return periods analyzed, a substantial portion of the area falls within the low and medium 
hazard categories. Although these regions are categorized as relatively low-hazard zones, they remain critical from the standpoint of 
nuisance flooding, which captures the recurrent, low-intensity flood events that cumulatively affect urban functioning. While the 
hazard magnitude is low, such events cause considerable discomfort to public movement, particularly for the elderly, children, and 
differently abled individuals. These floods often result in traffic congestion, temporary disruption of essential services, and localized 
waterlogging, which can contribute to the spread of waterborne diseases in densely populated neighborhoods. Therefore, even areas 
with lower hazard levels demand focused attention, as the repetitive occurrence of these minor floods imposes significant social and 
economic costs. Recognizing and addressing these impacts is crucial for developing effective urban flood resilience strategies that 
account for both the frequency and persistence of localized flooding, rather than focusing solely on large-scale or high-return-period 
events.

These zones are densely populated with large informal settlements, where encroachment on the riverbanks has significantly 
compromised the natural drainage system, reduced the river’s flood-carrying capacity and exacerbated inundation risk during extreme 
rainfall events. These settlements often occupy low-lying land, which is highly prone to flooding due to poor elevation, unplanned 
construction, and insufficient stormwater infrastructure. These findings highlight the urgent need for integrative flood risk management that combines infrastructure upgrades, socio-economic resilience, and inclusive urban planning.
4.2.1. Assessing vulnerability through sensitivity analysis of critical indicators
A sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially removing important indicators to assess their individual influence on the 
overall vulnerability scores across the wards in the Mithi River catchment. The findings reveal significant spatial variations in 
vulnerability response depending on the excluded variable. When hospitals were excluded from the analysis, 52.3 % of the wards 
exhibited an increase in vulnerability, while 47.6 % experienced a decrease, indicating the dual role of healthcare accessibility in 
influencing vulnerability patterns. In contrast, the exclusion of road infrastructure led to a sharp rise in vulnerability scores in over 90 
% of the wards, highlighting the critical role of transport connectivity in reducing flood-related and systemic vulnerabilities. Removal 
of buildings resulted in a decrease in vulnerability for approximately 62 % of the wards, suggesting that dense built-up areas may, in 
certain contexts, exacerbate local risk conditions. Notably, the exclusion of subways led to an increase in vulnerability in nearly 95 % of 
the wards, underscoring the importance of subterranean connectivity and evacuation pathways in risk mitigation. Interestingly, when 
slum areas were excluded from the analysis, approximately one-third of the wards exhibited an increase in vulnerability, whereas the 
remaining two-thirds experienced a decrease. This finding indicates that informal settlements exert a highly variable influence on 
vulnerability, likely shaped by factors such as their geographic concentration, lack of infrastructure, and high population densities. The 
fact that more than two-thirds of the wards showed a reduction in vulnerability upon excluding slum indicators is a particularly 
noteworthy and encouraging outcome, suggesting that targeted interventions addressing slum conditions, such as upgrading basic 
services, improving structural safety, or enabling resettlement from high-risk zones, could substantially lower overall vulnerability 
across the catchment. This underscores the strategic importance of slum-focused urban resilience planning in flood-prone metropolitan 
areas like Mumbai. Supplementary Fig. 5 illustrates the changes in vulnerability scores derived from the sensitivity analysis conducted

As emphasized in the prior studies by Thakur and Mohanty [67,73], understanding the dominant drivers of flood risk is essential for 
tailoring appropriate mitigation strategies, especially in the context of nuisance flooding, where floodwaters are typically shallow and 
slow-moving, but nonetheless disruptive. In areas where hazard dominance was identified, the focus should be on minimizing the 
frequency and spatial extent of surface water accumulation through lightweight structural interventions. These may include enhanced 
stormwater drains, permeable pavements, bioswales, and urban retention ponds, which are designed to improve localized drainage 
and prevent waterlogging without necessitating large-scale flood control infrastructure. In contrast, zones where vulnerability is the 
primary contributor require targeted non-structural measures that enhance the coping capacity of residents and reduce exposure to 
even minor flooding. This includes improved waste management to prevent drain blockages, revised land use policies to discourage 
settlement in low-lying areas, community-level flood preparedness training, and improved access to municipal services such as health 
care, mobility support, and sanitation. These interventions can significantly reduce the disruptive impacts of nuisance flooding on 
everyday life, particularly for vulnerable populations in informal settlements. In areas where hazard and vulnerability contribute 
equally, a combined approach is essential. Integrated strategies should couple moderate infrastructure upgrades, such as low-cost 
retrofitting of drains or culverts, with social interventions like participatory planning, risk communication campaigns. In the 
context of nuisance floods, such hybrid measures are not only cost-effective but also critical for maintaining urban functionality, public 
health, and economic continuity, particularly in densely populated urban neighborhoods.
5. Conclusions
Nuisance Flooding (NF), characterized by shallow and low-velocity inundation, is an increasingly pervasive yet under-recognized 
urban hazard. Although non-catastrophic, its recurrent nature disrupts mobility, affects livelihoods, and strains public infrastructure, 
particularly in densely built coastal cities such as Mumbai.
This study presents the first integrated framework for quantifying and mapping NF risk by combining hydrodynamic flood 
modeling with spatial vulnerability analysis through a Bivariate Flood Risk Classifier (BFRC). Using the coupled MIKE+ 1D–2D model, 
design rainfall, storm-tide, and discharge values for multiple return periods were simulated to generate high-resolution flood hazard 
maps. These were integrated with a Shannon Entropy–TOPSIS-based vulnerability index to capture infrastructural vulnerability to 
flooding.
The results demonstrate that even short-return-period events (e.g., 2-year) can cause significant nuisance flooding due to limited 
drainage capacity and extensive impervious surfaces. While most areas fall within low to medium hazard levels, critical zones near 
rivers, lakes, and the coast consistently show higher hazard values. Vulnerability mapping indicates that nearly three-fourths of the 
city’s wards exhibit moderate to very high vulnerability, largely influenced by informal settlements, infrastructure exposure, and 
encroachments along natural drainage channels. The BFRC results further reveal that vulnerability is the dominant driver of NF risk 
across much of the catchment, while compounded hazard–vulnerability interactions account for nearly half the affected area. Overall, 
this study introduces a novel bivariate approach that enhances understanding of localized NF risk by simultaneously visualizing hazard 
intensity and vulnerability distribution.
The framework provides a practical decision-support tool for identifying priority zones and balancing structural measures (e.g., 
drainage upgrades, embankments) with non-structural strategies (e.g., preparedness, policy interventions, and awareness). The study 
highlights the value of combining flood hazard modeling with infrastructure-based vulnerability assessment for managing nuisance 
flooding. However, there is scope to further strengthen the framework. Future research can incorporate socio-economic indicators at a 
much finer scale than wards, such as household data and demographics at grid level. Special attention should be given to vulnerable 
groups like children, the elderly, and the disabled, who face greater risks during even minor flood events. Including transportation 
network data, traffic flows, and public transit disruptions can also add depth to urban risk analysis. Moreover, integrating temporal 
exposure patterns, such as rush hour mobility, can help capture daily variations in flood impact. These enhancements will support 
more inclusive, data-rich, and adaptive flood resilience planning in the context of urban nuisance flood risk management.
