ABSTRACT
Early warnings save lives only when they move people to act. Yet during recent urban flood 
disasters in Japan, evacuation compliance remained extremely low, revealing a substantial 
intention–action gap at the heart of climate risk management. This study addresses that gap by 
proposing an Evacuation Threshold Framework (ETF), which extends the Protective Action Decision Model by conceptualizing the conversion from intention to action as a non-linear, 
threshold-gated process. The framework is tested using a factorial survey experiment conducted in Hiroshima, Japan (n = 1324), in which each respondent evaluated identical flood scenarios 
under both Standard Valuation and Inferred Valuation framings. The latter is used as the primary 
inferential basis because it yields more conservative estimates of evacuation propensity and helps 
attenuate self-presentation bias. The results yield three main findings. First, the behavioral 
activation threshold is calibrated at approximately 0.7 on a 0–1 scale, well above the conventional 0.5 cutoff, indicating substantial psychological inertia before intention translates into action. Second, evacuation intention is driven less by logistical frictions than by social and 
environmental cues: visible peer evacuation exerts a strong contagion effect, while improved 
shelter privacy substantially raises predicted evacuation rates. Third, even under extremely high 
perceived risk, predicted evacuation rates remain near zero when pull and contagion cues are 
absent, indicating that information alone is insufficient to close the intention–action gap. These 
findings suggest that effective climate risk management must move beyond information transmission toward the behavioral design of protective environments — lowering psychological 
thresholds and amplifying visible action. Although grounded in urban flood risk in Japan, the 
proposed framework offers a generalizable behavioral perspective for climate risk management 
across hazards and settings.

This enduring failure reflects not a shortcoming of information systems but a deeper challenge of human behavior. Evacuation is a 
complex social and psychological decision-making process under uncertainty, in which individuals must balance perceived risk of 
staying with the tangible costs of leaving—property protection, travel difficulty, family logistics, and the discomfort or perceived 
indignity of public shelters. Cognitive biases such as normalcy and status-quo bias reinforce inertia, leading individuals to underestimate their exposure and delay action until it is too late. Effective adaptation therefore requires more than improved forecasts or 
communication technology—it requires an understanding of the behavioral thresholds that determine when intention becomes action.
Yet, while research has identified many correlates of evacuation, the relative strength and interaction of these factors remain poorly 
understood. Do people move because of the warnings they receive, or because others around them are moving? Are they constrained 
more by logistical barriers, or by the unattractiveness of the protective options available? And crucially, how can policy design lower 
these behavioral thresholds in a world of escalating climate extremes?
This study addresses these questions through a large-scale factorial survey experiment (n = 1324) conducted in Hiroshima, 
Japan—one of the country's most disaster-prone urban regions. We propose the Evacuation Threshold Framework (ETF), a downstream extension of the Protective Action Decision Model (PADM; Lindell and Perry, 2012) that explicitly theorizes the conversion

activation threshold is empirically calibrated against observed evacuation compliance from the 2018 West Japan Floods.

2. Literature review and conceptual framework
2.1. Protective action and the behavioral limits of existing frameworks
The study of protective behavior during environmental hazards has been shaped by theoretical frameworks such as the Protective 
Action Decision Model (PADM) (Lindell and Perry, 2012), which conceptualizes evacuation as a multi-stage process of perception, 
evaluation, and decision under risk. A robust body of evidence confirms that subjective risk perception and social influence are among 
the most powerful drivers of protective action (Wachinger et al., 2013; Huang et al., 2012). In particular, social proof—observing 
others taking action—has been found to accelerate behavioral convergence during crises (Drabek and Stephenson, 1971; Lovreglio 
et al., 2016; Collins et al., 2018), transforming individual uncertainty into collective motion.
However, this literature also reveals the profound barriers that impede evacuation, even when warnings are clear. Logistical 
constraints such as travel time and mobility limitations exert modest influence compared to the more significant deterrent of perceived 
destination quality (Morss et al., 2011). Concerns about overcrowding, lack of privacy, and safety in shelters are especially salient for 
elderly or health-vulnerable populations (Al-Rousan et al., 2014; Eisenman et al., 2007; Dash and Gladwin, 2007). These findings 
suggest that effective adaptation policy cannot be limited to hazard communication; it must also redesign the behavioral environment 
in which decisions occur.
At the same time, empirical studies of evacuation remain constrained by self-report bias and the intention–behavior gap. When 
asked directly, individuals tend to overstate their willingness to evacuate, driven by social desirability (Sheeran and Webb, 2016). This 
limits the realism of conventional surveys and undermines the policy relevance of their findings. Furthermore, the widely assumed 
knowledge-deficit model—that more information yields more adaptive behavior—fails to explain paradoxical patterns observed in 
disaster response. Research increasingly suggests that excessive familiarity or technical literacy may foster overconfidence or risk 
habituation, reducing protective action even among the well-informed (Wachinger et al., 2013; Paton, 2013). Cognitive biases such as 
normalcy and status-quo bias (Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988; Kuhlicke et al., 2020) further reinforce inertia.
These theoretical and methodological limitations motivate the framework developed in Section 2.2. Three observations need to be 
addressed. First, PADM and related models implicitly assume that intention translates proportionally into action—an assumption 
inconsistent with the empirical record of low evacuation compliance. Second, the relative effectiveness of different intervention 
types—information-based versus environment-based—is poorly theorized. Third, the role of social proof as both an upstream input 
and a downstream amplifier of action remains under-formalized. The Evacuation Threshold Framework explicitly addresses each of 
these gaps.
2.2. The Evacuation Threshold Framework
The Protective Action Decision Model (PADM; Lindell and Perry, 2012) provides the most influential theoretical scaffold for

understanding how individuals respond to environmental hazards. PADM conceptualizes protective behavior as the product of a 
sequential cognitive pathway: environmental and social cues are received and attended to, generate core perceptions of threat and 
protective action, and are integrated through stakeholder-aware decision-making to produce a behavioral response. Within this architecture, perceived risk, response efficacy, and social influence are well-established correlates of evacuation intention, and the 
model's empirical robustness has been confirmed across hurricanes (Huang et al., 2016; Morss et al., 2011), earthquakes (Becker et al., 
2017), wildfires (McCaffrey et al., 2018; Strahan and Watson, 2019), and floods (Terpstra and Lindell, 2013) over more than a decade 
of application.
Yet most applications of PADM share an implicit assumption that has rarely been examined: that the conversion from intention to 
behavioral response is direct and proportional, such that individuals who form sufficient intention to evacuate will, in fact, do so. This 
assumption underpins the conventional binary classification rule in stated-preference and decision-tree models, which treats a 
midpoint cutoff (typically a 0.5 likelihood) as the boundary between predicted action and inaction. The empirical record, however, is 
inconsistent with this assumption. Reviews of intention–behavior correspondence across health and risk domains consistently find that 
a substantial share of people who form intentions fail to act on them (Sheeran and Webb, 2016). Disaster-specific evidence is even 
sharper: in the 2018 West Japan Floods, the average evacuation rate across affected prefectures was only 4.6% (Nikkei, 2018), despite 
official orders reaching hundreds of thousands of residents who had previously expressed strong willingness to comply. The intention–action gap is therefore not a measurement artifact but a structural feature of human behavior under disaster risk.
The Evacuation Threshold Framework (ETF) addresses this gap by explicitly theorizing the conversion from intention to action as a 
threshold-gated, non-linear process rather than a continuous one. The framework rests on a single thesis: protective action emerges

only when intention crosses a behaviorally meaningful activation threshold; that threshold sits well above the conventional midpoint;

ETF preserves rather than displaces the upstream architecture of PADM. The cognitive pathway from cues through perception and 
appraisal to intention remains intact, and the established PADM factors—perceived risk, response efficacy, perceived behavioral 
control, social cues, and institutional trust—continue to operate as determinants of intention formation. In Fig. 2, this preservation is 
shown explicitly in the middle strip: cues feed into perception, perception generates intention, and amplifiers and frictions modulate 
the strength of intention through the same PADM pathway that has been validated across hazards and decades. ETF therefore does not 
propose an alternative theory of how intention forms; it inherits PADM's well-tested account of upstream cognition.
The framework's distinct contribution lies in the downstream conversion that PADM treats as direct. ETF makes four extensions. 
First, it introduces threshold-gated activation: the intention-to-action mapping is non-linear, with a region of inaction even for 
moderately high intentions. Second, it identifies a structural intention–action gap that has theoretical, not stochastic, origins. This gap 
is jointly produced by loss aversion (Tversky and Kahneman, 1991), normalcy and status-quo bias (Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988), 
and hypothetical-response bias in stated-preference elicitation (Murphy et al., 2005; Loomis, 2011); the convergence of these three 
rationales places the activation threshold systematically above the conventional midpoint. Third, ETF reframes adaptation interventions through a push–pull dichotomy (Thaler and Sunstein, 2008): information-based interventions (warnings, comprehension, 
trust) raise intention through cognitive channels, while environment-based interventions (shelter dignity, social proof, peer cues) raise 
intention through affective and normative channels. Although both ultimately move intention along the same axis, their magnitudes 
differ—and our empirical estimates show pull effects to be considerably larger than push effects. Fourth, ETF integrates social 
contagion as a structural feature of the activation curve rather than as a one-shot upstream input (Centola, 2018): visible action raises 
others' intention through social-proof perception, and as more individuals cross the threshold, the population-level response steepens 
and accelerates. The upper strip of Fig. 2 summarizes these three intervention mechanisms.

ETF's conceptual claims map directly onto observable, testable quantities. Intention is operationalized as a continuous behavioral 
measure on a 0–10 scale, transformed and modeled through a beta regression that respects the bounded, asymmetric, and heteroscedastic nature of stated likelihoods (see Section 3.3). The factorial survey experiment manipulates five contextual attributes that map 
onto the channels and frictions visualized in Fig. 2. Three of the five operationalize the policy-modifiable amplifiers shown in the upper 
strip: 
– Perceived flood risk operationalizes the PUSH (Information & Cognition) channel — the perceived risk level that effective risk 
communication, source credibility, and message clarity are designed to produce;
– Neighbor evacuation status operationalizes the CONTAGION (Social Feedback) channel through visible peer evacuation, the 
central social-proof signal in the framework;
– Shelter privacy operationalizes the PULL (Environment & Incentive) channel through its privacy dimension, drawn from the 
broader set of dignity, comfort, privacy, and accessibility levers depicted in Fig. 2.
The remaining two attributes — time of evacuation order and travel time to shelter — operate as study-specific frictions, capturing 
timing and logistical costs that shift intention along the activation curve in the lower panel of Fig. 2 without themselves serving as 
amplifier channels. Individual covariates (literacy, trust, social ties, vulnerability) operate upstream within the PADM cognitive 
pathway preserved by ETF (the Cues → Perception → Intention flow in Fig. 2).
The activation threshold T* is empirically calibrated such that the model reproduces evacuation rates comparable to the 4.6% 
observed in the 2018 West Japan Floods; in our analysis this calibration yields T* ≈ 0.7, well above the conventional 0.5 cutoff and 
consistent in direction with the systematic overstatement of stated relative to actual behavior documented in the meta-analytic 
literature (Murphy et al., 2005; Loomis, 2011). To address the residual social-desirability bias inherent in stated-preference designs, every respondent evaluates each scenario under both Standard Valuation (SV; “what would I do”) and Inferred Valuation (IV; 
“what would my neighbors do”) framings, with order randomized between blocks (Lusk and Norwood, 2009). The IV estimates, which 
suppress respondent self-presentation, serve as the primary inferential basis for the framework's predictions.
The framework generates four sharp, falsifiable predictions that anchor the empirical analysis. (i) Models that adopt the conventional 0.5 cutoff will overstate observed evacuation; the calibrated threshold should fall closer to 0.7. (ii) Pull- and contagion-type 
amplifiers (shelter dignity, social proof) will exhibit larger marginal effects than push-type amplifiers (information, trust). (iii) The 
response to neighbor evacuation will be non-linear, accelerating sharply as visibility crosses an inflection point—the social-contagion 
signature. (iv) Even under maximal information provision, residual gaps will persist when pull factors are absent, demonstrating that 
information alone cannot close the intention–action gap. Section 3 specifies the survey instrument and estimation procedure that test 
these predictions; Section 4 reports the results; Section 5 returns to the framework to draw policy implications for adaptation 
governance.
A final clarification on the status of the activation threshold itself. In this study T* is treated as exogenous and population-level—a 
single calibrated constant, identified by match to observed compliance under typical urban-flood conditions. The framework's amplifiers and frictions therefore operate by moving intention along the horizontal axis of Fig. 2 rather than by modifying T* directly. This 
treatment is parsimonious and corresponds precisely to the empirical model used here. A more general formulation in which T* itself 
becomes endogenous to context—varying across individuals, hazards, or institutional settings—is conceptually consistent with ETF 
and a natural target of future research, but lies beyond the scope of the present analysis.
3. Methods
3.1. Study area and context
This study focuses on Hiroshima City, Japan, a coastal metropolis of approximately 1.17 million residents located in the western 
part of the country along the Ota River basin (Fig. 1). The city occupies a distinctive geomorphological setting in which multiple 
tributaries converge into a low-lying delta before flowing into Hiroshima Bay. Much of the central urban area lies below high-tide level, 
while surrounding residential districts—particularly in the Naka, Higashi, Nishi, Asaminami, and Asakita wards—extend into steep, 
geologically fragile hillsides. This combination of low-lying floodplains and steep slopes produces a highly complex risk environment 
where riverine flooding, inland inundation, landslides, and even storm surges can occur concurrently during extreme rainfall events. 
Hiroshima's exposure to such compound hazards makes it one of Japan's most disaster-prone cities and a representative case for 
understanding the social and behavioral dimensions of urban flood resilience.
Hiroshima's disaster history over the past decade vividly illustrates this vulnerability. In August 2014, record-breaking rainfall 
triggered massive landslides and debris flows across the northern districts of Asaminami and Asakita, killing 77 people, injuring 68, 
and destroying or severely damaging more than 600 houses. Merely four years later, in July 2018, the West Japan Floods struck the 
region with unprecedented intensity, causing widespread river flooding, inland inundation, and slope failures across the metropolitan 
area. Hiroshima Prefecture recorded 153 deaths, five missing persons, and over 6900 damaged houses, including 1176 that were 
completely destroyed. Despite major investments in warning technologies and evacuation planning following the 2014 disaster, the 
2018 floods revealed a persistent behavioral gap: evacuation orders were issued to hundreds of thousands of residents, yet actual 
compliance remained in the low single digits even in the most severely affected districts. Post-event analyses indicated that many 
residents underestimated their personal risk or hesitated to leave due to uncertainty about shelter conditions, family coordination, or 
the perceived inconvenience of night-time evacuation. These findings highlight that the challenge in Hiroshima is not merely technical

but behavioral, rooted in cognitive biases and social inertia that inhibit timely protective action.
From a broader climate adaptation perspective, Hiroshima exemplifies the urban compounding hazards increasingly faced by 
coastal cities worldwide. As climate change accelerates sea-level rise and intensifies extreme rainfall, many metropolitan areas in East 
and Southeast Asia will confront conditions strikingly similar to those of Hiroshima—aging infrastructure, densely populated floodplains, and limited evacuation compliance despite sophisticated warning systems. Understanding the psychological and social 
mechanisms that govern evacuation behavior in this setting therefore provides insights of global relevance, illuminating the human 
limits of early-warning effectiveness and the behavioral foundations of climate resilience.
3.2. Survey design and experimental structure
The survey was designed to capture both attitudinal and behavioral dimensions of flood preparedness among residents of Hiroshima City. It consisted of three major components: (1) general questions on flood risk perception and experience, (2) a factorial 
survey experiment (FSE) eliciting behavioral intentions under controlled hypothetical conditions, and (3) socio-demographic and 
household characteristics.
The questionnaire was administered online via Qualtrics between January and February 2024, targeting residents of five high-risk 
wards—Naka, Higashi, Nishi, Asaminami, and Asakita. The questionnaire was developed and administered in Japanese; all English 
text provided in this manuscript is for presentation purposes only. A quota-based sampling strategy ensured representativeness across 
gender, age, and residential areas, yielding a balanced sample of urban residents exposed to varying degrees of flood risk.
The first section of the questionnaire collected baseline information on participants' awareness and experience related to flood 
hazards. Respondents were asked whether they had previously experienced flood damage or evacuation, how frequently they checked 
official disaster information, and how much they trusted different sources such as television, local government alerts, and social media. 
These responses provided essential context for interpreting behavioral heterogeneity, linking situational choices to personal risk 
perception and communication trust.
The second section implemented a Factorial Survey Experiment (FSE) to quantify how contextual and social cues shape the decision 
to evacuate. Each participant was presented with a short vignette describing a hypothetical flood emergency in Hiroshima, designed to 
evoke realistic but controlled conditions (Fig. 3). The vignette text began by describing a record-breaking rainstorm exceeding the 
scale of the 2014 disaster, noting extensive landslides, flooding, and the issuance of an official citywide evacuation order. This 
contextual preamble ensured that all respondents shared a common situational frame—one of imminent, high-impact flooding with 
credible government alerts—before evaluating their behavioral response.
Following the vignette, participants were shown a visual interface depicting five key situational factors—the time of evacuation, 
perceived flood risk, the evacuation status of neighbors, travel time to the nearest shelter, and the level of shelter privacy—each

represented along a horizontal scale with a red indicator showing their assigned condition (Fig. 4). These five factors and their

difficulty, as night-time evacuation may entail greater physical and psychological resistance. Perceived risk varied from “no perceived 
risk” to “extremely high risk,” representing the subjective hazard severity. Neighbor evacuation operationalized social proof, ranging 
from “none evacuated” to “most evacuated,” reflecting the behavioral influence of community actions. Travel time to shelter varied 
from 5 to 45 min on foot, quantifying logistical constraints, while shelter privacy—ranging from “no privacy” to “high privacy”—represented the perceived dignity and comfort of the destination shelter. Together, these factors formed an orthogonal factorial 
structure designed to disentangle the relative contributions of environmental, social, and logistical determinants of evacuation 
behavior.
After reviewing the information, respondents were asked to rate their likelihood of evacuation on a continuous 0–10 scale, where 
0 = “definitely not evacuating” and 10 = “definitely evacuating.” Each participant evaluated multiple randomly assigned scenarios, 
enabling within-person comparisons and minimizing individual fixed effects. To mitigate potential social desirability bias, the survey 
adopted a within-subject Standard–Inferred Valuation (SV/IV) design: every respondent evaluated each scenario under both 
framings—as themselves (Standard Valuation; “how likely are you to evacuate?”) and as their neighbors (Inferred Valuation; “how 
likely are your neighbors to evacuate?”)—with the order of the two blocks randomized across participants. Comparing the inferred 
responses to the matched self-assessments provides a within-respondent test of reporting bias and yields more conservative, behaviorally realistic estimates of evacuation intention (Lusk and Norwood, 2009). This design generates 5296 observations per valuation 
framing (1324 respondents × 4 scenarios) and ensures that any observed SV–IV differences cannot be attributed to sample 
composition.
The final section of the questionnaire gathered information on socio-demographic and household attributes, including gender, age, 
marital status, education, income, health status, dwelling type, and proximity to rivers or slopes. It also measured the strength of local 
social ties by asking respondents whether they knew their neighbors and had participated in community disaster preparedness activities. These variables served as covariates in subsequent regression analyses, allowing the model to account for heterogeneity in 
social connectedness, vulnerability, and access to information.

the open unit interval (0,1). The dependent variable—evacuation likelihood rated on a 0–10 scale—was transformed to the open unit

We therefore report average marginal effects (AMEs), computed by numerically differentiating the fitted

3.4. Survey implementation and data quality
The empirical data used in this study were collected through an online survey conducted in January and February 2024 among 
adult residents of five flood-prone wards in Hiroshima City—Naka, Higashi, Nishi, Asaminami, and Asakita (Fig. 1). The survey was 
administered through the Japanese research panel of Intage Inc., whose standard panel-management protocol permits only one 
registered respondent per household and applies IP-based duplicate-response screening at the time of fielding. These procedures 
support the household-level independence of observations and minimize duplicate or invalid responses. In addition, the analysis focuses on within-sample experimental contrasts rather than household-level prevalence estimates.
The questionnaire was designed and hosted on Qualtrics. To ensure representativeness, a stratified sampling procedure was 
employed, balancing the sample by gender and age distribution based on the latest census statistics of the target wards. A total of 1324 
valid responses were obtained after excluding incomplete entries, providing a robust empirical basis for behavioral modeling. All 
participants gave informed consent before participation, and no personally identifiable information was collected, in compliance with 
ethical research standards.

aggregate and disaggregated across the five study wards. Population figures are drawn from the Hiroshima City Statistical Yearbook 
(2023) and restricted to residents aged 18 and older. At the ward level, the sample distribution closely mirrors the population: 
respondent shares from Naka, Higashi, Nishi, Asaminami, and Asakita (17.3%, 14.1%, 22.4%, 29.6%, and 16.6%, respectively) deviate 
by no more than 1.0 percentage point from the corresponding ward-level population shares (17.2%, 14.3%, 22.8%, 28.6%, and 
17.2%), indicating successful geographic stratification.

4. Results
4.1. Model comparison: standard and inferred valuation approaches
To identify the most appropriate analytical framework for understanding evacuation intention, we estimated two alternative beta 
regression models: a Standard Valuation (SV) model based on respondents' self-reported intentions, and an Inferred Valuation (IV) 
model in which respondents predicted the likely behavior of their neighbors. This comparison provides a test of whether social 
desirability bias affects conventional self-reported responses in disaster behavior research.
Table 4 summarizes the key estimation results. Because the SV and IV outcomes are different dependent variables, their pseudo-R2 
values are not directly comparable across models and should be read only as descriptive within-model fit statistics; we therefore do not 
interpret the SV–IV gap (0.25 vs 0.46) as evidence of causal superiority. The interpretive case for adopting IV as the primary inferential 
framework rests instead on its theoretical basis: inferred valuations re-route the response through respondents' own social embedding 
and thereby attenuate the self-presentation bias that pervades direct self-reports of socially desirable behavior (Lusk and Norwood, 
2009). This logic is consistent with the well-documented intention–behavior gap, in which individuals systematically overstate 
their own readiness for action relative to their assessment of collective behavior (Sheeran and Webb, 2016; Paton, 2013), and with the 
meta-analytic evidence that hypothetical responses systematically overstate actual behavior (Murphy et al., 2005; Loomis, 2011) — 
the rationale for the threshold calibration in Section 4.3.
A graphical comparison of SV and IV predictions is provided in Fig. 5, which plots the predicted evacuation rate under baseline 
conditions—the share of predicted intentions exceeding each threshold—across thresholds from 0.5 to 0.9 (the IV curve lies below the 
SV curve across the scanned range, with the gap narrowing at higher thresholds). This downward shift indicates that the IV approach 
produces systematically lower predicted intentions and is therefore consistent with a more conservative interpretation of evacuation 
propensity. On this basis, the IV model is adopted as the main framework for subsequent analyses. This finding supports Prediction (i): 
the conventional 0.5 cutoff is inconsistent with observed evacuation compliance, motivating the threshold recalibration developed in 
Section 4.3.
4.2. Behavioral determinants of evacuation intention
Table 4 reports the estimated marginal effects from the IV model. These results reveal the relative influence of risk perception, 
social cues, logistical factors, and individual characteristics on evacuation intention. Perceived risk was the single strongest driver. 
Respondents who assessed the flood risk as “extremely high” exhibited, on average, a 24 percentage-point higher intention to evacuate 
than those perceiving no risk. This finding underscores that the sense of immediacy and severity of threat is a crucial psychological 
trigger for behavioral activation.
Social influence also had a powerful effect. When respondents believed that “most of their neighbors had evacuated,” their predicted intention to evacuate increased by 20 points compared to those who believed no neighbors had left. This strong effect supports 
the concept of social proof—individuals are more likely to act when they perceive others taking similar protective measures.
In contrast, logistical constraints—while statistically significant—exerted smaller effects. Increasing the travel time to a shelter 
from 5 to 15 min decreased intention by about 2 percentage points, and to 30 min by approximately 3 points. Similarly, evacuation 
orders issued at 3:00 a.m. led to an average reduction of 3 percentage points in intention relative to those issued at 9:00 a.m. These 
modest yet consistent effects suggest that convenience and timing influence decisions, but only to a limited degree compared with 
cognitive and social factors.
Social and institutional trust further shaped behavioral intentions. Respondents who reported a lack of neighborhood connections 
had a 6-point lower predicted intention, while those expressing low trust in government disaster management had a 5-point reduction.

4.3. Calibrating the behavioral evacuation threshold
To translate continuous intention scores into binary behavioral outcomes, we calibrated the behavioral threshold that separates 
stated willingness from actual evacuation. A conventional cut-off of 0.5—often adopted in behavioral prediction models—would imply 
an unrealistically high evacuation rate of nearly 60%, which is inconsistent with real-world evidence. Empirical data from the 2018 
West Japan Floods show that the average evacuation rate across affected prefectures was only 4.6% (Nikkei, 2018). Because this figure 
includes rural areas, the actual rate in urban contexts such as Hiroshima is likely even lower. To align the simulation with these 
observed urban evacuation patterns, we identified the intention threshold that reproduces a comparable outcome in our model.
Fig. 5 presents the simulated evacuation response under typical conditions of recent Japanese urban flood disasters—specifically, 
9:00 a.m. evacuation time, extremely high perceived risk, some neighbors evacuated, 30-min travel time to shelter, and no privacy at 
the shelter. By incrementally varying the threshold from 0.5 to 0.9 (in 0.01 intervals), we found that the predicted evacuation rate fell 
to approximately 3% when the threshold reached 0.7. This threshold therefore provides the most realistic correspondence between 
modeled intention and observed evacuation behavior.
The resulting cut-off of 0.7 quantifies the substantial inertia that must be overcome before intention becomes action. Conceptually, 
this value marks the activation point within the Evacuation Threshold Framework (ETF), representing the psychological and social 
tipping point beyond which awareness is converted into movement. In practical terms, the result implies that even respondents 
expressing moderately high intentions (around 7 on a 0–10 scale) are unlikely to act unless motivational, social, or environmental cues 
further reinforce their decision.

4.4. Predicted evacuation rates under key scenarios
To operationalize these findings, we simulated predicted evacuation rates under different policy and behavioral scenarios using the 
newly calibrated threshold (0.7). The results, visualized in Fig. 6, demonstrate how modifiable factors—social influence, timing, travel 
time, and shelter quality—jointly determine collective outcomes under extreme flood-risk conditions.
The most striking pattern emerges from social amplification. When respondents perceived an extremely high flood risk but believed 
that no neighbors had evacuated, the model predicted an evacuation rate of only 0.2%. When some neighbors had left, the rate 
increased modestly to 2.6%, while many evacuees raised it to 9.8%, and most neighbors evacuated pushed the rate up to 23.3%. This 
exponential progression confirms that social proof acts as a powerful behavioral multiplier: once visible community action exceeds a 
critical threshold, evacuation cascades rapidly through imitation and normative convergence. These dynamics mirror contagion 
processes found in pro-environmental behavior, underscoring the potential for risk-communication strategies to harness social networks as accelerators of adaptation.
A similarly revealing pattern concerns evacuation timing. Orders issued during the night, especially around 3 a.m., produced the 
lowest predicted evacuation rate (≈ 1.3%), compared with 2.6% at 9 a.m., 2.0% at 3 p.m., and 4.2% at 9 p.m. This sharp nighttime 
decline reflects both perceptual and logistical barriers—darkness, fear of venturing out, and uncertainty about route safety—that 
substantially increase behavioral inertia. Real-world disasters illustrate the same pattern: during the 2018 West Japan Floods, 
many residents of Mabi District in Kurashiki City failed to evacuate after official orders were issued around midnight, and more than 50 
people lost their lives as floodwaters rose rapidly. The simulation results therefore capture not only statistical trends but also the lived 
behavioral reality of nocturnal evacuation failures.
In contrast, travel-time frictions were modest. When the walking time to a shelter increased from 5 to 30 min, the predicted rate 
declined from 4.2% to 2.6%, and even 45 min reduced intention only slightly (3.2%). These results indicate that while convenience 
matters, its impact is secondary once motivational and social cues are engaged.
The transformative influence of shelter quality was even more pronounced. Under otherwise identical conditions, upgrading from a 
no-privacy shelter (2.6%) to minimal privacy (11.2%), moderate privacy (9.4%), or high privacy (22.1%) dramatically increased 
predicted evacuation rates. The shift from “no privacy” to “high privacy” thus produced nearly a nine-fold increase, a behavioral gain 
comparable to the effect of strong social proof. These findings suggest that dignified, secure, and trust-enhancing shelter environments 
are not peripheral amenities but core instruments of behavioral adaptation.
Overall, these simulations provide behavioral evidence that adaptation outcomes hinge less on physical hazard intensity or 
logistical barriers than on the design of behavioral environments that align motivation, trust, and opportunity. Policies that enhance 
the attractiveness, visibility, and equity of protective actions—by improving shelter dignity, accounting for nighttime barriers, and 
amplifying social proof—can lower the effective behavioral threshold, transforming passive awareness into collective resilience.
Two of the framework's predictions are confirmed quantitatively in these simulations. The exponential progression of evacuation 
rates with peer visibility — 0.2% (none) → 2.6% (some) → 9.8% (many) → 23.3% (most) — confirms Prediction (iii): the populationlevel response to neighbor evacuation is sharply non-linear and accelerates once visibility crosses an inflection point, the contagion 
signature predicted by ETF. The persistence of a 0.2% evacuation rate under “extremely high” perceived risk when no neighbors 
evacuate confirms Prediction (iv): even maximal information-driven risk perception cannot close the intention–action gap when 
contagion and pull cues are absent.
4.5. Summary of key results: tests of ETF predictions
Synthesizing the above analyses, four key empirical findings emerge.
(1) Model performance: The Inferred Valuation model yields more conservative estimates of evacuation propensity than conventional self-reported intention models. This demonstrates the analytical value of indirect elicitation in capturing the hidden determinants of disaster behavior.
(2) Drivers of intention: Risk perception and social influence are the dominant predictors of evacuation intention, while logistical 
frictions—such as travel time or timing—exert only secondary effects. The steep, nonlinear rise in predicted evacuation rates as more 
neighbors evacuate highlights the power of social proof as a behavioral multiplier.
(3) Behavioral threshold: The calibrated activation threshold of 0.7 quantifies the substantial intention–behavior gap underlying 
low evacuation rates in Japan's recent flood disasters. This value marks the psychological point at which awareness transitions into 
action, explaining why even “high” intentions often fail to produce movement.
(4) Policy relevance: Scenario simulations reveal that interventions targeting social visibility (e.g., leveraging community norms) 
and shelter dignity (e.g., ensuring privacy and comfort) produce the largest behavioral gains—up to an order of magnitude increase in 
predicted evacuation. Conversely, nighttime evacuation (e.g., around 3 a.m.) sharply suppresses response rates, echoing the tragic 
outcomes observed in real events such as the 2018 West Japan Floods.

5. Discussion
5.1. Behavioral foundations of evacuation: the psychology of action and inertia
The results reveal that the act of evacuation is governed not by the accuracy of warning systems but by deep-seated psychological 
thresholds that must be surpassed before intention becomes action. The calibrated intention threshold of 0.7 demonstrates empirically 
that even individuals who express moderately high willingness often fail to act. This behavioral inertia reflects the combined influence 
of normalcy bias and status quo bias, whereby individuals underestimate the likelihood of extreme events and favor inaction as the 
default (Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988; Kuhlicke et al., 2020). The threshold thus represents more than a statistical calibration—it 
is a behavioral signal of resistance to change. Similar thresholds have been observed in climate adaptation, where households delay 
adopting protective measures until personal experience or peer behavior disrupts complacency (Grothmann and Patt, 2005; Van 
Valkengoed and Steg, 2019).
From a theoretical standpoint, this finding extends the Protective Action Decision Model (PADM) by introducing a nonlinear 
intention–action linkage. Whereas PADM depicts a sequential process—receiving, believing, personalizing, and acting—our data 
indicate that each stage must surpass a latent threshold of salience and urgency before behavioral activation occurs. This aligns with 
behavioral models of climate adaptation that conceptualize protective action as a tipping process rather than a continuous response 
(Moser and Ekstrom, 2010). It also supports the emerging notion of “behavioral bottlenecks” in adaptation governance, where psychological frictions—not information deficits—constitute the primary barriers to action (Bubeck et al., 2012).
Importantly, the analysis also reveals a temporal barrier to action: evacuation rates drop sharply when warnings are issued during 
the night. In our simulation, orders given at 3 a.m. produced the lowest predicted compliance. This finding is tragically mirrored in real 
events—most notably during the 2018 West Japan Floods, when evacuation orders in Mabi District, Kurashiki City, were issued at 
23:45 and 1:30 a.m., long after residents had gone to bed. Many did not evacuate, and more than 50 people lost their lives as 
floodwaters rose rapidly (Kurashiki City, 2019). This case exemplifies how late-night warnings can inadvertently amplify risk: even 
accurate alerts may fail to trigger movement when issued after circadian and perceptual barriers to action have set in.
From a policy perspective, this insight underscores the importance of anticipatory timing in warning issuance. If there is a foreseeable possibility that an alert may need to be issued late at night, authorities should err on the side of early activation. Proactive 
issuance of warnings before nightfall may increase false alarms but can substantially increase life-saving compliance. In short, delaying 
an evacuation order until midnight may protect institutional credibility but at the cost of human lives.
Notably, the non-linear influence of disaster literacy underscores that knowledge alone cannot guarantee behavioral activation. 
Respondents with both very low and very high literacy exhibited lower evacuation intention than those with moderate literacy. This 
inverted-U relationship challenges the knowledge-deficit model and introduces the concept of cognitive overconfidence: highly 
informed individuals may rationalize non-evacuation by assuming control or superior judgment (Wachinger et al., 2013; Paton, 2013). 
Meanwhile, those with limited literacy may simply fail to comprehend or personalize risk information. Effective risk communication 
must therefore focus on calibrating rather than increasing knowledge—helping individuals to correctly appraise their vulnerability 
and recognize when self-reliance becomes dangerous.
From a policy perspective, behavioral thresholds invite a paradigm shift in early warning systems—from emphasizing technical 
precision to emphasizing behavioral design. Information that remains below the activation threshold will not produce movement, no 
matter how accurate. Warning systems must therefore incorporate cues that heighten psychological immediacy—such as localized 
narratives, emotional salience, or dynamic visualizations—bridging the final gap between awareness and action. These insights position behavioral science as a critical complement to engineering in disaster-risk management, redefining preparedness as the art of 
lowering the threshold for action.
5.2. The behavioral meaning of the activation threshold
The calibrated threshold T* ≈ 0.7 should not be read as a study-specific tuning parameter. Three convergent rationales—drawn 
from stated-preference methodology, prospect theory, and the behavioral economics of inertia—together place the activation point 
systematically above the conventional 0.5 cutoff and elevate it from an empirical artifact to a theoretically grounded behavioral 
quantity.
The first rationale comes from the meta-analytic literature on hypothetical bias in stated-preference research, which finds that 
hypothetical responses systematically overstate actual behavior (Murphy et al., 2005; Loomis, 2011). This directional bias is consistent 
with the upward shift from 0.5 to 0.7 required to align our model with observed evacuation compliance during the 2018 West Japan 
Floods. Because the bias is documented independently of any particular sample or hazard, it provides external support for an activation 
threshold above the conventional midpoint, although the specific magnitude of the shift remains an empirical calibration.
The second and third rationales are psychological. Prospect theory predicts that loss aversion (Tversky and Kahneman, 1991) 
elevates the activation point above any naive midpoint because the disutility of leaving—loss of normalcy, comfort, and control over 
one's home environment—is weighted more heavily than the prospective utility of shelter; the threshold thereby encodes the asymmetric reference dependence that pervades risk-domain decision-making. Reinforcing this, normalcy and status-quo biases (Samuelson 
K. Tanaka et al. Climate Risk Management 54 (2026) 100901

and Zeckhauser, 1988) place a behavioral premium on inaction as the default, requiring abnormally salient cues to displace it. 
Together, these biases predict that protective action requires not merely a balance of perceived costs and benefits but a substantial 
overshoot of intention beyond neutrality.
The convergence of these three independent rationales—methodological, behavioral-economic, and psychological—suggests that 
an activation threshold substantially above 0.5 is not specific to the present sample but reflects a structural property of stated-intention 
data in disaster contexts. Future studies in different populations and hazard types may calibrate slightly different values, but a substantial elevation above 0.5 should be expected as a generic feature of the intention–action gap.
5.3. Social dynamics and collective behavior: evacuation as a social process
The second central insight is that evacuation is not an individual decision in isolation but a collective behavior embedded in social 
networks. The powerful effect of “neighbors evacuating” demonstrates the primacy of social proof: individuals interpret others' actions 
as credible signals of reality under uncertainty (Cialdini and Goldstein, 2004; Centola, 2018). In our model, the presence of visible 
community action increased evacuation intention by about 20 percentage points—an effect nearly as large as perceiving “extremely 
high risk.” This finding confirms that social contagion, rather than rational calculation, often governs crisis response.
Social influence theory suggests that imitation in high-uncertainty environments serves adaptive purposes—it substitutes social 
information for incomplete personal data. Yet, in modern disaster management, official communications are typically designed as oneway broadcasts, detached from this social logic. Integrating peer cues into warning design—e.g., messages such as “many households 
nearby have already evacuated”—could dramatically enhance compliance by legitimizing protective behavior as normative. Empirical 
evidence from other pro-social domains, such as energy conservation and vaccination uptake, shows that social-norm messaging can 
outperform factual appeals (Goldstein et al., 2008; Benartzi et al., 2017). The same principle can be strategically deployed in flood risk 
communication, where real-time visualization of local evacuation activity can transform passive awareness into social momentum.
However, reliance on social cues introduces a critical inequality: communities with stronger social capital are better positioned to 
trigger collective action, while socially isolated individuals remain inert. Trust and reciprocity thus become behavioral infrastructure. 
Our analysis revealed that low trust in government disaster management and weak social ties reduced evacuation intention by 5–6 
percentage points. This echoes Adger's (2010) argument that social capital constitutes adaptive capacity—those embedded in cohesive 
networks not only access information faster but also perceive collective efficacy. Yet excessive trust in institutions can backfire, 
breeding complacency or “blind trust bias,” where individuals expect authorities to manage risks for them (Tierney, 2014). Effective 
governance therefore requires a balanced ecosystem of trust: sufficient faith in institutions to heed warnings, but enough autonomy 
within communities to self-mobilize.
These findings highlight the necessity of shifting from individual-based communication to community-based activation systems. 
Policies that invest in neighborhood organizations, volunteer networks, and local disaster councils do more than distribute 
information—they create the social scaffolding through which action diffuses. In this light, building social capital is not a post-disaster 
recovery goal but a form of anticipatory adaptation. Equitable adaptation must recognize that social connectedness is itself a determinant of safety.
5.4. Incentive design and adaptive infrastructure: from push to pull
A third major contribution of this study lies in quantifying the immense leverage of positive incentives. Simulations revealed that 
improving shelter privacy increased the predicted evacuation rate nearly nine-fold—far exceeding the marginal effects of logistical 
frictions like travel time or time of day. This confirms that individuals are more responsive to attractive opportunities than to coercive 
pressures. Behavioral economics describes this as the asymmetry between push and pull: while removing barriers is necessary, genuine 
motivation arises from perceived gains (Thaler and Sunstein, 2008).
Psychologically, evacuation is framed not as the avoidance of loss but as a transition toward a safer, more dignified state. When 
shelters are perceived as chaotic or unsafe, staying home—despite evident danger—appears rational under the logic of loss aversion 
(Tversky and Kahneman, 1991). Conversely, when shelters offer privacy, comfort, and security, they generate a positive utility that can 
offset inertia. This insight extends beyond disaster contexts to the broader field of adaptive infrastructure design. Public facilities are 
not neutral spaces; they function as behavioral architectures that can invite or repel participation (Gehl, 2013). By designing shelters as 
psychologically supportive environments, authorities convert evacuation from a reluctant necessity into a socially acceptable and 
emotionally viable choice.
The implications for policy are profound. Investments in shelter quality—often dismissed as secondary to warning 
technology—constitute a behavioral adaptation strategy. Providing privacy partitions, clean sanitation, and medical support particularly benefits vulnerable groups such as the elderly, the sick, or caregivers of dependents, who face the steepest behavioral barriers. 
Our findings thus bridge behavioral science and social equity: improving comfort and dignity is not a matter of luxury but of justice. In 
this sense, dignified safety becomes a measurable public good.
Furthermore, integrating behavioral incentives into spatial planning can yield systemic resilience. Municipalities can develop a 
tiered network of “magnet shelters,” strategically located and designed to serve as both safe havens and visible signals of collective 
movement. These facilities can also double as community hubs during normal times, reinforcing social ties that later facilitate coordinated evacuation. Such multifunctional design embodies the core principle of adaptive infrastructure—spaces that evolve with 
both environmental and behavioral contingencies.

5.5. Implications for climate adaptation governance
Taken together, these findings call for a re-conceptualization of climate adaptation as behavioral governance. Traditional approaches equate adaptation with physical protection—levees, sensors, and forecasts—while neglecting the social and psychological 
dimensions that determine whether such systems actually save lives. Our results demonstrate that effective adaptation depends on 
lowering behavioral thresholds, activating social contagion, and designing environments that make protective choices easy, attractive, 
and equitable. These components form a unified framework for behaviorally informed adaptation policy.
At the institutional level, this implies a shift from risk communication to risk orchestration. Agencies must coordinate information, 
incentives, and community engagement as interdependent levers rather than isolated instruments. Embedding behavioral expertise 
within disaster-management agencies could enable the design of messages and infrastructures that resonate with human cognition. 
Moreover, the recognition of social capital and trust as adaptive assets demands cross-sectoral collaboration—linking urban planning, 
health care, and social welfare under the umbrella of resilience policy.
Equity considerations further reinforce the need for a governance model that prioritizes capability over mere exposure reduction 
(Adger et al., 2009). Vulnerable populations—those in poor health, limited mobility, or social isolation—face compounded disadvantages across behavioral, social, and physical dimensions. An inclusive adaptation strategy must therefore guarantee not only access 
to warnings but also the practical and psychological means to act upon them. This entails targeted investment in accessible shelters, 
localized outreach programs, and participatory drills that normalize collective action.
While this study is situated in Japan's urban flood context, its implications are globally transferable. The behavioral thresholds 
identified here—rooted in cognitive biases, social contagion, and perceived dignity—are not culturally unique but structurally universal across hazard contexts. Comparative research across Asia and Europe could test the generalizability of this behavioral framework, advancing the global understanding of climate adaptation as a social process.
Finally, the study underscores the potential of integrating behavioral metrics into adaptation assessment. Traditional evaluations 
focus on physical outcomes—such as reduced flood depth or infrastructure loss—but overlook behavioral indicators like evacuation 
uptake or response latency. Measuring these outcomes can provide a more accurate gauge of real resilience. In an era where climate 
risks increasingly outpace institutional capacity, adaptive governance must evolve toward systems that not only inform but actively 
mobilize populations. In short, saving lives in the Anthropocene will depend less on how well we forecast hazards, and more on how 
well we design societies that are psychologically prepared to move.
5.6. Limitations and future directions
The present analysis carries three sets of limitations, each pointing to a specific extension of the framework. They concern, in turn, 
the methodology of stated-intention elicitation, the scope of the empirical setting, and the theoretical treatment of the activation 
threshold itself. Setting these out explicitly clarifies what the study has and has not established, and identifies the most productive 
avenues for follow-up research.
The first set concerns the underlying use of stated rather than revealed preferences. Although the Inferred Valuation design substantially attenuates social-desirability bias by re-routing the response through respondents' inferences about neighbors, residual 
hypothetical-response bias cannot be eliminated entirely; integration with revealed-preference data from real disaster events—postevent evacuation registries, mobility traces, or shelter occupancy records—would further strengthen the framework's empirical 
grounding. A closely related concern is that, when respondents form Inferred-Valuation judgments about their neighbors, the social 
reference group is implicitly self-defined; in heterogeneous neighborhoods with mixed age, language, or mobility profiles, this 
reference group may be poorly anchored, and explicit elicitation of the relevant peer set is a useful methodological extension.
The second set concerns the empirical setting and design. The study focuses on a single metropolitan area (Hiroshima City), and 
although the behavioral mechanisms identified here are theorized to be structurally general, cross-city and cross-hazard 
replication—particularly in coastal Asian cities facing comparable urban-flood compounding—will be required before strong claims 
of generalizability can be made. In addition, the factorial survey design holds attribute combinations at orthogonally varied levels and 
does not directly model higher-order interactions among amplifiers and frictions; subsequent work could specify and estimate such 
interactions to refine the framework's policy implications, particularly for understanding how multiple pull factors (such as shelter 
dignity combined with strong social proof) jointly shift the activation point.
The third and most theoretically substantive limitation concerns the treatment of the activation threshold itself. The present 
analysis takes T* as exogenous and population-level—a single calibrated constant—and the framework's amplifiers and frictions 
therefore operate by moving intention along the horizontal axis of Fig. 2 rather than by modifying T* directly. A more general 
formulation in which T* itself becomes endogenous to context—varying across individuals (e.g., by mobility status or prior experience), hazards (riverine versus coastal versus landslide), or institutional settings (warning trust, governance quality)—is conceptually 
consistent with the ETF and a natural target of follow-up research. We anticipate addressing this generalization in a forthcoming 
companion study using a finite-mixture beta-regression approach with covariate-dependent threshold estimation. Together, these 
extensions promise a richer behavioral architecture in which the threshold itself becomes a designable feature of climate-adaptation 
governance, deepening rather than displacing the empirical framework developed here.

This study investigated the complex determinants of flood evacuation decisions, employing an Inferred Valuation (IV) methodology

to provide a realistic assessment less affected by social desirability bias. Our findings reveal three critical insights into the challenge of 
promoting public safety in the face of catastrophic flooding.
First, a profound intention–behavior gap persists, as evidenced by the high activation threshold of 0.7 required to align simulated 
intentions with observed evacuation rates of only a few percent. This empirically quantifies the powerful psychological inertia—rooted 
in normalcy and status quo biases—that inhibits protective action even under extreme risk. The finding underscores that warnings 
alone, however precise, cannot overcome this latent behavioral resistance.
Second, our analyses identify the strongest behavioral drivers unequivocally: evacuation is dictated less by logistical barriers and 
more by risk perception and, above all, social proof. When residents observe others taking protective action, intention rises nonlinearly, triggering collective evacuation cascades. Conversely, the timing of warnings plays a crucial role. Orders issued during latenight hours—such as those in Mabi District, Kurashiki City during the 2018 West Japan Floods—face profound psychological and 
logistical barriers, often resulting in fatal inaction. These insights emphasize the necessity of anticipatory decision-making and the lifesaving value of issuing alerts before nightfall whenever potential escalation is foreseen.
Third, the study reveals that neither knowledge nor infrastructure alone guarantees safety. High disaster literacy may foster 
overconfidence rather than compliance, while dignified and private shelter environments can increase predicted evacuation rates 
nearly nine-fold. The implication is clear: evacuation behavior responds not to information quantity but to the quality of behavioral 
environments—those that align motivation, trust, and opportunity.
The practical and policy implications of these findings are substantial. For emergency managers, risk communication must evolve 
from broadcasting information to orchestrating behavior. Messages should integrate social-norm cues (“many households nearby have 
already evacuated”) and leverage real-time visibility of community actions. Moreover, if late-night issuance is likely, proactive prenightfall alerts should be institutionalized as standard operating practice. Simultaneously, investment in dignified 
shelters—offering privacy, sanitation, and safety—should be recognized not as amenities but as essential behavioral infrastructure. 
These dual strategies—enhancing social visibility and environmental dignity—form the behavioral foundation of climate-resilient 
evacuation systems.
In conclusion, this research provides a behaviorally grounded understanding of how individuals and communities respond to flood 
risks. It demonstrates that saving lives depends not only on the accuracy of forecasts but on our ability to design environments—social, 
physical, and psychological—that pull people toward safety. As climate extremes intensify, adaptation policy must embrace this 
behavioral paradigm: resilience is not merely about resisting hazards, but about enabling movement. Understanding and shaping 
human behavior is no longer an adjunct to adaptation—it is its very core.
