ABSTRACT
Studying human consequences caused by landslides and floods is important to understand their 
impact and to investigate public risk perception. We analysed a catalogue of 1188 landslide and 
752 flood fatalities that occurred in Italy during the 60-year period 1965–2024, for which information on sex, age, and circumstances of death was available. Observed fatalities were 
compared with expected distributions modelled from census data by sex and age for two nonoverlapping 30-year periods. With the exception of children, male fatalities were consistently 
over-represented relative to the census-based demographic expectations, whereas female fatalities were generally under-represented. To investigate possible relationships with risk perception, 
we analysed responses from four nationwide surveys conducted in 2012, 2013, 2015, and 2023. 
Women reported slightly higher levels of self-perceived exposure to natural and technological 
risks than men, whereas no substantial gender differences emerged in the perceived threat posed 
by landslides and floods to personal safety. The national-scale analysis was complemented by a 
regional comparison between perceived threat and historical mortality. Regional Threat Indices 
were calculated separately for floods and landslides across the 20 Italian regions. A moderate and 
statistically significant positive association emerged between perceived flood threat and flood 
mortality, whereas no association was found for landslide mortality. Overall, the results highlight 
a persistent mismatch between gender patterns in mortality and perceived personal threat and 
support targeted, gender-sensitive risk communication and disaster risk reduction strategies.

of fatalities worldwide [1–4]. Reducing the loss of life and the number of people affected by natural disasters, including landslides and 
floods, is an expected outcome of the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (DDR) 2015–2030 [5] that integrates and

emphasizes the importance of a gender perspective in DRR policies and practices worldwide [6]. As stated by Erman et al. [7], natural 
hazards are gender neutral, however, their effects are not. Gender dynamics impact both the way men and women are affected by 
disasters and their capacity to withstand and recover from them. Neumayer and Plümper [8] emphasised how women were more 
adversely affected by natural disasters and that, on average, disasters kill more women than men mostly depending on the severity of 
the disaster and the poor socio - economic status of the women. The higher the status of women, the smaller the differential negative 
effect on mortality, which means that men and women die in roughly equal numbers. With particular reference to the context of the 
developing world researchers [9–12] highlights how women's vulnerability (and men's vulnerability as well, albeit in a different way) 
to environmental risks is linked to caregiving roles, differences in access to resources, and cultural norms that shape behaviour in crisis 
situations. Conversely, in high - income contexts, disaster - related mortality is higher for men than for women. This appears to be due 
to exposure [7] and their willingness to take risks [13] during damaging events [14]. showed that in USA and Europe men account for 
70% of flood - related deaths. Men are often over-represented in high-risk rescue work and other outdoor activities, increasing their 
direct exposure to natural hazards and often results in more fatalities [15–17]. Understanding the relationship between types of 
disaster and men's and women's mortality would shed further light on how natural disasters impact them differently. Causes, circumstances and behaviour that led to death due to a geo-hydrological hazard have already been examined ([18] and references 
therein), while gender differences due to such phenomena are less investigated particularly in terms of how risk is perceived across 
sexes. Perception of risk depends on the subjective judgment and evaluation by an individual of a specific risk [19–21]. However, what 
a person perceives as potentially dangerous (i.e., risky), another person may consider safe (i.e., free of risk). Researchers agree that 
people's behaviour, decisions and actions are dependent on their perceptions [22,23], and the gender differences in risk perception has 
become an important research agenda in disaster risk science [12]. Numerous studies have suggested that, on average, women show a 
slightly higher risk perception than men, regarding environmental, health [24] and natural risks [12,25,26]. This gap has been 
interpreted as the result of different “risk cultures”, whereby men are often socialized to emphasize invulnerability and suppress fear, 
whereas women are more often encouraged to adopt caution and attentiveness to potential threats [27]. Some authors hypothesise that 
men may downplay certain dangers due to social pressures tied to self-presentation as “brave” or “capable of handling risk,” whereas 
women, often bearing caregiving responsibilities and tasked with safeguarding family well-being, develop greater risk awareness and a 
tendency to adopt preventive or protective behaviours [28]. Gender is not purely a biological variable but a cultural and relational 
dimension that defines different expectations, duties, and roles for men and women [28]. Enarson [29] points out that there are 
situations where men's and women's risk perceptions converge, especially when a community frequently experiences certain hazards 
and develops protection and preparedness habits involving both genders. We can therefore state that gender differences in risk 
perception should be viewed as a complex phenomenon in which social roles, cultural values, information opportunities, and territorial context interact dynamically, producing patterns that vary significantly from one situation to another [27,30,31].
The goal of the present study is to investigate the relationship between landslide and flood mortality and risk perception in the 
Italian context, and to assess whether differences in risk perception may contribute to understanding gender-related differences in 
responses to potentially fatal geo-hydrological events. Although awareness of landslide and flood risk perception is increasing (e.g., 
[32]), Italy still has limited quantitative studies that incorporate a gender perspective in this field of research at the synoptic scale. In 
particular, this work will focus on three key questions. 
1. How does the gender dimension manifest itself in mortality patterns related to landslides and floods?
2. Are there gender differences in how landslide and flood risks are perceived?
3. What are the temporal trends in the perception of threats to personal safety?
The main objective and novelty of this work lie in combining the analysis of risk perception with the distribution and variation of 
mortality (due to landslides and floods) by sex, in order to highlight a possible relationship between them. The ultimate goal is to 
identify the most vulnerable or exposed categories of people to geo-hydrological events, with the aim of enhancing community 
resilience and promoting greater social equity when responding to and preventing natural disasters.
2. Research methodology and data
The methodological design of this research is quantitative. To adopt a rigorous approach to gender analysis, we follow the decision 
tree proposed in Tannenbaum et al. [33] and apply it to data on flood and landslide fatalities, for which information was collected by 
sex. Data on risk perception come from four nationwide surveys carried out over an eleven-year period. We first analysed the temporal 
distribution of landslide and flood fatality data in Italy over the 60-year period 1965–2024, disaggregated by sex. Similarly, the results 
of the four surveys were analysed by considering gender-based differences in risk perception. We then integrated these results by 
developing an index aimed at relating landslide and flood mortality data to the perceived threat of losing one's life due to a landslide or 
flood.
In this study, the term “sex” is used to refer to the presumed biological sex inferred from the victims’ names. The term “gender”, in 
addition to its occurrence in bibliographic citations, is used when referring to survey responses disaggregated by men and women.
2.1. Geo-hydrological fatalities in Italy by sex
Extensive and detailed information on the consequences caused by geo-hydrological events on the Italian population is known and 
has been collected in the historical catalogue of landslide and flood events with direct consequences to the population [4,13,17,34,35].

The most up-to-date version of the national catalogue lists 2209 fatalities in the 60-year period 1965–2024, this corresponds to an 
average of at least 37 fatalities per year (minimum, one, in 1997, maximum, 285, in 1985). The data allows to quantitatively estimate 
levels of individual risk using the mortality rates given by the number of landslide and flood fatalities (deaths and missing persons) per 
100,000 people over one year [36,37]. At the national scale, the average flood and landslide mortality is 0.07 in the considered period, 
i.e. seven lives lost every 10 million people per year, with the average landslide-related mortality rate (0.04) higher than the 
flood-related mortality rate (0.03).
For this work, we used a set of 1188 landslide and 752 flood fatalities that occurred in Italy during the 60-year period 1965–2024, 
for which joint information on sex, age and exact time of occurrence of the fatal events, and type of landslide and flood are available. 
This subset corresponds to the 90% and the 85%, respectively, of the total landslide and flood fatalities that occurred in the 60-year 
period in Italy. We consider it to be a representative sample of the human losses that have occurred in Italy as a result of geohydrological events. During the study period, landslides and floods in Italy caused more fatalities among males (1141; 58.8%) than 
among females (799; 41.2%). In particular, 670 males lost their lives due to landslides and 471 due to floods, while 518 females died 
due to landslides and 281 due to floods.
2.2. Data on risk percetion
To understand the public perception of geo-hydrological risks and assess awareness levels in Italy, four national surveys have been 
designed and commissioned since 2012 by two leading Italian companies. Doxa Italia executed three surveys (in 2012, 2013 and 2015) 
using their Computer Assisted Telephone Interview (CATI) system and their omnibus samples. Istituto Piepoli executed a fourth survey 
in 2023 used the CATI/CAMI/CAWI (Computer Aided Telephone/Mobile/Web Interview) system. Across all surveys, 10,709 people 
were interviewed and answered five questions related to risk perception, four of which were included in each survey (Table S1 in 
supplementary material). We asked the companies to carry out the questionnaires to ensure that the sample was truly representative of 
the Italian population, both in terms of stratification and in terms of size. The sampling strategy used a classification based on demographic variables, including: (i) the size and distribution of the population in each Italian region, (ii) gender by age, (iii) education, 
and (iv) occupation. Specifically, questionnaires were designed to encourage respondents first to reflect on their general feelings about 
environmental and natural risks and then on their specific understanding and fear of landslides and floods. To our knowledge, this 
long-term effort to monitor risk perception related to geo-hydrological processes is unique at the national level in Italy. In the design of 
the 2023 survey, particular attention was paid to the distribution of responses by gender and age. A weighting process was applied by 
Doxa and Piepoli to all samples during the analysis, to have an accurate and proportional reconstruction with respect to gender and age; 
in this way, it is possible to take advantage of valid information and data for the whole sample. The results of the 2012 and 2013 
surveys, analysed at the regional scale in Italy, were previously published by Salvati et al. [38]. In that study, the authors compared 
regional levels of risk perception with the individual risk estimated for each Italian region, for both landslides and floods. Differently, 
the present study analyses the results of four surveys aggregated at the national level, with the aim of updating knowledge on risk 
perception in Italy and identifying potential temporal trends over approximately ten years from a gender perspective. The national 
scale was selected to allow a consistent comparison across the four surveys and to investigate temporal and gender-related patterns. 
Risk perception is also influenced by the geographical distribution and frequency of hazards as well as by previous experience of 
hazardous events and respondents’ age. Therefore, the local dimension was incorporated into the survey, as respondents were asked to 
refer to their municipality or surrounding area when assessing hazard frequency and previous experience (Q2 and Q3 in Table S1).
3. Data analysis
3.1. Modelled versus empirical fatalities by sex and age
In answering the first research question regarding how the gender dimension manifest itself in mortality patterns, we analysed the 
distribution of landslide and flood fatalities compared to the census data disaggregated by sex and age. For this purpose, we adopted a 
statistical approach, first used by Salvati et al. [17], to assess whether the observed differences in geo-hydrological fatalities by sex, 
were significant and representative, given the relatively small number of fatalities (a few hundred) compared to the size of the 
population of Italy. In addition, to explore possible temporal variations in how men and women experienced geo-hydrological hazards, 
we split the 60-year period 1965–2024 dataset of fatalities in two, non-overlapping 30-year data subsets: t30y-1 (1965–1994) and 
t30y-2 (1995–2024). To model the expected number of fatalities based on census data, we averaged information from the Italian 
general censuses conducted every ten years by ISTAT (Italian National Institute of Statistics) in 1961, 1971, 1981 and 1991 to the 
t30y-1 (1965–1994) subset. Similarly, for the more recent t30y-2 (1995–2024) subset, the 1991, 2001, 2011 and 2021 general censuses were used. The census data provide the demographic reference distribution used to model the expected fatalities. Thus, the 
expected distributions account for differences in the demographic composition of the male and female populations by age. This demographic reference should be distinguished from event-specific exposure, i.e. the actual number of males and females present in 
hazardous locations at the time of individual landslide or flood events, which cannot be systematically reconstructed from the 
available historical information. The mean demographic distributions by sex and age used for the two periods are shown in Fig. S1 in 
the supplementary material. These distributions explicitly account for differences in the relative size and age structure of the male and 
female populations when estimating the expected number of fatalities. Using these data, a large set of samples was produced randomly 
drawn from a multinomial distribution, with each sample having the same size (number) of fatalities as in the specific sex and age 
category. Using the Gamma function notation [39], the probability mass function of the multinomial distribution is given by,

3.2. Behavioural analysis by sex
In order to better investigate the dependency of male and female fatalities on their adopted behaviour, we calculated the ratio of 
male to female fatalities, separately for landslides and floods, for some situation for which we know the exact place people were and 
activities people were doing (Table 1). The majority of the road–related fatalities were male both for flood (ratio = 1.8) and landslide 
processes (ratio = 3.2). During a flood event people killed along the levees or embankment were mostly males (ratio = 9.7), and the 
crossing ford fatalities were males (ratio = 2.3). Of the 66 males that died along levees, underpass or crossing a ford only the 15% were 
there because workers (e.g. farmer, shepherd). Overall, of the 111 victims with evidence of risk underestimation and therefore 
inappropriate behaviour (i.e. trying to care for and secure goods and personal belongings, going down to a lower level, accessing 
restricted areas), gathered from survivor testimonies and chronicles and news reports, the vast majority (79%) were males and only 
21% females. The male percentage while attempting to rescue others is also very high (92%), showing their readiness to take risks to 
save lives, both as trained rescuers (35%), and as occasional citizen rescuers (65%). These results suggest sex-related differences in 
behavioural responses and in the circumstances in which fatal exposure occurred.

3.4. Fear-Index and Regional Threat Index analysis
The temporal analysis of responses to Question 4 across the four surveys indicates that no substantial differences emerged between 
the sexes regarding the perceived fear of being affected by a landslide or a flood. Although perceived fear was similar between men and 
women, mortality rates differed markedly. Using Italian census data, we calculated the cumulative mortality rates for landslides and 
floods by sex over the 60-year period 1965–2024. The mortality rate was 2.8 and 4.2 for women and men, respectively, i.e. one and a 
half times higher for men.
We then compared mortality data with the results of Question 4 (Q4 in Table S1), which directly explores whether respondents 
perceive landslides and floods as a threat to their personal safety. For this purpose, we calculated the Fear-Index, cumulatively for 
floods and landslides, as the male-to-female ratio of “yes” responses (yes–flood; yes–landslide) and “no” responses. The Fear-Index 
values were very similar, being 1.02 for “no” and 0.97 for “yes”, indicating comparable levels of perceived threat among men and 
women. However, when these values were compared with the mortality rates, a marked discrepancy emerged between perceived fear 
and observed mortality.
To further investigate the relationship between perceived threat and the observed mortality rate, we performed an additional 
analysis at the regional scale, considering floods and landslides separately (Fig. 6A). Regional historical mortality rates for both 
hazards are available for Italy for the 50-year period 1975–2024 from the POLARIS website (https://polaris.irpi.cnr.it/report/). Using 
respondents' region of residence, we calculated two Regional Threat Indices for each Italian region: the Flood Regional Threat Index 
(FRTI) and the Landslide Regional Threat Index (LRTI). The indices were calculated as the ratio between positive and negative responses to Question 4 pooling males and females responses for each hazard. Values greater than 1 indicate a predominance of positive 
responses, reflecting a higher perceived threat to personal safety, whereas values lower than 1 indicate a predominance of negative 
responses and, therefore, a lower perceived threat. Spearman's rank correlation was used to assess the association between regional

census data, is consistently lower than the number actually recorded due to landslides and floods. Based on the information recorded

Our statistical analysis by gender and age in the two different 30-year periods t30y-1 (1965–1994) and t30y-2 (1995–2024) 
confirmed that, apart from children, the landslide and flood male fatalities are above the expected (modelled) values. The observed

number of fatalities (50th), given the size of the population in the same genders and age categories. In detail, in the period t30y-1 
(1965–1994) males are over represented in the range 20 years and older up to the age of 89, and in the more recent period t30y-2 
(1995–2024) males in the range 30 years and older up to the age of 89. This trend may reflect differences in both risk-taking propensity and exposure between males and females [50]. We note that in the studied period (1965–2024) males were certainly more 
numerous than females in outdoor works and activities, and that until recently rescue services (e.g., fire fighters, police, and defence 
forces) consisted entirely of males and therefore people involved in rescue operations were only males. It is confirmed by the available 
(few) number of workers involved in a fatal landslide or flood that are 97.7% males and only 2.3% females. To our knowledge, these 
kinds of male workers involved in the fatal events represent a minor percentage (7.5%) of all the males killed by a landslide or a flood 
recorded in the catalogue. Although these observations support the hypothesis of differential exposure between males and females, the 
available data do not allow event-specific exposure to be quantified systematically over the entire 60-year period. We consider the 
overrepresentation of males to be independent of their efforts in working activities or in rescue operations. It seems likely that other 
important causes include males' greater propensity for risky behaviour than females [52]. Our gender analysis identified some main 
hazardous behaviour (Table 1) for males, in particular during flooding events: fording a river with a vehicle, moving towards (or 
staying on) the banks or levees of a river, staying on a bridge during a flood, trying to care for and secure goods and personal belongings, going down to a lower level or accessing restricted areas. The data show that the people, in the majority males, were 
generally unaware or underestimated the dangerousness of these potentially deadly actions. As reported by Pearson and Hamilton [53] 
in their study on willingness to drive through flooded waterways, men were more willing to drive through the flooded waterway in the 
20 cm scenario than women. Male drivers expected themselves to have adequate driving skills in uncommon conditions, and misjudged the ability of their vehicles to stay or travel on a road covered by water, debris or mud. The existence of gender differences in 
propensity to take risks has been documented in a large number of studies in the literature [52,54–56] and has been linked to different 
factors, such as a different perception of risk or interpreted as “showing off” for potential mates, as males are more likely to take risks 
when there are females present around, whereas the reverse is not true [57]. In recent study the close association between risk-seeking 
behaviour and the emotional-regulation network led Bajaj and Killgore [58] to hypothesize that the sex differences in risk tacking 
propensity would be associated with sex differences in brain morphometry of the limbic network. Kruger and Nesse [59] studying the 
external causes of death resulting directly from behaviour that contributes to the human sex differential in mortality, found that almost 
all cases showing an increase in male mortality.
The aim of our research was to analyse whether differences in landslide and flood mortality by gender exist and how these differences can be related to risk perception. There is a broad consensus among social scientists that considerable gender disparities in risk 
perception exist, particularly in the context of natural risk [60,61].
To this end, we used perception data from four national surveys to investigate different dimensions of risk perception, including 
self-perceived exposure, perceived hazard frequency, direct or indirect knowledge of previous events, and perceived threat to personal 
safety.
The broad timeframe under study, with surveys from 2012 to 2023, allows, for the first time in Italy, to identify certain evolving 
trends, revealing both progress in collective awareness and persistent challenges arising from the unequal distribution of information 
and differences in the social roles of men and women. Three main lines of reasoning emerge from these survey findings. First, it is 
evident that geo-hydrological risk perception struggles to become as prominent in public consciousness as other threats (Figs. 3 and 4). 
Analysis of the survey's responses showed that people in Italy feel more exposed to technological risks (environmental pollution and 
car accidents) than to natural risks and this in line with previous research [21]. Of the natural risks, people in Italy feel more exposed to 
earthquakes, followed by floods, landslides, and volcanic eruptions. Focusing on Italy, Guzzetti et al. [62] and Salvati et al. [35] found 
a similar ranking when estimating individual [36] and societal risk [63] levels associated with different natural hazards. In 2023 
survey, we examined the responses to Question Q1 disaggregated by gender (Fig. 3). In general, both men and women reported 
relatively low levels of perceived exposure to floods and landslides (Fig. 3A). Although it is necessary to consider the 2% uncertainty, 
females are slightly more likely to acknowledge some degree of exposure of 'considerably' answers (5.1% and 4.1% for landslides and 
floods, respectively), higher than men's responses (3.9% and 4.0% for landslides and floods, respectively). The results are in line with 
previous studies on risk perception for natural hazards [12,25,26].
When comparing the 2023 survey with those conducted in the past years, in none of the annual surveys did more than 30% of 
respondents feel “considerably” or “somewhat” exposed to landslides or floods (Fig. 3B). This suggests a persistently low self-perceived 
exposure to landslide and flood hazards, despite frequent media reports of disastrous events in different parts of the country (https:// 
polaris.irpi.cnr.it/). Geo-hydrological risks often seem distant and sporadic, possibly because many landslides and floods are

“localized”—while they may devastate specific areas, they do not always trigger nationwide media coverage comparable to that of a 
catastrophic earthquake. For instance, men were more likely to see themselves as “not at all” exposed to landslides and floods, whereas 
women displayed a slightly greater inclination to recognize their vulnerability (Fig. 3B). These data confirm how men show a stronger 
tendency to consider themselves not exposed to or distant from the dangers posed by landslides and floods—an attitude that may 
reflect cultural models linked to invulnerability and the denial of fear, as discussed in the literature on “risk cultures” [64]. This lower 
self-perceived exposure among men may be consistent with the greater propensity for risk-taking behaviour reported in the literature.
Women, on the other hand, tend to express a more cautious perception and appear to have internalized more preventive attitudes 
likely linked to social and family roles that place them in a position of heightened awareness of vulnerability. Special selection 
pressures faced by females may also have increased gender differences in the tendency to engage in risky behaviours [65]. These roles 
prompt women to take the possibility of danger more seriously and to adopt more cautious behaviour. The more cautious responses 
provided by women may explain the significant discrepancies in the data relating to female and male fatalities (Fig. 1).
At the same time, in terms of frequency estimate (Q2) and direct or indirect knowledge (Q3) of geo-hydrological events (Fig. 4A–D), 
women appear less informed about the frequency of landslides and floods in the territories they lived and of the knowledge of floods. 
The percentages of both men and women of “no” answers showed that half of the Italian population stated that they have not 
experienced, either directly or indirectly, a landslide or flood in the municipality in which they live or in the surrounding area. This is 
surprising, especially considering that Italy is one of the most hazard-prone countries in Europe. All the 20 Italian regions have 
experienced recurrent landslides or floods, and 107 out of 110 (97%) Italian provinces have experienced at least one landslide or flood 
with direct consequences to the population (https://polaris.irpi.cnr.it/report/last-report/).The Italian landslide inventory map of the 
IFFI project, carried out by ISPRA and the Regions and Autonomous Provinces since 1998, has been updated several times and the 
latest figure on the number of landslides detected is 620,808 [66], which is a density of 2.4 landslides per km2 and they affect an area of 
almost 24,000 km2
, or 7.9% of the national territory. This apparently very high figure represents only the "tip of the iceberg" of 
landslides in our country. It is enough to say that more than 80,997 new landslides were recorded during the two alluvial events in 
Emilia-Romagna in May 2023, when heavy rainfall hit the region and 21 rivers overflowed [67].
The survey results show that half of the Italian population ignored the risk of landslides and floods, but there is not an enormous 
gender gap. The small difference (5%) may reflect differences in previous experience, mobility, occupational context, or access to 
information.
The third major finding is the overall increase in the perception of threat to personal safety, rising from about 35% in 2012 to 51% 
in 2023. This is an encouraging trend, suggesting that the public is gradually recognising that landslides and floods can indeed 
jeopardise human life and well-being. However, when responses were disaggregated by gender, men and women showed very similar 
levels of perceived threat (Fig. 5), in marked contrast with the substantial differences observed in male and female fatalities. The FearIndex, used to quantitatively compare the perceived fear (Q4) of being a victim of a landslide or flood event with the mortality rate, 
confirmed that there are comparable levels of perceived fear among men and women confirmed this discrepancy between perceived 
threat and observed mortality. We explain the mismatch between the perceived risk of threat and the react to a potentially fatal actual 
risky situation, by considering that the latter can be the result of a sudden, instinctive reaction to an unexpected event rather than a 
response to a survey mediated by a cognitive, albeit quick, reasoning process. As argued by Kruger and Nesse [65], however, male 
mortality results from complex interactions among sex, behaviour, and culture, thus precluding any simple explanation. Nevertheless, 
the greater propensity of males to engage in risky behaviour remains an important factor to consider in the prevention of avoidable 
deaths associated with natural hazards.
This study was primarily designed to provide a synoptic overview of gender differences in perception and mortality patterns across 
Italy, with the aim of understanding the limits of knowledge, awareness, and perception through a broad comparative analysis. 
However, because the spatial distribution, frequency, and experience of landslide and flood hazards vary considerably across Italy, the 
national-scale perspective was complemented by a regional comparison between perceived threat and mortality, conducted separately 
for landslides and floods. Further disaggregation by gender would have resulted in subsamples that were too small to provide statistically robust estimates. The regional analysis should therefore be regarded as complementary to the gender-based analysis, which 
remains the main focus of this study.
The regional comparison revealed contrasting patterns between the two hazards. A moderate and statistically significant positive 
association emerged between regional flood mortality and the perceived flood threat measured using the regional threat index (FRTI 
Spearman's ρ = 0.53, p = 0.016; n = 20) whereas no significant association was found between landslide mortality and the perceived 
landslide threat (LRTI Spearman's ρ = 0.02, p = 0.940; n = 20). This suggests that the spatial pattern of historically observed flood 
mortality is, at least partly, reflected in regional perceptions of personal threat, while no comparable relationship emerges for 
landslides.
One possible explanation may lie in differences in the spatial extent and public visibility of the impacts produced by the two 
hazards. During extreme weather-related events, floods and landslides may occur simultaneously [68–70], but floods often affect 
wider areas and larger populations and may therefore receive greater public and media attention. Landslide impacts, by contrast, are 
generally more spatially localised as individual slope failures typically affect relatively limited areas [34,71–73], even when numerous 
landslides occur during the same event, and their cumulative effects may consequently be less visible to the general public. More 
generally, these results suggest that geographical differences in risk perception may be influenced not only by historical mortality, but 
also by event frequency, direct or indirect experience, spatial extent, the number of people affected, and public and media visibility. 
Likewise, it is important to scale down the analysis and investigate in greater depth, at the local level, the groups of people found to be 
more vulnerable, less aware, or less prepared. To develop effective plans and allocate resources efficiently, it is crucial to assess the 
critical factors that determine social vulnerability and disaster response capacity from a gender perspective.

5. Conclusions
This study provides a comprehensive assessment of gender differences in flood- and landslide-related mortality in Italy by integrating a unique 60-year fatality database with evidence from four national surveys on risk perception. We used information on floods 
and landslides with human consequences in Italy to analyse possible gender differences in the response to risky situations due to such 
events. Levels of risk perception disaggregated by gender, observed in four national-scale surveys conducted between 2012 and 2023, 
were used to investigate possible relations between the over representation of male fatalities, particularly due to evident risk-taking 
behaviours during geo-hydrological disasters. The national-scale perspective, the granularity of the fatality database, and the integration of long-term empirical evidence with social survey data provide new insights into the relationships between gender, risk-taking 
behaviour, and disaster vulnerability.
Our gender analysis revealed that floods and landslides have killed more males (1141) than females (799) in Italy over the study 
period. We compared the empirical data on fatalities by sex with the multinomial probability mass function of expected fatalities 
estimated using census data by gender and age. The statistical analysis revealed a systematic over-representation of male fatalities, and 
conversely an under-representation of female fatalities when compared with those modelled with the census data that do not change 
significantly over the two non-overlapping 30-year periods (1965–1994 and 1995–2024).
When we analysed the responses to the four surveys by gender, we found differences in risk perception, with women's risk 
perception generally higher than men's. However, we observed a contrasting trend in the number of fatalities by gender and the 
perceived threat to personal safety posed by landslides and floods. The answers to the specific question (Q4) do not show gender 
differences in the surveys. The Fear Index values, measured as the male-to-female ratio of “yes” and “no” responses, resulted quite 
identical, indicating comparable perceived treats among men and women. The results of the national-scale gender analysis were 
complemented by a regional analysis of the Regional Threat Index, calculated separately for landslides (LRTI) and floods (FRTI) across 
the 20 Italian regions and compared with the corresponding historical mortality rates. Spearman's rank correlation, used to assess the 
association between regional perceived threat and mortality, revealed a moderate and statistically significant positive association for 
floods, whereas no significant association was found for landslides. This suggests a closer correspondence between regional patterns of 
perceived threat and historical mortality for floods than for landslides. Overall, these results indicate that risk perception alone cannot 
explain disaster outcomes and that behavioural responses and exposure patterns should also be considered when developing disaster 
risk reduction strategies. The combined results help identify population groups and behavioural contexts that may deserve targeted 
risk communication and prevention strategies that can contribute to reduce the human toll of landslides and floods in Italy, and 
elsewhere. In our research, we attempted to fully consider both the social actors in their entirety and the diversity of gender and 
behaviour in response to risk.
The analysis of the circumstances of deaths revealed unambiguously that (car) drivers, in particular males, often adopted hazardous 
behaviour, and that the avoidance of the dangerous behaviour would have saved lives. A specific education effort needs to be targeted 
to drivers, in particular young male adults.
Our study would offer valuable recommendations for drawing up policies in disaster risk reduction management, particularly in 
non-structural mitigation measures (e.g. early warning systems) also including a gender perspective, which is rarely considered [74]. 
Since we recognize the riskiest situations and the most dangerous type of geo-hydrological process, we can use this knowledge to 
design appropriate gender-differentiated non-structural risk reduction measures. Finally, the results provide new insights into the 
social dimensions of disaster vulnerability and can support the development of gender-sensitive risk communication strategies and 
targeted disaster risk reduction measures (e.g., training and communication campaigns for specific population groups) aimed at 
reducing future loss of life.
